The one idea behind the whole standard

A curve is hard to read, but a straight line through the origin has the equation \(y = mx\), and its gradient m can be measured. So you change what you plot until the graph is straight, then compare the gradient with the theory. If theory says \(t = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}} \times \sqrt{d}\), plotting t against \(\sqrt{d}\) gives a straight line with gradient \(\sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\), and from that you find a.

Phase 0.3 · The standard and the mark sheet

ASSESSMENT

AS91168 (Physics 2.1): carry out a practical physics investigation that leads to a non-linear mathematical relationship. Level 2, 4 credits, internally assessed.

ACHIEVEMENT

Carry out a practical investigation:

  • collect data relevant to the aim, changing the independent variable over a reasonable range and number of values
  • draw a graph showing the relationship between the independent and dependent variables
  • write a conclusion stating the type of relationship
MERIT

In-depth investigation, which adds:

  • controlling the variables that could significantly affect the results
  • using techniques that increase the accuracy of the measurements
  • a conclusion giving the equation of the relationship found from your data
EXCELLENCE

Comprehensive investigation, with a discussion of critical issues such as:

  • why there is a limit at each end of the range of the independent variable
  • why a variable needed to be controlled
  • difficulties in measuring and how you overcame them
  • how your findings relate to the physics ideas

How to type your answer

Use the keypad, or type: / for divide, * or nothing for multiply (2ad means 2 × a × d), ^2 for squared, sqrt( ) for a square root and asin( ) for sin⁻¹. Use brackets around a whole top or bottom line: (v_f - v_i)/a. Subscripts can be typed with or without the underscore: vf and v_f both work, and Δ can be typed as D.

The golden rule

Whatever you do to one side, do to the other. Undo things in reverse order: undo + and − first, then × and ÷, then squares and square roots. To undo \(+\), subtract; to undo \(\times\), divide; to undo \(x^{2}\), square root; to undo \(\frac{1}{x}\), take the reciprocal of both sides.

Phase 1.1 · Variables & uncertainties

FOUNDATIONS

Every measurement has some uncertainty. Merit and Excellence depend on knowing where it comes from and what you did about it.

Three kinds of variable
Independent

The one you change on purpose. Usually on the x-axis. Choose at least 5 values over a sensible range.

Dependent

The one you measure, which changes as a result. Usually on the y-axis.

Control

Everything else that could affect the dependent variable, kept the same throughout, so only the independent variable causes the change.

Example. A trolley rolls down a ramp: you choose the distance (independent), time how long it takes (dependent), and keep the ramp height, the trolley and the start position the same (control).

Blunders: mistakes, not uncertainties

Misreading a scale, swapping two digits, measuring the wrong quantity, or using a diameter where a radius was needed are blunders. No technique removes them; you avoid them by being careful:

  • follow the method closely and write every reading straight into your table
  • do a quick rough calculation to check an answer is the right size
  • check you're using the right equation and its assumptions
  • repeat measurements, so a blunder stands out as different from the others

Experimental uncertainties are different: they are part of the measuring process and don't disappear however carefully you work. There are two kinds.

Accuracy and precision
Accuracy · systematic errors

How close the results are to the true value. Systematic errors shift every reading the same way, so they hurt accuracy. Repeating doesn't help; you have to fix the cause.

Precision · random errors

How close repeated readings are to each other. Random errors scatter readings above and below the true value, so they hurt precision. Repeating and averaging reduces them.

Systematic errors: SCRAPZ

SCRAPZ is a checklist of things that can shift all your readings one way (R is the reminder to deal with random errors too).

LetterWhat it meansExample and fix
S · Scale selectionUsing a scale that's too coarse or too big for the readingA 5 A ammeter range for a 0.20 A current reads to the nearest 0.1 A. Start on the biggest range for safety, then switch down to the smallest range the reading fits.
C · CalibrationAn instrument that reads consistently high or lowA thermometer reading 102 °C in boiling water; a stopwatch that runs fast; a stretched tape measure. Check against a known value.
R · Repeating and averagingThe fix for random errorRepeat each reading (at least three times) and average; time several cycles and divide.
A · Approximate theoryThe theory assumes something that isn't quite trueIgnoring friction or air resistance; a pendulum swung through a big angle when the formula assumes a small one.
P · ParallaxReading a scale from an angleRead a ruler, meniscus or pointer with your eye directly in line with the mark.
Z · Zero errorAn instrument that doesn't read zero when it shouldScales not tared; callipers reading 0.45 mm when closed; a meter needle resting at −0.1 A. Zero it, or subtract the offset from every reading.
Random errors and how to reduce them

Random errors make each reading equally likely to be too big or too small: your reaction time on a stopwatch, judging a scale between marks, vibrations, draughts, electrical noise. They show up as scatter when you repeat a measurement.

Measure many, divide

Time 10 swings of a pendulum and divide by 10. Your reaction-time error (about 0.2 s) is spread over 10 periods, so it's 10 times smaller per swing.

Repeat and average

Measure the same thing several times and average: 17.46 g, 17.42 g and 17.44 g average to 17.44 g. An odd one out (a blunder) can be spotted and re-measured.

In the write-up, every accuracy technique needs three parts: what you did, why it was needed for this measurement, and how it improved accuracy. "I repeated it" earns nothing on its own.

Phase 2.1 · Graphs and linear data

FOUNDATIONS

A straight-line graph is the best way to average a whole set of data. Practise on linear relationships first, then the same skills work on linearised non-linear data.

Drawing a graph properly
  • A line graph, with a title that says what is plotted against what.
  • Independent variable on the x-axis, dependent variable on the y-axis.
  • Each axis labelled with the quantity, its symbol and its unit, for example mass (m) [kg].
  • An even scale that makes the points fill most of the page. Look at the biggest and smallest values in your table first.
  • A straight line of best fit (not dot to dot), with roughly as many points above it as below.
The gradient

Draw a large right-angled triangle on your line of best fit (not between two data points), covering at least half the line:

\(\text{gradient} = \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\)

The gradient has a unit: the y unit divided by the x unit. Weight in N against mass in kg gives N kg⁻¹, which is the same as m s⁻².

You can't find "the gradient" of a curved graph this way: that's why non-linear data is linearised first.

From the graph to an equation

A straight line has the equation \(y = mx + c\), where m is the gradient and c the y-intercept. In these experiments the line usually goes through the origin, so \(y = mx\). Then put the real quantities in place of x and y:

\(y = 0.4x \;\rightarrow\; F_{w} = 0.4\,m\) (if weight is on the y-axis and mass on the x-axis)

Comparing with the theory

Line up your equation with the theory to see what the gradient stands for. Theory: \(F_{w} = g \times m\). Your graph: \(F_{w} = 10.2 \times m\). So the gradient is g: you measured \(g = 10.2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).

For Excellence, compare it with the accepted value and comment: \(\frac{10.2 - 9.8}{9.8} = 4\%\) high, so the result is close but slightly high, possibly because the spring balance reads high (a calibration or zero error).

Linear experiments you might meet: Hooke's law (\(F = kx\)), Ohm's law (\(V = IR\)), Snell's law (\(\sin\theta_{i} = n\sin\theta_{r}\)), electric field (\(V = Ed\)).

Phase 2.4 · Snell's law experiment

VIRTUAL PRACTICAL

Aim: find the relationship between the sine of the angle of incidence and the sine of the angle of refraction for light entering glass, and use it to find the refractive index n of the glass.

Equipment & theory

Semicircular glass block, ray box with a single slit, protractor (or a printed template). Theory: \(\sin\theta_{i} = n\sin\theta_{r}\). The ray enters the flat face at its centre, so it leaves the curved face along a radius without bending again.

Method

Line the block up on the template. Shine a single ray at the centre of the flat face. Mark the incident and refracted rays and measure both angles from the normal. Repeat for at least 5 angles of incidence, recording every reading in a headed table.

RAY BOX · SEMICIRCULAR BLOCK
Take a reading

Read the angle of refraction off the protractor yourself: the refracted ray is shown, but its value is not. Type your reading to the nearest degree.

\(\theta_{r}\) =°
Results
θi [°]θr [°]sin θisin θr

Analysis, conclusion and discussion

  • Work out sin θᵢ and sin θᵣ for each pair. Plot sin θᵢ (y) against sin θᵣ (x). It should be a straight line through the origin: \(\sin\theta_{i} \propto \sin\theta_{r}\).
  • The gradient has no unit (sines have none). Your equation is \(\sin\theta_{i} = (\text{gradient})\sin\theta_{r}\); comparing with the theory, the gradient is the refractive index n. Glass is usually about 1.5.
  • Discussion: why each accuracy technique was needed for these measurements (thin ray, protractor at eye level, marking the ray with crosses far apart), and why the ray must hit the centre of the flat face.
Show a model discussion

Thin ray. A wide beam has no single direction to measure. Using a single narrow slit gives a thin ray, so the angle can be read to within about 1°.

Marking the ray with two crosses far apart. A short pencil line can be drawn a degree or two off. Two crosses at least 5 cm apart fix the direction much more precisely, reducing the error in θ.

Aiming at the centre of the flat face. Then the ray leaves the curved face along a radius, at 90° to the surface, so it doesn't refract a second time. If it missed the centre, the second refraction would change the measured θᵣ and spoil the result.

Range. Below about 10° both angles are small, so a 1° reading error is a large percentage. Above about 80° the ray grazes the surface, most of the light reflects, and the refracted ray is too faint to see.

Phase 3.2 · Linearising non-linear data

FOUNDATIONS

Your raw graph for this standard will be a curve. To straighten it, add a calculated column to your table and draw a second graph. There are two ways to decide what to calculate.

Method 1: use the theory you're given

The theory for the rolling trolley can be written two ways. They are the same equation, rearranged:

\(d = \tfrac{1}{2}a \times t^{2} \qquad\text{or}\qquad t = \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{a}} \times \sqrt{d}\)

Pick the version whose subject is the quantity on your y-axis. Everything else on the right is constant except the x quantity, and how that appears tells you what to calculate.

d on the y-axis

\(d = \tfrac{1}{2}a \times t^{2}\), so \(d \propto t^{2}\). Add a column for \(t^{2}\) [s²] and plot d against \(t^{2}\).

t on the y-axis

\(t = \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{a}} \times \sqrt{d}\), so \(t \propto \sqrt{d}\). Add a column for \(\sqrt{d}\) [m½] and plot t against \(\sqrt{d}\).

Phase 3.4 · Writing it up: the rolling trolley

MODEL ANSWERS

These are the questions your write-up must answer, with model answers for the worked-example data in (t against √d, gradient 2.0 s m−½). Write your own answer first, then compare. Your answers must be about your measurements, not general statements.

Analysis and conclusion

1. Which is the independent variable? A

Model answer

The distance d from the start line to the mark, in metres. I chose it by placing the tape marks.

2. What type of relationship is there between distance and time? A

Model answer

Time is proportional to the square root of distance, \(t \propto \sqrt{d}\) (equivalently \(d \propto t^{2}\)). The raw graph of t against d curves, rising quickly then flattening, and t against √d is a straight line through the origin.

3–4. Calculate the gradient of the straight-line graph, with its unit. M

Model answer

Using a large triangle on the line from (0, 0) to (1.00 m½, 2.00 s): \(\text{gradient} = \frac{2.00\,\mathrm{s}}{1.00\,\mathrm{m^{1/2}}} = 2.0\,\mathrm{s\,m^{-1/2}}\).

5. State the equation of the relationship between d and t. M

Model answer

\(t = 2.0\sqrt{d}\), with t in seconds and d in metres.

6. Use your equation and the theory to find the acceleration of the trolley. M

Model answer

Theory: \(t = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\sqrt{d}\). My graph: \(t = 2.0\sqrt{d}\). So \(\sqrt{\frac{2}{a}} = 2.0\), \(\frac{2}{a} = 4.0\), \(a = 0.50\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).

7. What did you do to improve accuracy, and why? M E

Model answer

Timed each distance three times and averaged. Starting and stopping the stopwatch by hand adds a random reaction-time error of about 0.1–0.2 s, which is up to a fifth of the 0.87 s time for the shortest distance. Averaging three readings reduces that random error, and let me spot and repeat a reading that was clearly wrong.

Released the trolley from rest with its front edge on the start line every time. A small push would give it a starting speed, so every time would be too short and the graph would no longer go through the origin.

Watched the mark from directly above when stopping the clock. Looking from an angle (parallax) makes the trolley appear to reach the mark early or late.

Discussion

8–9. Which variables did you control, and why did they need controlling? M

Model answer

The height of the raised end (two blocks every time), the same trolley, the same start line, release from rest, the same bench surface and the same person timing. Each of these affects the trolley's acceleration or the timing, so if one changed, part of the change in time would be caused by it instead of by the distance, and the points wouldn't lie on one line.

10. Justify in depth why one control variable needed controlling. E

Model answer

The slope. The trolley accelerates because a component of its weight acts down the slope, \(mg\sin\theta\), minus friction. Raising the bench further increases θ and so increases a. The gradient of my graph is \(\sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\), so it only gives one value of a if a is the same for every run. If the slope had changed between runs, the points would come from different accelerations, wouldn't lie on one straight line, and the calculated a would be meaningless.

11. Give a reason for the limit at each end of your range of distances. E

Model answer

Lower limit, 0.20 m: shorter distances take less than about 0.8 s, so a reaction-time error of 0.2 s would be 25% or more of the measured time, too big for a reliable point. Upper limit, 1.00 m: the bench was only about 1.2 m long, and the trolley needed room to be stopped safely before the end.

12. Describe any difficulties in making the measurements and how you overcame them. E

Model answer

Starting the stopwatch exactly when the trolley was released was hard when one person did both. My partner released the trolley on a count of "3, 2, 1, go" so I could start the clock on "go". The trolley also drifted sideways on longer runs; we laid a metre ruler along the bench as a guide so it ran straight and travelled the measured distance.

13. Describe any unexpected results, a possible cause, and their effect on your conclusion. E

Model answer

The point at 0.20 m sat slightly above the line: its time was too long. The most likely cause is reaction time, which matters most for the shortest, fastest run. I re-timed it and the new mean was closer to the line. Because I drew a line of best fit through all the points (and the origin), one slightly high point had little effect on the gradient, so the conclusion is still valid.

14. Relate your findings to the physics ideas. E

Model answer

A straight line of t against √d through the origin shows that \(d = \tfrac{1}{2}at^{2}\) holds, which is only true for constant acceleration from rest. That makes sense: the forces on the trolley (the weight component down the slope and friction) stay the same all the way down, so by Newton's second law the acceleration is constant. Without friction, \(a = g\sin\theta\); for a bench raised 0.06 m over 1.2 m, \(\sin\theta = 0.05\) and \(g\sin\theta = 0.49\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), very close to my 0.50 m s⁻². A measured value well below \(g\sin\theta\) would show that friction and the rotating wheels take some of the energy.

A cart's distance is plotted against time squared. The line of best fit goes through the origin with gradient 4.89 m s⁻². Using \(d = \tfrac{1}{2}at^{2}\), find the cart's acceleration.

TASK TWOMatch the curve to its straight line

Four raw graphs and four straight-line graphs:

  • A rises quickly then flattens · B curves upwards more and more steeply · C falls and levels off gently · D falls very steeply then levels off.
  • 1 y against x², gradient 25 · 2 y against √x, gradient 4 · 3 y against 1/x, gradient 2.4 · 4 y against 1/x², gradient 0.3.

Match them, link each to one of \(y = kx^{2}\), \(y = 4k\sqrt{x}\), \(y = \frac{k}{2} \times \frac{1}{x}\), \(y = \frac{4\pi^{2}}{k} \times \frac{1}{x^{2}}\), and find k for each.

Show worked answer

B → 1, \(y = kx^{2}\): k = 25. A → 2, \(y = 4k\sqrt{x}\): 4k = 4, k = 1.0. C → 3, \(y = \frac{k}{2} \times \frac{1}{x}\): \(\frac{k}{2} = 2.4\), k = 4.8. D → 4, \(y = \frac{4\pi^{2}}{k} \times \frac{1}{x^{2}}\): \(\frac{4\pi^{2}}{k} = 0.3\), so \(k = \frac{4\pi^{2}}{0.3} = 132\).

A trolley is released from rest at the top of a bench raised at one end on 4 textbooks. Distance d [m] is the independent variable and time t [s] the dependent variable. Theory: \(d = \tfrac{1}{2}at^{2}\), or \(t = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\sqrt{d}\). The results were:

  1. Explain the upper and lower limits of the independent variable.
  2. Give four control variables, and explain one in detail.
  3. Show that the acceleration of the trolley is about 0.6 m s⁻².
Show worked answers

1. Lower limit 0.20 m: below this the trolley takes less than about 0.7 s, so a stopwatch reaction-time error of about 0.2 s would be over 25% of the time, too large for reliable data. Upper limit 1.00 m: the length of the bench (and room to stop the trolley safely) means it can't travel further.

Phase 4.5 · The pole vaulter: a formative task

TRY AT HOME

After seeing a vaulting pole snap on TV, Hemi models a pole with a strand of spaghetti. He holds the bottom 1 cm of a long strand fixed upright on graph paper, pushes sideways on it with a pen at a height h above the fixed part, and marks where the spaghetti is when it snaps. He measures how far the strand has bent sideways (d) at that moment, then repeats for different heights.

Theory (simplified)

\(d = \frac{k}{\pi r^{2}} \times h^{2}\) or \(h = \sqrt{\frac{\pi r^{2}}{k}} \times \sqrt{d}\), where r is the radius of the spaghetti and k is an elasticity constant, about \(1.76 \times 10^{-5}\) for dry, uncooked spaghetti.

Your report

Method: variables, controls, accuracy techniques. Data: a headed table with units. Analysis: raw graph with a smooth curve, the relationship, a linearised graph. Conclusion: the equation. Discussion: limits of the range, physics ideas, unexpected results, controls, difficulties, accuracy techniques.

Sample results to practise with

From one spaghetti strand of diameter 1.8 mm, each height repeated three times and averaged.

height (h) [m]0.0500.0700.0900.1100.1300.150
sideways distance (d) [m]0.0180.0330.0570.0830.1180.155
Show a model analysis

Variables. Independent: the height h where the pen pushes (x-axis). Dependent: the sideways distance d at the moment of snapping (y-axis). Controls: the same packet and thickness of spaghetti (r), dry and uncooked (k), the same 1 cm fixed at the bottom, pushing horizontally.

Relationship. d curves upwards as h increases: \(d \propto h^{2}\). Column \(h^{2}\) [m²]: 0.0025, 0.0049, 0.0081, 0.0121, 0.0169, 0.0225.
