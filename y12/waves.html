Speed of light, c3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹
01

Waves

Textbook ch. 5
  • A wave carries energy from place to place without carrying the material along with it.
  • Transverse: the particles vibrate at right angles to the direction the wave travels. Longitudinal: they vibrate along it, making compressions and rarefactions.
  • Mechanical waves (sound, water, a slinky) need a medium. Electromagnetic waves (light, radio, X-rays) can travel through a vacuum, all at \(c\).
  • The source sets the frequency. The medium sets the speed. So when a wave enters a new medium, f stays the same and \(\lambda\) changes.
  • Waves reflect, refract, diffract (spread through gaps) and interfere (add up where they overlap).
\(v = f\lambda\)\(f = \frac{1}{T}\)\(v = \frac{d}{t}\)

Watch out: wavelength is crest to crest (one whole cycle), not crest to trough. Amplitude is measured from the middle (rest position), not trough to crest.

02

Reflection of light

Textbook ch. 6
  • Law of reflection: the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, both measured from the normal.
  • A plane mirror gives a virtual, upright, same-size image, as far behind the mirror as the object is in front.
  • A concave (converging) mirror reflects parallel rays through the focal point F. A convex (diverging) mirror spreads them out as if from F behind the mirror.
  • Real images form where rays actually meet and can be caught on a screen. Virtual images are where rays only appear to come from.
  • A convex mirror always gives a virtual, upright, diminished image (wide field of view: shop and road mirrors).
\(\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{d_{o}} + \frac{1}{d_{i}}\)\(m = \frac{d_{i}}{d_{o}} = \frac{h_{i}}{h_{o}}\)\(s_{i}s_{o} = f^{2}\)

Watch out: for a convex mirror f is negative, and a negative \(d_{i}\) means the image is virtual (behind the mirror).

03

Refraction

Textbook ch. 7
  • Light bends when it crosses a boundary at an angle because its speed changes. Slowing down, it bends towards the normal.
  • The refractive index tells you how much a material slows light: \(n = \frac{c}{v}\). Air is taken as 1.00.
  • Going from a higher n to a lower n, beyond the critical angle all the light reflects: total internal reflection (optical fibres, prisms in binoculars).
  • A converging (convex) lens brings parallel rays to a focus. A diverging (concave) lens spreads them out as if from F.
  • Lenses use the same formula as mirrors. A diverging lens has a negative f and always gives a virtual, upright, diminished image.
\(n_{1}\sin\theta_{1} = n_{2}\sin\theta_{2}\)\(\frac{n_{1}}{n_{2}} = \frac{v_{2}}{v_{1}} = \frac{\lambda_{2}}{\lambda_{1}}\)\(\sin\theta_{c} = \frac{n_{2}}{n_{1}}\)

Watch out: angles in Snell's law are measured from the normal, not from the surface. Total internal reflection only happens going into a lower refractive index.

The big idea that links everything: speed depends on the medium

A wave's frequency is fixed by whatever makes it. Its speed is fixed by what it travels through. Put those together with \(v = f\lambda\) and most of this standard follows: a wave that slows down gets a shorter wavelength and, if it arrives at an angle, bends (refraction). Lenses work because glass slows light. Optical fibres work because light can't escape into a medium where it would have to go faster at too steep an angle.

Phase 0.3 · Exam skills: answers that earn marks

EXAM SKILLS

Knowing the physics is half the job. The other half is showing it the way the marking schedule rewards. The grade depends on how deep your answer goes.

ACHIEVEMENT

Describe, identify, calculate (one step). Name the wave behaviour, label a diagram, or get a one-step answer such as \(v = f\lambda\) with the right unit.

"The wave diffracts: it spreads out after passing through the gap."

MERIT

Explain, multi-step calculate. Give the physics reason. Draw an accurate ray diagram. Calculations where you need to find something first, such as the image distance before the magnification.

"The light slows down as it enters the glass, so the part of the wavefront that enters first falls behind and the wave bends towards the normal."

EXCELLENCE

Connect concepts. Link two or more ideas into one complete argument, and use it to reach a conclusion, often with a calculation that supports it.

"Water has a lower refractive index than glass, so the critical angle at a glass–water boundary is bigger (62°), and the 50° ray now escapes instead of totally internally reflecting."

A structure for "explain" questions

Name itName the behaviour or principle: refraction, diffraction, the law of reflection, total internal reflection.
Apply itSay what happens in this situation: what slows down, which path is longer, where rays meet.
Link itConnect cause to effect with "so", "therefore" or "because".
ConcludeAnswer the exact question: towards or away from the normal, real or virtual, closer or further apart.

Lay out every calculation the same way

Equation

Write the equation you are using first, in symbols.

Substitute

Put the numbers in with units. Show conversions (nm → m, cm → m, ms → s).

Answer

Give the answer with a unit and a sensible number of significant figures. For images, also describe them: real or virtual, upright or inverted, magnified or diminished.

A wrong final answer can still gain credit if the marker can follow a correct method. A bare number cannot.

Ray diagrams that earn the marks

Do this

  • Use a ruler. Draw at least two rays from the top of the object, each with an arrow.
  • Draw real rays as solid lines and the backwards extensions to a virtual image as dashed lines.
  • Mark F (and C or 2F) on both sides of a lens and in front of a mirror.
  • Measure angles from the normal, and draw the normal as a dashed line.
  • Say where the energy goes, or what stays the same (frequency) and what changes (speed, wavelength).

Avoid this

  • Saying light "bends because it hits the glass". It bends because it changes speed.
  • Saying the frequency changes when a wave enters a new medium.
  • Putting arrowheads on dashed construction lines, or leaving them off real rays.
  • Calling the image on the screen "virtual". Anything that forms on a screen is real.
  • Expecting total internal reflection when light goes from air into glass.

The ten most common traps

Phase 1.1 · Wave foundations

FOUNDATIONS

A wave is a moving disturbance that carries energy. The material it moves through only vibrates about where it started. Work through each step, then try the two labs.

Transverse and longitudinal waves
Transverse

The particles vibrate at right angles to the direction the wave travels. Crests and troughs. Examples: a wave sent along a rope or slinky by moving one end sideways, ripples on water, all electromagnetic waves.

Longitudinal

The particles vibrate parallel to the direction the wave travels. Compressions (squashed together) and rarefactions (spread out). Examples: sound, a slinky pushed and pulled along its length.

Mechanical and electromagnetic waves

A mechanical wave needs a medium (solid, liquid or gas) to travel through: sound, water waves, earthquake waves, waves on a string. Sound can't cross a vacuum.

An electromagnetic wave is a vibrating electric and magnetic field, so it needs no medium. All EM waves travel through a vacuum at the same speed, \(c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). In order of increasing frequency (decreasing wavelength): radio, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays. Visible light runs from about 700 nm (red) to 400 nm (violet).

MechanicalElectromagnetic
Needs a medium?YesNo, travels through a vacuum

Describing a wave: A, λ, T, f and v
Amplitude, A

Biggest displacement from the rest position. More amplitude means more energy.

Wavelength, λ

Length of one complete wave, in m. Crest to crest, or compression to compression.

Period, T

Time for one complete wave to pass a point, in s.

Frequency, f

Number of waves per second, in hertz (Hz). \(f = \frac{1}{T}\).

Speed, v

How fast the wave pattern moves. In one period it moves one wavelength, so \(v = \frac{\lambda}{T} = f\lambda\).

\(v = f\lambda \qquad f = \frac{1}{T}\)

Example. A radio station broadcasts at 92.4 MHz. \(\lambda = \frac{v}{f} = \frac{3.00 \times 10^{8}}{92.4 \times 10^{6}} = 3.25\,\mathrm{m}\).

The key rule: the source decides the frequency and the medium decides the speed. In one medium, a higher frequency means a shorter wavelength (v stays the same). In a new medium the frequency stays the same, so if the speed drops, the wavelength drops in proportion.

Reflection of waves and pulses

Waves reflect from a barrier with the angle of incidence equal to the angle of reflection, both measured from the normal. Speed, wavelength and frequency are unchanged after reflection (same medium).

Fixed end

A pulse on a rope tied to a wall comes back inverted: a crest returns as a trough. The wall pulls back on the rope.

Free end

A pulse reaching a free end (a loose ring on a pole) comes back the same way up.

When a pulse reaches a join with a different rope, part is transmitted and part is reflected. Going into a heavier rope (slower), the reflected pulse is inverted; going into a lighter rope (faster), it stays upright. The transmitted pulse is always upright.

Refraction of water waves

Water waves travel slower in shallow water. The frequency can't change, so the wavelength gets shorter: the wavefronts bunch up.

If the waves cross into shallow water at an angle, the part of each wavefront that reaches the shallow water first slows down first. The wavefront swings round and the wave changes direction, bending towards the normal. This is refraction. Going into deeper (faster) water, it bends away from the normal.

The arrows show the direction of travel, always at right angles to the wavefronts.

Diffraction: spreading out through a gap

When waves pass through a gap or round the edge of an obstacle, they spread out. This is diffraction. The wavelength, frequency and speed do not change.

  • Diffraction is most noticeable when the gap is about the same size as the wavelength, or smaller.
  • A wide gap compared with λ: the wave goes almost straight through, with only a little spreading at the edges.
  • So, for a fixed gap, longer wavelengths (lower frequencies) diffract more. That's why you can hear round a corner but not see round it: sound has wavelengths of about a metre, light about half a micrometre.
Interference from two sources

Where two waves overlap, their displacements add (superposition). Two sources vibrating in step with the same frequency (coherent sources) make a fixed pattern:

Antinodal lines · constructive

Crest meets crest and trough meets trough, so the water moves up and down with a big amplitude. The path difference is a whole number of wavelengths: \(0, \lambda, 2\lambda, \ldots\)

Nodal lines · destructive

Crest meets trough and they cancel, so the water stays almost still. The path difference is \(\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda, 1\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda, 2\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda, \ldots\)

\(\text{path difference} = |S_{1}P - S_{2}P|\)

  • Increase the frequency → shorter λ → the lines get closer together (more of them).
  • Move the sources further apart → more lines, closer together.
  • The central line, the same distance from both sources, is always antinodal.

Two loudspeakers playing the same note do the same thing: walk across the room and the sound gets loud and quiet. Light from two narrow slits makes bright and dark bands.

Phase 1.5 · Exam-style problems: waves

EXAM PRACTICE

Three original practice questions in the style of NCEA Level 2 AS91170 papers. Attempt every part before opening the worked answer.

QUESTION ONESurf beach · wave speed

Swells arrive at a beach every 8.0 s. A surfer waiting beyond the break estimates the crests are 96 m apart.

(a) Calculate the frequency of the waves. A

(b) Calculate the speed of the waves. A

(c) As the waves move into shallower water near the beach they slow down. Explain what happens to their frequency and wavelength, and why the crests line up nearly parallel to the beach even when they started at an angle. E

Show worked answer · Question One

(a) \(f = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{1}{8.0} = 0.125\,\mathrm{Hz}\) (0.13 Hz)

(b) \(v = f\lambda = 0.125 \times 96 = 12\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)

(c) The frequency stays at 0.125 Hz, because it is set by the source (the storm far out at sea), and a crest arrives at the shallow water as often as it leaves the deep water. Since \(v = f\lambda\) and v decreases while f is constant, the wavelength decreases: the crests bunch up. If a crest approaches at an angle, the end in shallower water slows down first while the other end keeps going faster, so the crest swings round towards the beach. The wave refracts towards the normal, and the crests end up almost parallel to the shore.

QUESTION TWOHarbour entrance · diffraction

Sea waves of wavelength 15 m reach a harbour wall with an entrance 20 m wide.

(a) Name the wave behaviour that makes the waves spread out inside the harbour. A

(b) A storm produces waves of wavelength 40 m. Explain how the waves inside the harbour change. M

(c) Engineers want to protect boats moored behind the wall, to the side of the entrance. Explain whether they should make the entrance wider or narrower. E

Show worked answer · Question Two

(a) Diffraction.

(b) The gap is now smaller than the wavelength (20 m compared with 40 m), so the waves diffract more: they spread out further round behind the wall, in almost semicircular wavefronts. Their wavelength (40 m), frequency and speed are unchanged by passing through the gap.

(c) Wider. Diffraction is greatest when the gap is about the same size as the wavelength or smaller. Making the entrance many wavelengths wide means the waves pass through mostly in a straight line with little spreading, so less wave energy reaches the sheltered areas to the side. (A narrower entrance would make the waves spread round more, although less energy would get through overall.)

QUESTION THREETwo speakers · interference

Two speakers 1.5 m apart are connected to the same signal generator and play a note of 680 Hz. The speed of sound is 340 m s⁻¹.

(a) Calculate the wavelength of the sound. A

(b) A student stands at a point P that is 4.25 m from one speaker and 5.00 m from the other. Show whether the sound at P is loud or quiet. M

(c) The frequency is changed to 340 Hz. Explain, with a calculation, what the student now hears at P, and how the pattern of loud and quiet places in the room changes. E

Show worked answer · Question Three

(a) \(\lambda = \frac{v}{f} = \frac{340}{680} = 0.50\,\mathrm{m}\)

(b) Path difference = 5.00 − 4.25 = 0.75 m = 1.5λ. That's a whole number plus a half, so a compression from one speaker arrives with a rarefaction from the other and they cancel. P is on a nodal line: quiet.

(c) New wavelength \(\lambda = \frac{340}{340} = 1.0\,\mathrm{m}\). The path difference is still 0.75 m, which is now 0.75λ: neither a whole number nor a half-number of wavelengths, so the sound at P is in between, neither as loud as possible nor silent. Overall, the longer wavelength spreads the pattern out: there are fewer loud and quiet lines across the room and they are further apart.

Phase 2.1 · Reflection of light: foundations

FOUNDATIONS

Light travels in straight lines, drawn as rays. A ray diagram tracks a few rays from the top of an object to find where its image forms.

The law of reflection

The normal is a line drawn at right angles to the surface where the ray hits it. The angle of incidence \(i\) and the angle of reflection \(r\) are both measured from the normal, and

\(\text{angle of incidence} = \text{angle of reflection}\)

The incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal all lie in the same plane. A smooth surface gives a clear (specular) reflection; a rough surface scatters light in all directions (diffuse reflection), although each tiny bit still obeys the law.

Plane mirrors

The image in a flat mirror is:

  • virtual: rays only appear to come from behind the mirror, so it can't be caught on a screen
  • upright and the same size as the object
  • the same distance behind the mirror as the object is in front
  • laterally inverted: left and right are swapped

To find a plane-mirror image on a diagram: draw the image the same distance behind, then draw reflected rays to the eye and extend them back to the image as dashed lines.

Curved mirrors: the words you need
Concave · converging

Caved in, like the inside of a spoon. Parallel rays reflect and meet at the principal focus F, in front of the mirror.

Convex · diverging

Bulging out, like the back of a spoon. Parallel rays reflect and spread out as if they came from F behind the mirror.

Principal axis, F, C, f

The axis goes through the centre of the mirror (the pole). C is the centre of curvature. F is halfway between C and the mirror, so \(f = \frac{r}{2}\).

Three rays that are easy to draw
Ray 1

Parallel to the axis → reflects through F (concave), or as if from F behind the mirror (convex).

Ray 2

Through F (or heading towards F behind a convex mirror) → reflects parallel to the axis.

Ray 3

To the pole (centre of the mirror) → reflects at the same angle on the other side of the axis. (A ray through C reflects straight back.)

Where the reflected rays actually cross, there is a real image. If they spread apart, extend them back behind the mirror with dashed lines: where those cross is a virtual image.

What kind of image?
Object position (concave mirror)ImageUse

A convex mirror always gives a virtual, upright, diminished image, between the mirror and F. It shows a wide field of view: shop security mirrors, blind corners, car wing mirrors.

The mirror formula and magnification

\(\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{d_{o}} + \frac{1}{d_{i}} \qquad m = \frac{d_{i}}{d_{o}} = \frac{h_{i}}{h_{o}}\)

Signs: distances to real objects and real images are positive. A virtual image has a negative \(d_{i}\). A concave mirror has a positive f; a convex mirror has a negative f.

Example. An object is 30 cm in front of a concave mirror with f = 20 cm. \(\frac{1}{d_{i}} = \frac{1}{20} - \frac{1}{30} = \frac{1}{60}\), so \(d_{i} = 60\,\mathrm{cm}\) (positive: real). \(m = \frac{60}{30} = 2.0\): the image is real, inverted and twice the size.

Example. The same object 10 cm from the mirror: \(\frac{1}{d_{i}} = \frac{1}{20} - \frac{1}{10} = -\frac{1}{20}\), so \(d_{i} = -20\,\mathrm{cm}\): a virtual image 20 cm behind the mirror, magnified ×2, upright.

The sheet also gives the same relationship measured from the focal points: \(s_{i}s_{o} = f^{2}\), where \(s_{o} = d_{o} - f\) and \(s_{i} = d_{i} - f\). In the first example \(s_{o} = 10\,\mathrm{cm}\) and \(s_{i} = \frac{20^{2}}{10} = 40\,\mathrm{cm}\), so \(d_{i} = 60\,\mathrm{cm}\) again.

EXAM PRACTICE

QUESTION ONEDressing-room mirror · plane mirror

Aroha stands 1.5 m in front of a tall plane mirror.

(a) State how far Aroha is from her image. A

(b) Give three properties of the image. A

(c) Explain, using the law of reflection, why the image appears to be behind the mirror. M

Show worked answer · Question One

(a) The image is 1.5 m behind the mirror, so it is 3.0 m from Aroha.

(b) Any three of: virtual, upright, the same size, laterally inverted, the same distance behind the mirror as she is in front.

(c) Rays from each point on Aroha reflect from the mirror with the angle of reflection equal to the angle of incidence. The reflected rays spread apart as they travel to her eyes. Her brain assumes light travels in straight lines, so it traces the rays straight back: they appear to come from a point behind the mirror, where the dashed extensions meet. No light actually goes there, so the image is virtual.

QUESTION TWOMake-up mirror · concave mirror

A concave make-up mirror has a focal length of 25 cm. Mere holds her face 15 cm from it.

(a) Calculate the image distance. M

(b) Calculate the magnification and describe the image fully. M

(c) Mere moves back until she is 50 cm from the mirror. Explain, with a calculation, how her image changes. E

Show worked answer · Question Two

(a) \(\frac{1}{d_{i}} = \frac{1}{f} - \frac{1}{d_{o}} = \frac{1}{25} - \frac{1}{15} = -0.0267\), so \(d_{i} = -37.5\,\mathrm{cm}\) (−38 cm).

(b) \(m = \frac{d_{i}}{d_{o}} = \frac{37.5}{15} = 2.5\). The image is virtual (negative \(d_{i}\), 37.5 cm behind the mirror), upright, and magnified 2.5 times.

(c) At 50 cm she is at C (\(2f\)): \(\frac{1}{d_{i}} = \frac{1}{25} - \frac{1}{50} = \frac{1}{50}\), so \(d_{i} = +50\,\mathrm{cm}\) and m = 1. Her face is now outside F, so the reflected rays actually cross in front of the mirror: the image becomes real, inverted and the same size, 50 cm in front of the mirror (at the same place as her face). A make-up mirror only works when the face is inside F.

QUESTION THREEShop security mirror · convex mirror

A convex security mirror has a focal length of magnitude 0.60 m. A shopper 1.7 m tall stands 3.0 m in front of it.

(a) Calculate the image distance. M

(b) Calculate the height of the shopper's image. M

(c) Explain why a convex mirror is used for security rather than a plane mirror of the same size. E

Show worked answer · Question Three

(a) A convex mirror has a negative focal length, f = −0.60 m. \(\frac{1}{d_{i}} = \frac{1}{-0.60} - \frac{1}{3.0} = -2.0\), so \(d_{i} = -0.50\,\mathrm{m}\): virtual, 0.50 m behind the mirror.

(b) \(m = \frac{0.50}{3.0} = 0.167\), so \(h_{i} = 0.167 \times 1.7 = 0.28\,\mathrm{m}\), upright.

(c) The convex surface makes rays from a very wide area reflect towards the viewer: parallel rays spread out as if from F behind the mirror. Every image is diminished and upright, so a whole aisle fits into a small mirror and nothing appears upside down. A plane mirror the same size only shows an area the same size as itself (at the same distance), so it covers a much narrower field of view.

Phase 3.1 · Refraction: foundations

FOUNDATIONS

Light slows down in glass, water and plastic. When it crosses a boundary at an angle, that change of speed bends it. Everything in this topic follows from that one fact.

Why light bends

When a wavefront reaches a slower medium at an angle, one side of it slows down first. The wavefront swings round, just like water waves entering shallow water, and the ray changes direction.

Into a slower medium (higher n)

Bends towards the normal: air → glass.

Into a faster medium (lower n)

Bends away from the normal: glass → air.

A ray travelling along the normal (angle of incidence 0°) slows down but doesn't bend. Some light is always reflected at the boundary as well.

Refractive index

The refractive index n compares the speed of light in a vacuum with its speed in the material: \(n = \frac{c}{v}\). It has no unit. Air ≈ 1.00, water 1.33, glass about 1.5, diamond 2.42.

Between any two materials:

\(\frac{n_{1}}{n_{2}} = \frac{v_{2}}{v_{1}} = \frac{\lambda_{2}}{\lambda_{1}}\)

The frequency (the colour) doesn't change, so the wavelength shrinks in the same ratio as the speed. Example. Red light, λ = 650 nm in air, enters water: \(\lambda_{2} = \frac{n_{1}\lambda_{1}}{n_{2}} = \frac{1.00 \times 650}{1.33} = 489\,\mathrm{nm}\). Its speed in water is \(\frac{3.00 \times 10^{8}}{1.33} = 2.26 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

Snell's law

\(n_{1}\sin\theta_{1} = n_{2}\sin\theta_{2}\)

\(\theta_{1}\) and \(\theta_{2}\) are measured from the normal. Example. Light in air hits glass (n = 1.50) at 40°: \(\sin\theta_{2} = \frac{1.00\sin 40^\circ}{1.50} = 0.429\), so \(\theta_{2} = 25^\circ\). It bent towards the normal, as expected for a slower medium.

Critical angle and total internal reflection

Going from a higher n into a lower n (glass → air), the ray bends away from the normal. As the angle of incidence increases, the refracted ray gets closer to the surface. At the critical angle \(\theta_{c}\) it runs along the surface (\(\theta_{2} = 90^\circ\)). Put that into Snell's law with \(\sin 90^\circ = 1\):

\(\sin\theta_{c} = \frac{n_{2}}{n_{1}} \quad (n_{1} > n_{2})\)

Beyond the critical angle no light can refract out: all of it reflects back inside. This is total internal reflection (TIR). Glass to air: \(\sin\theta_{c} = \frac{1.00}{1.50}\), \(\theta_{c} = 41.8^\circ\).

  • Optical fibres keep light inside a thin glass core by repeated TIR, for internet cables and endoscopes. The core has a higher n than the cladding round it.
  • Prisms in binoculars and periscopes turn light through 90° or 180° using TIR, which reflects better than a mirror.
  • Diamonds sparkle because their huge n gives a small critical angle (24°), so light bounces around inside many times.

TIR can never happen going into a higher n (air → glass), because the ray bends towards the normal and can always get through.

Lenses
Converging (convex) lens

Thicker in the middle. Parallel rays refract through the principal focus F on the far side. Positive f.

Diverging (concave) lens

Thinner in the middle. Parallel rays spread out as if they came from F on the near side. Negative f.

A lens has a focal point on each side, the same distance f from the centre. The three easy rays:

  • Parallel to the axis → through F on the far side (converging), or away from F on the near side (diverging).
  • Through the centre of the lens → straight on, undeviated.
  • Through F on the near side (converging) or towards F on the far side (diverging) → comes out parallel to the axis.
Images formed by lenses
Object position (converging lens)ImageUse
Beyond 2FReal, inverted, diminished, between F and 2F on the far sideCamera, eye
At 2FReal, inverted, same size, at 2FPhotocopier at 100%
Between 2F and FReal, inverted, magnified, beyond 2FProjector
At FNo image: rays come out parallelLighthouse beam
Inside FVirtual, upright, magnified, on the same side as the objectMagnifying glass

A diverging lens always gives a virtual, upright, diminished image (glasses for short sight). The lens formula and magnification are the same as for mirrors: \(\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{d_{o}} + \frac{1}{d_{i}}\), \(m = \frac{d_{i}}{d_{o}} = \frac{h_{i}}{h_{o}}\). For a lens, a real image is on the far side and a virtual image (negative \(d_{i}\)) is on the same side as the object.

Dispersion

Glass has a slightly bigger refractive index for violet light than for red, so violet slows down more and bends more. White light entering a prism at an angle spreads into a spectrum. Raindrops do the same, refracting and reflecting sunlight to make rainbows.

QUESTION ONEFish tank · Snell's law

A ray of light in air strikes the water surface of a fish tank at 50° to the normal. Water has a refractive index of 1.33.

(a) Calculate the angle of refraction. A

(b) Calculate the speed of light in the water. A

(c) The light has a wavelength of 590 nm in air. Calculate its wavelength in water, and explain why its colour does not change. M

Show worked answer · Question One

(a) \(\sin\theta_{2} = \frac{n_{1}\sin\theta_{1}}{n_{2}} = \frac{1.00 \times \sin 50^\circ}{1.33} = 0.576\), so \(\theta_{2} = 35^\circ\) (35.2°).

(b) \(\frac{n_{1}}{n_{2}} = \frac{v_{2}}{v_{1}}\): \(v_{2} = \frac{1.00 \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}}{1.33} = 2.26 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)

(c) \(\lambda_{2} = \frac{n_{1}\lambda_{1}}{n_{2}} = \frac{590}{1.33} = 444\,\mathrm{nm}\). Colour depends on frequency, and the frequency is set by the source: every wave crest that reaches the surface carries on into the water, so the same number pass each second. The speed falls, so by \(v = f\lambda\) the wavelength falls in proportion while f, and the colour, stay the same.

QUESTION TWOOptical fibre · total internal reflection

An optical fibre has a glass core of refractive index 1.52, surrounded by cladding of refractive index 1.46.

(a) Calculate the critical angle at the core–cladding boundary. M

(b) Explain why the light stays inside the core even when the fibre bends gently. M

(c) Explain why the cladding must have a lower refractive index than the core, and why a fibre with no cladding (core in air) is less reliable, even though its critical angle is smaller. E

Show worked answer · Question Two

(a) \(\sin\theta_{c} = \frac{n_{2}}{n_{1}} = \frac{1.46}{1.52} = 0.961\), so \(\theta_{c} = 74^\circ\) (73.9°).

(b) Light travelling nearly along the fibre hits the core–cladding boundary at a large angle of incidence, measured from the normal. As long as that angle is bigger than 74°, the light can't refract out and is totally internally reflected. It zig-zags along the core by repeated TIR. A gentle bend only changes the angle a little, so it stays above the critical angle.

(c) TIR only happens when light goes from a higher to a lower refractive index; if the cladding had the higher n the light would always refract into it. With no cladding the critical angle at glass–air is smaller (\(\sin\theta_{c} = \frac{1.00}{1.52}\), 41°), but the bare surface touches whatever is around it: scratches, dirt, water or other fibres change the conditions at the surface so light leaks out or crosses into a neighbouring fibre. The cladding protects the boundary and keeps the conditions for TIR the same all the way along.

QUESTION THREEProjector and magnifying glass · lenses

A converging lens has a focal length of 12 cm.

(a) A slide is placed 15 cm from the lens. Calculate where the image forms and its magnification. M

(b) Describe the image fully and explain whether it could be shown on a screen. M

(c) The same lens is used as a magnifying glass to read print 8.0 cm away. Calculate the image distance and magnification, and explain why the image can't be caught on a screen. E

Show worked answer · Question Three

(a) \(\frac{1}{d_{i}} = \frac{1}{12} - \frac{1}{15} = \frac{1}{60}\), so \(d_{i} = 60\,\mathrm{cm}\) on the far side. \(m = \frac{60}{15} = 4.0\).

(b) Real, inverted, magnified four times. The rays from each point on the slide actually meet 60 cm beyond the lens, so a screen placed there catches a sharp image. (That's why slides go into a projector upside down.)

(c) \(\frac{1}{d_{i}} = \frac{1}{12} - \frac{1}{8.0} = -\frac{1}{24}\), so \(d_{i} = -24\,\mathrm{cm}\), and \(m = \frac{24}{8.0} = 3.0\). The print is inside F, so the lens can't bend the rays enough to make them meet: they still spread apart after the lens. They only appear to come from a point 24 cm behind the lens, on the same side as the print, where the dashed extensions cross. No light actually reaches that point, so the image is virtual (and upright), and a screen there would stay blank.
