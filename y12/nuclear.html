01

Atomic structure

Textbook ch. 15
  • Thomson's "plum pudding" model: a sphere of spread-out positive charge with electrons dotted through it.
  • Rutherford's gold foil experiment: most alpha particles went straight through, a few were deflected through large angles, and about 1 in 8000 bounced back.
  • So the atom is mostly empty space, with almost all its mass and all its positive charge in a tiny nucleus, tens of thousands of times smaller across than the atom.
  • The nucleus holds protons and neutrons, held together by the strong nuclear force. \(^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X}\): Z protons, A − Z neutrons.
  • Isotopes have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
\(A = Z + N\)\(^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U}\): 92 p, 143 n

Watch out: the big deflections are the evidence for the nucleus. "Most went straight through" is the evidence for empty space. Keep the two observations and their conclusions separate.

02

Radioactivity

Textbook ch. 15
  • Unstable nuclei decay randomly and spontaneously, giving out alpha (\(^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\)), beta (\(^{\;\,0}_{-1}e\)) or gamma (\(\gamma\)) radiation.
  • Alpha: most ionising, least penetrating (stopped by paper). Beta: in between (stopped by a few mm of aluminium). Gamma: least ionising, most penetrating (reduced by thick lead).
  • In every nuclear equation the mass numbers (top) and the atomic numbers (bottom) balance.
  • Half-life: the time for half the radioactive nuclei (or the activity) to decay. It never changes for a given isotope.
\(N = N_{0}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{n}\)\(n = \frac{t}{t_{1/2}}\)

Watch out: after two half-lives a quarter is left, not none. Ionising and penetrating are opposites: the more a radiation ionises, the sooner it loses its energy and stops.

03

Nuclear energy

Textbook ch. 16
  • In fission a heavy nucleus (uranium-235) absorbs a neutron and splits into two smaller nuclei and 2–3 neutrons, which can cause a chain reaction.
  • In fusion two light nuclei (hydrogen isotopes) join to make a heavier one. It needs enormous temperatures to overcome the electrical repulsion: it powers the Sun.
  • In both, the products have slightly less mass than the starting particles. That missing mass becomes energy: \(E = mc^{2}\).
  • A reactor controls the chain reaction with control rods (absorb neutrons), a moderator (slows neutrons) and a coolant (carries the heat away).
\(E = mc^{2}\)\(P = \frac{E}{t}\)

Watch out: in \(E = mc^{2}\), m is the mass lost (in kg), not the mass of the fuel. Square c: \(c^{2} = 9.00 \times 10^{16}\).

Before every calculation

Write the equation, substitute with units, give the answer to 2 or 3 significant figures with a unit. Convert first: g → kg, MeV → J (× 1.60 × 10⁻¹³), hours or days → s for power. The lists everything you need.

The big idea that links everything: the nucleus

Rutherford's experiment showed that an atom's mass and positive charge sit in a tiny nucleus. Some nuclei are unstable and change into others by giving out radiation (radioactivity). Very heavy nuclei can split (fission) and very light ones can join (fusion). In every one of these changes, the numbers of nucleons and of protons are conserved, and any mass that disappears is turned into energy, \(E = mc^{2}\).

ASSESSMENT

AS91172 is internally assessed. However your teacher runs it, the grade depends on how deeply you explain and connect the ideas.

ACHIEVEMENT

Describe. State the facts and use the terms correctly: what Rutherford observed, the properties of alpha, beta and gamma, what fission is, one-step calculations.

"Alpha radiation is stopped by a sheet of paper."

MERIT

Explain. Give the reasons: why the nucleus must be small and positive, why alpha is so ionising, why a chain reaction grows. Calculations where you need to find something first.

"Alpha particles are large and doubly charged, so they ionise many atoms along their path, lose their energy quickly and travel only a few centimetres in air."

EXCELLENCE

Connect. Link two or more ideas into one argument, and use it to reach a judgement: properties of radiation with a use, half-life with safety, mass loss with power output.

"Technetium-99m suits medical imaging: its gamma rays penetrate the body to reach the camera but ionise little, and its 6-hour half-life means the activity has dropped to 1/16 within a day."

A structure for "explain" questions

Name itName the idea: nucleus, ionisation, half-life, conservation, mass defect.
Apply itSay what it means for this situation, using the particles and materials in the question.
Link itConnect cause to effect with "so", "therefore" or "because".
ConcludeAnswer the exact question: which radiation, how long, safe or not, how much energy.

Do this · avoid this

Do this

  • Pair each gold foil observation with its own conclusion.
  • Check every nuclear equation: top numbers balance, bottom numbers balance.
  • Count half-lives step by step in a table if the numbers aren't easy.
  • Say where the energy comes from: a loss of mass, \(E = mc^{2}\).
  • When judging a use or a risk, link the type of radiation, its penetration and ionisation, and its half-life.

Avoid this

  • Saying alpha is "stronger" or "more dangerous" without saying when (inside or outside the body).
  • Thinking a radioactive sample is all gone after two half-lives.
  • Confusing fission (splitting) and fusion (joining).
  • Using the mass of the fuel in \(E = mc^{2}\) instead of the mass lost.
  • Saying gamma changes the mass or atomic number. It changes neither.

Phase 1.1 · Atomic structure foundations

FOUNDATIONS

How do you find out what's inside something far too small to see? Fire things at it and watch how they bounce. That's how the nucleus was discovered.

Thomson's plum pudding model (1904)

J. J. Thomson discovered the electron, a tiny negative particle found in every atom. Atoms are neutral, so there must be positive charge too. His model: the atom is a sphere of positive charge spread evenly through it, with the electrons dotted through it like plums in a pudding. The mass is spread through the whole atom.

If that were true, the positive charge would be spread so thinly that a fast, heavy alpha particle would pass through any atom with almost no deflection.

Rutherford's gold foil experiment (1909)

Geiger and Marsden, working for Ernest Rutherford (who was born and studied in New Zealand), fired alpha particles at gold foil only a few hundred atoms thick, in a vacuum, and counted the flashes on a zinc sulfide screen around it.

ObservationConclusion
Most alpha particles went straight through, undeflected.The atom is mostly empty space.
A few were deflected through large angles.There is a concentrated positive charge in the atom that repels the positive alpha particles strongly when they pass close to it.
About 1 in 8000 bounced back towards the source.That positive charge, and almost all the atom's mass, is packed into a tiny volume: the nucleus. Only a very massive, concentrated charge could turn back a fast alpha particle.

Rutherford's nuclear model: a tiny, dense, positive nucleus with the electrons orbiting far outside it. A gold nucleus is about \(10^{-14}\) m across; the atom about \(10^{-10}\) m. If the atom were a 100 m rugby field, the nucleus would be a pea on the halfway line.

Protons and neutrons are both called nucleons. A nucleus is written

\(^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X} \qquad A = \text{mass number (nucleons)} \qquad Z = \text{atomic number (protons)} \qquad N = A - Z \text{ neutrons}\)

Example. \(^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U}\) has 92 protons and 235 − 92 = 143 neutrons. A neutral uranium atom also has 92 electrons.

Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element (the same number of protons, Z) with different numbers of neutrons (different A). Carbon-12 (\(^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}\)) and carbon-14 (\(^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}\)) both have 6 protons; carbon-14 has two extra neutrons. Isotopes have the same chemistry (the same electrons) but different masses, and some isotopes are unstable: radioactive.

What holds the nucleus together?

The protons are all positive and only about \(10^{-15}\) m apart, so they repel each other with a large electrostatic force. They are held together by the strong nuclear force, which attracts every nucleon (proton or neutron) to its neighbours. It is stronger than the electrostatic repulsion, but only acts over very short distances, about the width of a couple of nucleons.

Neutrons add strong-force attraction without adding repulsion, so they help hold a nucleus together. Big nuclei need more and more neutrons; beyond lead, no arrangement is completely stable, and these heavy nuclei are radioactive.

QUESTION ONEGold foil · evidence for the nucleus

In Rutherford's experiment, alpha particles were fired at a very thin gold foil.

(a) Describe the three main observations. A

(b) Explain what each observation shows about the structure of the atom. M

(c) Explain why the results could not be explained by Thomson's model, and why the experiment used very thin foil in a vacuum. E

Show model answer · Question One

(a) Most alpha particles passed straight through; a small number were deflected through large angles; about 1 in 8000 bounced back.

(b) Going straight through: the alpha particles met nothing that could deflect them, so most of the atom is empty space. Large deflections: a strong repulsive force acted on a few of them, so there is a concentration of positive charge (alpha particles are positive). Bouncing back: only a head-on approach to something much more massive than the alpha particle could reverse it, so the positive charge and almost all the mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus.

(c) In Thomson's model the positive charge is spread through the whole atom, so the electric force on an alpha particle anywhere in the atom is small, and all the alpha particles would pass through with tiny deflections. Nothing in that model is concentrated enough to turn an alpha particle back. Thin foil means each alpha particle passes only a few atoms, so a large deflection must come from a single close encounter, not many small ones added together. The vacuum stops the alpha particles being absorbed by air, because they only travel a few centimetres in air.

QUESTION TWOUranium isotopes · notation

Natural uranium contains \(^{238}_{92}\mathrm{U}\) and a small amount of \(^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U}\).

(a) State the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in a neutral atom of uranium-235. A

(b) Explain why these two atoms are called isotopes, and why they can't be separated by a chemical reaction. M

Show model answer · Question Two

(a) 92 protons, 235 − 92 = 143 neutrons, 92 electrons.

(b) Both have 92 protons, so both are uranium, but U-238 has 146 neutrons and U-235 has 143: the same element with different numbers of neutrons. Chemical reactions depend on the electrons, and both have the same 92 electrons arranged the same way, so they react identically. They can only be separated by methods that depend on their slightly different masses.

QUESTION THREEHolding the nucleus together · forces

A helium-4 nucleus contains two protons and two neutrons.

(a) Explain why the two protons repel each other strongly. A

(b) Explain why the nucleus doesn't fly apart, and why neutrons make nuclei more stable. E

Show model answer · Question Three

(a) Both are positive, and like charges repel. They are only about \(10^{-15}\) m apart, and the electrostatic force gets much stronger as the separation decreases, so the repulsion is large.

(b) All four nucleons attract their neighbours through the strong nuclear force. At nuclear distances this attraction is stronger than the electrostatic repulsion between the protons, so the nucleus stays together. The strong force has a very short range, so it only acts between neighbouring nucleons, while the repulsion acts between every pair of protons. Neutrons add strong-force attraction but no charge, so they bind the nucleus without adding any repulsion. That's why larger nuclei need proportionally more neutrons to be stable.

Phase 2.1 · Radioactivity foundations

FOUNDATIONS

An unstable nucleus becomes more stable by giving out radiation. You can't predict when any one nucleus will decay, but with billions of them the pattern is perfectly regular.

Why ionising and penetrating are opposites

Radiation ionises atoms by knocking electrons off them, and each ionisation uses up some of its energy. An alpha particle is big, slow and doubly charged, so it interacts with almost every atom it passes: it causes about 10 000 ionisations per centimetre of air and loses all its energy within a few centimetres. Beta is smaller, faster and singly charged, so it ionises less and travels further. Gamma has no charge and rarely interacts, so it ionises least and penetrates furthest.

That explains the safety rules: outside the body, alpha is the least dangerous (your dead skin stops it); inside the body (breathed in or swallowed), alpha is the most dangerous, because all its ionisation happens in living cells.

Deflection in fields

Alpha and beta are charged, so electric and magnetic fields push them sideways, in opposite directions (opposite charges). Beta is deflected far more, because its mass is about 7000 times smaller, even though its charge is half as big. Gamma has no charge and goes straight on.

Nuclear equations

In every nuclear change, the total mass number (top) and the total atomic number (bottom) are the same on both sides.

Alpha decay

\(^{226}_{88}\mathrm{Ra} \rightarrow{}^{222}_{86}\mathrm{Rn} +{}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\)

A falls by 4, Z falls by 2: a new element.

Beta decay

\(^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C} \rightarrow{}^{14}_{7}\mathrm{N} +{}^{\;\,0}_{-1}e\)

A neutron turns into a proton and an electron: Z rises by 1, A stays the same.

Gamma emission

\(^{60}_{28}\mathrm{Ni}^{*} \rightarrow{}^{60}_{28}\mathrm{Ni} + \gamma\)

The nucleus loses energy only; A and Z don't change. Usually follows alpha or beta decay.

Half-life

Decay is random: every unstable nucleus has the same chance of decaying in the next second, whatever has happened before. So in each equal time a fixed fraction decays. The half-life is the time for half the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay, which is also the time for the activity (decays per second, becquerels, Bq) to halve.

\(N = N_{0}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{n}, \quad n = \frac{t}{t_{1/2}}\)

Example. Iodine-131 has a half-life of 8.0 days. A sample of 400 MBq after 24 days (3 half-lives): 400 → 200 → 100 → 50 MBq. After 32 days, \(\frac{1}{16}\) of the activity, 25 MBq, remains.

Half-lives range from fractions of a second to billions of years (uranium-238: 4.5 billion years). The half-life can't be changed by heating, pressure or chemistry.

Background radiation, uses and safety

Background radiation is always present: radon gas from rocks and soil, cosmic rays, food and water (potassium-40), buildings, medical scans. Subtract the background count from every measurement.

Uses of alpha

Smoke detectors (americium-241): alpha ionises the air so a small current flows; smoke absorbs the alpha and the current drops.

Uses of beta

Thickness control for paper or foil: more absorbed, the sheet is too thick. Tracers. Radiocarbon dating (carbon-14, half-life 5730 years).

Uses of gamma

Medical imaging (technetium-99m), killing cancer cells, sterilising equipment and food, finding leaks in pipes.

Safety: keep exposure short, stay far away, use shielding, and handle sources with tongs, never pointing them at people.

QUESTION TWODecay chain · nuclear equations

(a) Radium-226 (\(^{226}_{88}\mathrm{Ra}\)) decays by alpha emission. Write the equation, naming the daughter. A

(b) Strontium-90 (\(^{90}_{38}\mathrm{Sr}\)) decays by beta emission. Write the equation and explain what happens inside the nucleus. M

(c) Uranium-238 (Z = 92) eventually becomes lead-206 (Z = 82) through a series of alpha and beta decays. Show how many of each there must be. E

Show model answer · Question Two

(a) \(^{226}_{88}\mathrm{Ra} \rightarrow{}^{222}_{86}\mathrm{Rn} +{}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\): radon-222. (226 = 222 + 4; 88 = 86 + 2.)

(b) \(^{90}_{38}\mathrm{Sr} \rightarrow{}^{90}_{39}\mathrm{Y} +{}^{\;\,0}_{-1}e\): yttrium-90. A neutron in the nucleus changes into a proton, which stays, and an electron, which is ejected at high speed as the beta particle. The nucleon number stays at 90 but there is one more proton, so Z rises from 38 to 39 and the element changes.

(c) Only alpha decay changes A, by 4 each time: 238 − 206 = 32, so there are 8 alpha decays. They lower Z by 8 × 2 = 16, from 92 to 76. But lead has Z = 82, so Z must have gone back up by 6: 6 beta decays, each raising Z by 1 without changing A.

QUESTION THREEMedical tracer · half-life

Iodine-131 (half-life 8.0 days) is used to treat thyroid conditions. A patient is given a dose with an activity of 400 MBq.

(a) Calculate the activity after 24 days. A

(b) Calculate how long it takes for the activity to fall to 25 MBq. M

(c) Explain why a medical isotope should have a short half-life, but not too short. E

Show model answer · Question Three

(a) 24 ÷ 8.0 = 3 half-lives: 400 → 200 → 100 → 50 MBq.

(b) 400 → 25 is a factor of 16 = 2⁴, so 4 half-lives = 32 days.

(c) The radiation ionises the patient's cells, so the activity should drop quickly once it has done its job: a short half-life keeps the total dose low and the patient isn't radioactive for long. But if the half-life were too short (minutes), much of the activity would have gone before the isotope could be made, transported and reach the right part of the body, so a bigger initial dose would be needed. A half-life of hours to days balances these.

QUESTION FOURSmoke detector · choosing a source

Smoke detectors contain a tiny amount of americium-241, an alpha emitter with a half-life of 432 years.

(a) Explain how the alpha particles allow the detector to sense smoke. M

(b) Discuss why americium-241 is a good choice, and why it is safe in a home. E

Show model answer · Question Four

(a) The alpha particles strongly ionise the air between two charged plates, so ions carry a small current across the gap. Smoke particles absorb the alpha particles and attach to the ions, so fewer ions reach the plates, the current falls, and the alarm sounds.

(b) Alpha is the most ionising radiation, so a tiny source gives a measurable current; it is also the least penetrating, so it can't get out of the plastic casing (or travel more than a few centimetres in air), keeping the radiation inside the detector. A 432-year half-life means the activity barely changes over the detector's 10-year life, so it doesn't need recalibrating. The danger would only come from getting the americium inside the body, where the alpha would ionise living cells: so detectors must not be broken open.

Phase 3.1 · Nuclear energy foundations

FOUNDATIONS

Nuclear reactions release millions of times more energy per kilogram than burning fuel. Where does it come from? From mass itself.

Mass–energy equivalence: \(E = mc^{2}\)

Einstein showed that mass is a form of energy. When a nuclear reaction happens, the particles that come out have slightly less mass than those that went in. That missing mass (the mass defect) has been converted into energy, mostly the kinetic energy of the products:

\(E = mc^{2}\)

Because \(c^{2} = 9.00 \times 10^{16}\), a tiny mass gives a huge energy. Example. Converting 1.0 g (\(1.0 \times 10^{-3}\) kg) completely: \(E = 1.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 9.00 \times 10^{16} = 9.0 \times 10^{13}\,\mathrm{J}\), about the energy of 2000 tonnes of petrol.

Fission

A uranium-235 nucleus absorbs a slow neutron, becomes very unstable and splits into two medium-sized nuclei, releasing 2 or 3 fast neutrons and about 200 MeV of energy. One possibility:

\(^{1}_{0}n +{}^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U} \rightarrow{}^{141}_{56}\mathrm{Ba} +{}^{92}_{36}\mathrm{Kr} + 3\,{}^{1}_{0}n\)

Check: top 1 + 235 = 141 + 92 + 3 = 236; bottom 0 + 92 = 56 + 36 + 0 = 92. The products have about \(3.2 \times 10^{-28}\) kg less mass, so \(E = 3.2 \times 10^{-28} \times 9.00 \times 10^{16} = 2.9 \times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{J}\) per fission.

Chain reactions

Each fission releases 2–3 neutrons, which can cause more fissions, which release more neutrons… If on average more than one neutron from each fission causes another fission, the reaction grows explosively (a bomb). If exactly one does, it runs steadily (a reactor). If fewer, it dies out.

A lump of uranium smaller than the critical mass loses too many neutrons through its surface for a chain reaction to keep going.

Inside a nuclear reactor
Fuel rods

Uranium enriched in U-235. Fission heats them.

Moderator

Water or graphite slows the fast neutrons down, because U-235 captures slow neutrons far more easily.

Control rods

Boron or cadmium absorb neutrons. Lowering them in slows the chain reaction; raising them speeds it up; dropping them fully shuts it down.

Coolant

Water or gas carries heat from the core to make steam.

Turbine & generator

Steam spins a turbine connected to a generator, as in any thermal power station.

Shielding & waste

Thick concrete stops the radiation. The fission products are intensely radioactive, some with very long half-lives, and must be stored safely for thousands of years.

Fusion

Two light nuclei join to form a heavier one, again with a loss of mass. The reaction planned for fusion reactors:

\(^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H} +{}^{3}_{1}\mathrm{H} \rightarrow{}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He} +{}^{1}_{0}n\)

The mass lost is \(3.13 \times 10^{-29}\) kg, releasing \(2.8 \times 10^{-12}\) J (17.6 MeV). That's less per reaction than fission, but far more per kilogram of fuel, because the nuclei are so light.

Both nuclei are positive and repel strongly. To get close enough for the strong force to take over, they must collide at enormous speeds, which means temperatures of over 100 million °C. In the Sun, gravity squeezes the core to about 15 million °C and huge densities, and fusion turns hydrogen into helium. The Sun loses about \(4 \times 10^{9}\) kg of mass every second.

Power: \(P = \frac{E}{t}\)

Power is the rate of energy transfer, in watts (J s⁻¹). A reactor producing \(3.0 \times 10^{9}\) W of heat needs \(\frac{3.0 \times 10^{9}}{2.9 \times 10^{-11}} = 1.0 \times 10^{20}\) fissions every second, and converts \(\frac{3.0 \times 10^{9}}{9.00 \times 10^{16}} = 3.3 \times 10^{-8}\) kg of mass to energy every second: about 3 g a day.

Phase 3.5 · Practice questions: nuclear energy

PRACTICE

Three original questions in the style of AS91172 assessments. \(c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

QUESTION ONEFission of uranium-235

\(^{1}_{0}n +{}^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U} \rightarrow{}^{141}_{56}\mathrm{Ba} +{}^{92}_{36}\mathrm{Kr} + x\,{}^{1}_{0}n\)

(a) Find x, showing that mass number and atomic number are conserved. A

(b) The products have \(3.2 \times 10^{-28}\) kg less mass than the reactants. Calculate the energy released. M

(c) Explain how this reaction can become a chain reaction, and how a reactor keeps it under control. E

Show model answer · Question One

(a) Mass numbers: 1 + 235 = 236 = 141 + 92 + x, so x = 3. Atomic numbers: 0 + 92 = 56 + 36 + 3(0) = 92 ✓.

(b) \(E = mc^{2} = 3.2 \times 10^{-28} \times (3.00 \times 10^{8})^{2} = 2.9 \times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{J}\) (about 180 MeV).

(c) Each fission releases 3 neutrons. If these are absorbed by other U-235 nuclei, each causes another fission releasing 3 more neutrons, so the number of fissions can multiply rapidly. In a reactor, the moderator slows the neutrons so U-235 can capture them, and the control rods absorb some of the neutrons. They are adjusted so that, on average, exactly one neutron from each fission causes another fission. The rate of fission then stays constant, giving a steady power output. Pushing the rods further in absorbs more neutrons and the reaction slows or stops.

QUESTION TWOPower station · P = E/t

A nuclear power station produces \(3.0 \times 10^{9}\) W of heat from fission. Each fission releases \(2.9 \times 10^{-11}\) J.

(a) Calculate the energy produced in one day. A

(b) Calculate the mass converted into energy in one day. M

(c) Calculate the number of fissions per second, and explain why the mass of uranium used each day is much larger than your answer to (b). E

Show model answer · Question Two

(a) \(E = Pt = 3.0 \times 10^{9} \times 86\,400 = 2.6 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{J}\)

(b) \(m = \frac{E}{c^{2}} = \frac{2.59 \times 10^{14}}{9.00 \times 10^{16}} = 2.9 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg}\) (about 3 g).

(c) \(\frac{3.0 \times 10^{9}}{2.9 \times 10^{-11}} = 1.0 \times 10^{20}\) fissions per second. Only the mass lost in each fission (about 0.1% of the uranium nucleus's mass) becomes energy; the rest of the uranium becomes the fission products. So the uranium-235 that splits each day is about 1000 times more than 3 g: roughly 3 kg. In practice the fuel is mostly U-238, which hardly splits, so many tonnes of fuel are loaded at a time.

QUESTION THREEFusion · the Sun and future reactors

\(^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H} +{}^{3}_{1}\mathrm{H} \rightarrow{}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He} +{}^{1}_{0}n\)

(a) The mass lost in each reaction is \(3.13 \times 10^{-29}\) kg. Calculate the energy released in joules and in MeV. M

(b) The Sun's power output is \(3.8 \times 10^{26}\) W. Calculate the mass it loses every second. M

(c) Explain why fusion only happens at extremely high temperatures, and discuss one advantage and one difficulty of fusion compared with fission for generating electricity. E

Show model answer · Question Three

(a) \(E = mc^{2} = 3.13 \times 10^{-29} \times 9.00 \times 10^{16} = 2.82 \times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{J}\); in MeV: \(\frac{2.82 \times 10^{-12}}{1.60 \times 10^{-13}} = 17.6\,\mathrm{MeV}\).

(b) In one second \(E = Pt = 3.8 \times 10^{26}\,\mathrm{J}\), so \(m = \frac{3.8 \times 10^{26}}{9.00 \times 10^{16}} = 4.2 \times 10^{9}\,\mathrm{kg}\) every second.

(c) Both nuclei are positively charged, so they repel. The strong force that joins them only acts at about \(10^{-15}\) m, so they must collide fast enough to overcome the repulsion and get that close. Only at temperatures of many millions of degrees are the nuclei moving fast enough. Advantage: the fuel (deuterium from seawater) is plentiful, there is no long-lived radioactive waste, and a runaway chain reaction can't happen. Difficulty: no material can hold a plasma at 100 million °C, so it has to be held by magnetic fields; so far, fusion reactors haven't produced much more energy than is needed to run them.
