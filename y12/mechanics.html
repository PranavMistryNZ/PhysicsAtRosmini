The big idea that links everything: forces change motion

Every part of this standard comes back to Newton's laws. A net force causes acceleration (F = ma). Acting over a time, it changes momentum (FΔt = Δp). Acting over a distance, it does work and changes energy (W = Fd). Projectiles and circular motion are just two special cases: a constant force downwards, and a force that always points to the centre.

Phase 0.2 · Exam skills: answers that earn marks

BRIEFING

Knowing the physics is half the job. The other half is showing it the way the marking schedule rewards. The grade depends on how deep your answer goes.

ACHIEVEMENT

Describe, identify, calculate (one step). Show you know which simple idea applies. Name the force, state the law, or get a one-step answer with the right unit.

"The horizontal velocity stays constant because no horizontal force acts."

MERIT

Explain, multi-step calculate. Give the physics reason, not just the fact. Calculations where the useful number isn't handed to you, such as finding time first and then range.

"Gravity is the only force, so vx is unchanged, while vy drops by 9.8 m s⁻¹ every second until it is zero at the top."

EXCELLENCE

Connect concepts. Link two or more ideas into one complete argument, such as impulse and momentum, or energy and friction, and use that link to reach a conclusion.

"Δp is the same either way, so a longer stopping time Δt means a smaller average force, because F = Δp ÷ Δt."

A structure for "explain" questions

Name itName the physics: the law, the force or the quantity that is conserved.
Apply itSay what that idea means for this situation, using the objects in the question.
Link itConnect cause to effect with "so", "therefore" or "because".
ConcludeAnswer the exact question asked: bigger, smaller, the same, or a direction.

Lay out every calculation the same way

Equation

Write the equation you are using first, in symbols.

Substitute

Put the numbers in with SI units. Show any conversions (km h⁻¹ → m s⁻¹, g → kg, cm → m).

Answer

Give the answer with a unit, a sensible number of significant figures and a direction for vectors.

A wrong final answer can still gain credit if the marker can follow a correct method. A bare number cannot.

Do this · avoid this

Do this

  • Choose a positive direction and stick to it, especially for momentum and free fall.
  • Split projectile motion into horizontal and vertical parts linked only by time.
  • Say which real force provides the centripetal force.
  • For torque, pick the pivot where an unknown force acts so it drops out.
  • Check the answer is physically sensible (a car does not reach 300 m s⁻¹).

Avoid this

  • Saying "centrifugal force pushes it outwards". The net force points inwards.
  • Using the slope length instead of the vertical height in mgΔh.
  • Forgetting that a rebound reverses velocity, so Δp is bigger, not smaller.
  • Calling a Newton's third law pair "balanced". They act on different objects.
  • Writing "energy is lost". Say where it went: heat, sound, deformation.

Phase 1.1 · Motion foundations

FOUNDATIONS

Five ideas underpin everything in this module. Open each panel for the detail you need.

1.1.1Scalars and vectors

A scalar has size only: distance, speed, time, mass, energy. A vector has size and direction: displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, momentum.

A rover drives 30 m east then 10 m west. Its distance is 40 m, but its displacement is 20 m east.

In one dimension, direction is shown with a sign. Choose which way is positive at the start of every question and stick to it.

1.1.2Velocity and acceleration

Velocity is the rate of change of displacement: v = Δd ÷ Δt. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity: a = Δv ÷ Δt, in m s⁻².

An acceleration of 3 m s⁻² means the velocity changes by 3 m s⁻¹ every second. If the acceleration points opposite to the velocity, the object slows down. Exams often call this deceleration, but in the equations it is a negative a.

1.1.3Reading motion graphs

d–t graph: the gradient is the velocity. A straight line means constant velocity. A curve getting steeper means speeding up.

v–t graph: the gradient is the acceleration and the area under the line is the displacement.

1.1.4Free fall

When air resistance is negligible, every object near Earth's surface accelerates downwards at g = 9.8 m s⁻², whatever its mass.

An object thrown straight up slows by 9.8 m s⁻¹ every second, stops for an instant at the top, then speeds up on the way down. At the top v = 0 but a is still 9.8 m s⁻² down. Without air resistance the up and down journeys take equal times.

1.1.5Projectiles: two motions at once

After launch, the only force on a projectile (ignoring air resistance) is its weight, which acts straight down. So the two directions behave completely differently:

Horizontal

No force, so no acceleration. vx is constant and dx = vxt.

Vertical

a = 9.8 m s⁻² down. Use the constant-acceleration equations with vy.

The only thing the two directions share is time. Find it from whichever direction you have enough information for, then use it in the other.

QUESTION ONERover test run · straight-line motion

A test rover starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at 2.5 m s⁻² for 8.0 s. It then brakes uniformly and stops in a further 50 m.

(a) Calculate the rover's top speed. A

(b) Calculate the distance covered while accelerating. A

(c) Calculate the braking acceleration and the time taken to stop. M

(d) Describe the shape of the v–t graph for the whole run and use it to find the average speed. E

Show worked answer · Question One

(a) vf = vi + at = 0 + 2.5 × 8.0 = 20 m s⁻¹

(b) d = vit + ½at² = 0 + ½ × 2.5 × 8.0² = 80 m

(c) No time is given, so use vf² = vi² + 2ad: 0 = 20² + 2a(50), so a = −4.0 m s⁻². The minus sign shows the acceleration is opposite to the motion. Time: t = Δv ÷ a = (0 − 20) ÷ (−4.0) = 5.0 s.

(d) A straight line rising from 0 to 20 m s⁻¹ over 8.0 s, then a steeper straight line falling to 0 at 13.0 s. The area under the graph is the total distance: 80 m + 50 m = 130 m. Average speed = 130 ÷ 13.0 = 10 m s⁻¹. This is half the top speed because both stages are uniform changes from or to rest.

Marking focus: equation, substitution and unit in each part; the sign of the braking acceleration explained; graph features linked to the calculations.

QUESTION TWODrone drop · free fall

A drone hovers 45 m above flat ground and releases a camera. Ignore air resistance.

(a) Calculate how long the camera takes to hit the ground. A

(b) Calculate the camera's speed just before it hits the ground. M

(c) The drone repeats the drop, but this time it is rising at 5.0 m s⁻¹ when it releases the camera from 45 m. Explain why the camera first moves upwards, and calculate the new fall time. E

Show worked answer · Question Two

(a) Take down as positive. d = vit + ½at²: 45 = 0 + ½ × 9.8 × t², so t = √(90 ÷ 9.8) = 3.0 s (3.03 s).

(b) vf² = 0 + 2 × 9.8 × 45 = 882, so vf = 29.7 m s⁻¹ (about 30 m s⁻¹).

(c) At release the camera has the drone's velocity, 5.0 m s⁻¹ upwards. Nothing pushes it up any more, but it keeps that velocity while gravity slows it down, so it rises briefly before falling. Take up as positive: −45 = 5.0t − 4.9t², so 4.9t² − 5.0t − 45 = 0. The positive solution is t = (5.0 + √(25 + 882)) ÷ 9.8 = 3.6 s (3.58 s). This is longer than before because it first has to go up and stop.

Marking focus: a consistent sign convention; the camera keeps the drone's velocity (Newton's first law); the quadratic set up correctly.

QUESTION THREECliff kick · horizontal launch

A ball is kicked horizontally at 12 m s⁻¹ from the top of a 20 m cliff. Ignore air resistance.

(a) Calculate the time the ball is in the air. A

(b) Calculate how far from the base of the cliff the ball lands. M

(c) Calculate the ball's velocity (size and direction) just before it lands. M

(d) The ball is kicked at 24 m s⁻¹ instead. Explain what happens to the flight time and the range. E

Show worked answer · Question Three

(a) Vertically, vi = 0 because the kick is horizontal. 20 = ½ × 9.8 × t², so t = 2.02 s.

(b) Horizontally, the velocity is constant: dx = 12 × 2.02 = 24.2 m.

(c) vy = 9.8 × 2.02 = 19.8 m s⁻¹ down and vx = 12 m s⁻¹. v = √(12² + 19.8²) = 23.2 m s⁻¹ at tan⁻¹(19.8 ÷ 12) = 59° below the horizontal.

(d) The flight time depends only on the vertical motion: the same 20 m drop with vy starting at 0. So the time stays 2.02 s. The horizontal velocity doubles over the same time, so the range doubles to 48.5 m.

Marking focus: independence of horizontal and vertical motion stated and used; vector addition for the final velocity with a direction.

QUESTION FOURProbe launch · angled projectile

A probe is launched from level ground at 20 m s⁻¹ at 40° above the horizontal. Ignore air resistance.

(a) Calculate the horizontal and vertical components of the launch velocity. A

(b) Calculate the time of flight and the range. M

(c) Calculate the maximum height. M

(d) Explain why a launch at 50° at the same speed lands at the same place, and describe how air resistance would change the path. E

Show worked answer · Question Four

(a) vx = 20 cos 40° = 15.3 m s⁻¹, vy = 20 sin 40° = 12.9 m s⁻¹

(b) Time to the top: t = 12.86 ÷ 9.8 = 1.31 s. The flight is symmetric, so the total time is 2.62 s. Range: 15.32 × 2.62 = 40.2 m.

(c) h = vy² ÷ 2g = 12.86² ÷ 19.6 = 8.4 m

(d) At 50°, vy is larger (so the probe is in the air longer) but vx is smaller. The two changes exactly balance: 20 sin 50° and 20 cos 50° are 20 cos 40° and 20 sin 40° swapped, so the product vx × time is the same and the range is 40.2 m again. Angles that add to 90° give equal ranges on level ground. Air resistance acts against the velocity, so vx falls during the flight and the upward climb is shorter. The probe reaches a lower maximum height, has a shorter range, and comes down more steeply than it went up, so the path is no longer symmetric.

Marking focus: components; the link between time, range and height; a reasoned comparison rather than a statement.

Phase 2.1 · Force foundations

FOUNDATIONS

Forces are pushes and pulls, measured in newtons. Every force is a vector, so direction matters as much as size.

2.1.1Newton's three laws
First law

If the net force is zero, an object stays at rest or keeps moving at a constant velocity.

Second law

A net force causes acceleration in the direction of the net force: F = ma.

Third law

If A pushes on B, B pushes back on A with an equal and opposite force. The pair act on different objects, so they never cancel.

2.1.2Net force and free-body diagrams

Draw the object as a dot or box and add an arrow for every force acting on it, starting at the object. Add the forces as vectors to find the net force. Only the net force goes into F = ma.

Here the vertical forces balance. The horizontal net force is thrust minus friction, and that sets the acceleration.

2.1.3Mass and weight

Mass (kg) is the amount of matter and resists acceleration. Weight (N) is the gravitational force on that mass: Fg = mg. A 60 kg astronaut weighs 588 N on Earth, about 97 N on the Moon, but still has a mass of 60 kg.

2.1.4Adding and resolving forces

Add forces tip to tail. For forces at right angles, use Pythagoras for the size and trigonometry for the direction.

Any single force can be split into two perpendicular components that together have the same effect: Fx = F cos θ, Fy = F sin θ (θ measured from the horizontal).

2.1.5Torque and equilibrium

A torque is the turning effect of a force: τ = Fd, where d is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of the force.

An object is in equilibrium when both conditions hold: the net force is zero (so it doesn't accelerate) and the net torque about any point is zero (so it doesn't start to rotate). The weight of a uniform object acts at its centre.

2.1.6Springs: Hooke's law

Up to its limit of proportionality, a spring's extension is proportional to the force: F = −kx. The minus sign shows the spring pulls back, opposite to the extension. A stiffer spring has a larger k (N m⁻¹).

QUESTION ONETransport vehicle · net force

A 1200 kg transport vehicle on a flat road has a driving force of 3600 N. Friction and air resistance total 1200 N.

(a) Calculate the weight of the vehicle. A

(b) Calculate the vehicle's acceleration. A

(c) As the vehicle speeds up, air resistance increases until the resistive forces total 3600 N. Explain what happens to the vehicle's motion. M

Show worked answer · Question One

(a) Fg = mg = 1200 × 9.8 = 1.18 × 10⁴ N

(b) Net force = 3600 − 1200 = 2400 N forwards. a = F ÷ m = 2400 ÷ 1200 = 2.0 m s⁻²

(c) The forward and backward forces are now equal, so the net force is zero. By Newton's first law the acceleration is zero: the vehicle keeps moving at a constant (maximum) speed. It doesn't stop. Zero net force means no change in velocity.

QUESTION TWOCargo crate · components

A 40 kg cargo crate is pulled across a floor by a rope at 30° above the horizontal with a tension of 150 N. Friction on the crate is 90 N.

(a) Calculate the horizontal and vertical components of the rope's pull. A

(b) Calculate the crate's acceleration. M

(c) Explain why the support force from the floor is less than the crate's weight, and calculate it. E

Show worked answer · Question Two

(a) Fx = 150 cos 30° = 130 N, Fy = 150 sin 30° = 75 N upwards.

(b) Net horizontal force = 129.9 − 90 = 39.9 N. a = 39.9 ÷ 40 = 1.0 m s⁻²

(c) The crate does not accelerate vertically, so the vertical forces balance: support + 75 N = weight. The rope is already holding up part of the weight, so the floor needs to push up less. Support force = 40 × 9.8 − 75 = 392 − 75 = 317 N.

QUESTION THREEPainter's plank · torque

A uniform plank 4.0 m long with a mass of 20 kg rests on supports at each end, A and B. A 60 kg technician stands 1.0 m from A.

(a) State the two conditions for the plank to be in equilibrium. A

(b) Calculate the support forces at A and B. M

(c) Support B is moved to 3.0 m from A. The technician walks towards the overhanging end. Calculate how far from A the technician can stand before the plank starts to tip, and explain your reasoning. E

Show worked answer · Question Three

(a) The net force on the plank is zero, and the net torque about any point is zero.

(b) Take torques about A so the unknown force at A has no torque. Clockwise: 20 × 9.8 × 2.0 + 60 × 9.8 × 1.0 = 392 + 588 = 980 N m. Anticlockwise: FB × 4.0. So FB = 245 N. Forces balance: FA = 80 × 9.8 − 245 = 539 N.

(c) Just before the plank tips, it lifts off support A, so FA = 0 and B is the pivot. Torques about B: the plank's weight acts at 2.0 m, which is 1.0 m on the A side of B: 196 × 1.0 = 196 N m. The technician, a distance y beyond B: 588 × y. Balance: y = 0.33 m. So the technician can stand up to 3.3 m from A. Any further and the technician's torque about B is bigger than the plank's, and the plank rotates about B.

QUESTION FOURSuspension spring · Hooke's law

A spring has spring constant 250 N m⁻¹. A 2.0 kg instrument hangs from it at rest.

(a) Calculate the spring's extension. A

(b) Calculate the elastic potential energy stored. M

(c) Explain why the energy stored is ½Fx and not Fx, and what happens to the energy stored if the mass is doubled. E

Show worked answer · Question Four

(a) At rest, kx = mg: x = (2.0 × 9.8) ÷ 250 = 0.078 m (7.8 cm).

(b) Ep = ½kx² = ½ × 250 × 0.0784² = 0.77 J

(c) The energy stored equals the work done stretching the spring, which is the area under the F–x graph. The force doesn't stay at its final value. It rises steadily from zero, so the average force is half the final force and the area is a triangle: ½Fx = ½kx². Doubling the mass doubles the force and so doubles x. Energy depends on x², so it increases four times, to 3.1 J.

Phase 3.1 · Circular motion foundations

FOUNDATIONS

At Level 2 you study objects moving in a horizontal circle at constant speed, where one force alone provides the centripetal force.

3.1.1Constant speed, changing velocity

Velocity is a vector. Moving in a circle, the direction keeps changing even if the speed doesn't, so the velocity is changing and the object is accelerating.

The velocity at any instant is along the tangent to the circle.

3.1.2Centripetal acceleration points to the centre

Compare the velocity at two nearby points. The change, Δv = v2 − v1, points towards the centre, so the acceleration points towards the centre too: ac = v² ÷ r.

3.1.3Centripetal force is a job, not a new force

By Newton's second law, an acceleration towards the centre needs a net force towards the centre: Fc = mv² ÷ r. This is not an extra force to add to a diagram. It is the role played by a real force:

Ball on a string

Tension in the string

Car on a flat bend

Friction between tyres and road

Satellite

Gravity from the planet

3.1.4Period and speed

The period T is the time for one revolution. In that time the object travels the circumference, so v = 2πr ÷ T. Frequency is revolutions per second: f = 1 ÷ T.

3.1.5When the force is removed

If the string snaps, there is no longer a net force. By Newton's first law the object moves off in a straight line along the tangent at constant speed. It does not fly outwards along the radius. There is no "centrifugal force" in this model; the feeling of being thrown outwards is your body trying to carry straight on.

QUESTION ONETethered sample · string tension

A 0.50 kg sample canister is whirled in a horizontal circle of radius 0.80 m on a smooth table at a constant 4.0 m s⁻¹.

(a) Calculate the centripetal acceleration. A

(b) Calculate the tension in the string. A

(c) Explain why the canister is accelerating even though its speed is constant. M

(d) The string snaps. Describe and explain the canister's path. M

Show worked answer · Question One

(a) ac = v² ÷ r = 4.0² ÷ 0.80 = 20 m s⁻² towards the centre.

(b) The tension is the centripetal force: F = ma = 0.50 × 20 = 10 N.

(c) Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. Velocity is a vector, and its direction is always changing as the canister goes round, so the velocity changes and the canister accelerates. The change in velocity points towards the centre.

(d) With no string there is no net horizontal force, so by Newton's first law the canister moves in a straight line at 4.0 m s⁻¹ along the tangent to the circle at the point where the string broke.

QUESTION TWORover on a bend · friction

A 1100 kg rover drives around a flat bend of radius 50 m. The maximum friction force the tyres can provide sideways is 6600 N.

(a) Name the force that provides the centripetal force. A

(b) Calculate the maximum speed the rover can take the bend at. M

(c) Explain why the rover skids if it enters the bend at double this speed, and say which way it moves. E

Show worked answer · Question Two

(a) Sideways friction between the tyres and the road, acting towards the centre of the bend.

(b) F = mv² ÷ r, so v = √(Fr ÷ m) = √(6600 × 50 ÷ 1100) = √300 = 17 m s⁻¹ (17.3 m s⁻¹, about 62 km h⁻¹).

(c) Fc ∝ v², so doubling the speed needs four times the force: 26 400 N. Friction can provide only 6600 N, so the net inward force is too small for that radius. The rover cannot turn tightly enough and slides towards the outside of the bend, following a wider path closer to the tangent. It is not pushed outwards; there just isn't enough inward force.

QUESTION THREETraining roundabout · period

A 25 kg cadet sits 2.0 m from the centre of a roundabout that makes one revolution every 4.0 s.

(a) Calculate the cadet's speed. A

(b) Calculate the centripetal force on the cadet. M

(c) The cadet says, "I feel pushed outwards, so there must be an outward force on me." Explain what is really happening. E

Show worked answer · Question Three

(a) v = 2πr ÷ T = 2π × 2.0 ÷ 4.0 = 3.1 m s⁻¹

(b) F = mv² ÷ r = 25 × 3.14² ÷ 2.0 = 123 N towards the centre.

(c) The cadet's body tends to keep moving in a straight line (inertia, Newton's first law). The seat or rail has to push the cadet inwards to change their direction. The cadet feels the seat pressing against them and wrongly interprets it as being pushed outwards. The net force on the cadet is 123 N inwards; there is no outward force.

QUESTION FOURSpace station · gravity as centripetal force

A space station of mass 4.2 × 10⁵ kg orbits at radius 6.78 × 10⁶ m from Earth's centre at 7.66 × 10³ m s⁻¹.

(a) Calculate the station's centripetal acceleration. M

(b) Calculate the gravitational force on the station. M

(c) Explain why astronauts inside appear weightless, even though gravity still acts on them. E

Show worked answer · Question Four

(a) a = v² ÷ r = (7.66 × 10³)² ÷ 6.78 × 10⁶ = 8.65 m s⁻² towards Earth's centre.

(b) Gravity provides the whole centripetal force: F = ma = 4.2 × 10⁵ × 8.65 = 3.6 × 10⁶ N.

(c) Gravity is still strong at this height: the acceleration is 8.65 m s⁻², not much less than 9.8 m s⁻². The station and the astronauts are both in free fall, accelerating towards Earth at the same rate, so the floor does not need to push on the astronauts. With no support force, they feel weightless. Their sideways speed is so large that they keep "falling around" Earth instead of into it.

Phase 4.1 · Momentum foundations

FOUNDATIONS

Momentum is how hard it is to stop something. All the collision questions in this standard are in one dimension, so direction is shown with a sign.

4.1.1Momentum is a vector

p = mv, in kg m s⁻¹. It has the same direction as the velocity. A 2.0 kg trolley at 3.0 m s⁻¹ to the left has p = −6.0 kg m s⁻¹ if right is positive.

4.1.2Change in momentum: watch the signs

Δp = pf − pi = m(vf − vi). A 0.15 kg ball hits a wall at +12 m s⁻¹ and rebounds at −8.0 m s⁻¹: Δp = 0.15(−8.0 − 12) = −3.0 kg m s⁻¹. A bounce changes momentum more than just stopping, because the object has to stop and then go back.

4.1.3Impulse

From Newton's second law, F = Δp ÷ Δt, so Δp = FΔt. This quantity is the impulse (N s). On a force–time graph, impulse is the area under the curve.

For a fixed change in momentum, a longer contact time means a smaller average force. That is the idea behind crumple zones, airbags, bending your knees when you land, and padded helmets.

4.1.4Conservation of momentum

When objects interact, they push on each other with equal and opposite forces (Newton's third law) for the same time. So the impulses are equal and opposite and the changes in momentum cancel.

total momentum before = total momentum after

This holds whenever no external force acts on the system (an isolated system). In practice, friction during the short collision is usually small enough to ignore.

4.1.5Collisions and explosions
Elastic

Momentum and kinetic energy conserved. Objects bounce apart.

Inelastic

Momentum conserved; some kinetic energy becomes heat, sound and deformation. If they stick together it is perfectly inelastic.

Explosion

Starts at rest, so total momentum is zero before and after. Pieces move apart in opposite directions; stored energy becomes kinetic energy.

QUESTION ONECapsule docking · conservation

A 2400 kg capsule moving at 0.60 m s⁻¹ docks with a 3600 kg module at rest. They lock together.

(a) Calculate the capsule's momentum before docking. A

(b) Calculate the velocity of the combined craft. M

(c) Show that kinetic energy is not conserved and explain where it went. E

Show worked answer · Question One

(a) p = mv = 2400 × 0.60 = 1440 kg m s⁻¹ in the capsule's direction of travel.

(b) Momentum is conserved: 1440 = (2400 + 3600)v, so v = 0.24 m s⁻¹ in the same direction.

(c) Before: ½ × 2400 × 0.60² = 432 J. After: ½ × 6000 × 0.24² = 173 J. About 259 J of kinetic energy was transformed into heat, sound and work done deforming the docking latches. Momentum is conserved because there is no external force, but the collision is inelastic.

QUESTION TWORebound · impulse with signs

A 0.15 kg ball hits a wall at 12 m s⁻¹ and rebounds along the same line at 8.0 m s⁻¹. It is in contact with the wall for 0.020 s.

(a) Calculate the change in momentum of the ball. M

(b) Calculate the average force the wall exerts on the ball. M

(c) Explain why the force would be smaller if the ball hit the wall at the same speed but stuck to it. E

Show worked answer · Question Two

(a) Take towards the wall as positive. Δp = m(vf − vi) = 0.15(−8.0 − 12) = −3.0 kg m s⁻¹: 3.0 kg m s⁻¹ away from the wall.

(b) F = Δp ÷ Δt = 3.0 ÷ 0.020 = 150 N away from the wall.

(c) Sticking, the ball only has to lose its 1.8 kg m s⁻¹ of momentum, so Δp = −1.8 kg m s⁻¹. Bouncing, the wall must stop the ball and then push it back, so Δp is larger. For the same contact time, F = Δp ÷ Δt is smaller when Δp is smaller (90 N instead of 150 N).

QUESTION THREESpacewalk · recoil from rest

An 80 kg astronaut floating at rest throws a 20 kg toolbox away at 3.0 m s⁻¹.

(a) State the total momentum of the astronaut and toolbox before the throw. A

(b) Calculate the astronaut's velocity after the throw. M

(c) Calculate the total kinetic energy after the throw and explain where it came from, and why momentum is conserved but kinetic energy is not. E

Show worked answer · Question Three

(a) Zero: neither is moving.

(b) 0 = 20 × 3.0 + 80v, so v = −0.75 m s⁻¹: 0.75 m s⁻¹ in the opposite direction to the toolbox.

(c) ½ × 20 × 3.0² + ½ × 80 × 0.75² = 90 + 22.5 = 113 J. It came from chemical energy in the astronaut's muscles. The forces between astronaut and toolbox are internal, equal and opposite, and act for the same time, so their impulses cancel and total momentum stays zero. Kinetic energy is not a conserved quantity on its own: energy can be converted into or out of kinetic form, and here it was converted into it.

QUESTION FOURCrash safety · impulse

A 70 kg driver is travelling at 15 m s⁻¹ when the vehicle crashes and stops.

(a) Calculate the driver's change in momentum. A

(b) The airbag brings the driver to rest in 0.15 s. Calculate the average force on the driver. M

(c) Without an airbag the driver would hit the dashboard and stop in about 0.010 s. Use impulse to explain how the airbag reduces injury. E

Show worked answer · Question Four

(a) Δp = 70(0 − 15) = −1050 kg m s⁻¹ (1.05 × 10³ kg m s⁻¹ backwards).

(b) F = Δp ÷ Δt = 1050 ÷ 0.15 = 7.0 × 10³ N

(c) The driver's change in momentum is the same either way: they go from 15 m s⁻¹ to rest. Impulse FΔt equals that change, so it is fixed too. The airbag makes the stopping time 15 times longer, so the average force is 15 times smaller: 7.0 × 10³ N instead of 1.05 × 10⁵ N. The airbag also spreads that force over a bigger area of the body.

Phase 5.1 · Work, energy & power foundations

FOUNDATIONS

Energy is never created or destroyed, only transformed. Work is how a force moves energy from one place or form to another.

5.1.1Work done by a force

W = Fd, in joules, where d is the distance moved in the direction of the force. If the force is at right angles to the motion it does no work. That is why the centripetal force in uniform circular motion does no work: the speed, and so the kinetic energy, never changes.

5.1.2Kinetic and gravitational potential energy
Kinetic

Ek = ½mv². Double the speed, four times the energy.

Gravitational potential

ΔEp = mgΔh. Δh is the change in vertical height, not the distance along a slope.

5.1.3Elastic potential energy

A stretched or compressed spring stores Ep = ½kx², the area under its force–extension graph. Spring launchers turn this into kinetic energy. See the springs lab in module 02.

5.1.4Conservation of energy

With no friction or air resistance, mechanical energy is conserved: Ek + Ep = constant. For something dropped or rolling down a frictionless track from rest, mgh = ½mv², so v = √(2gh). The mass cancels.

With friction, some energy is transformed into heat (and sound). The work done against friction equals the mechanical energy lost: Ffriction × d = Ebefore − Eafter.

5.1.5Power and efficiency

Power is the rate of doing work or transforming energy: P = W ÷ t, in watts (J s⁻¹). A motor that lifts the same load in half the time has twice the power.

Efficiency = useful energy out ÷ total energy in (× 100%). No real machine is 100% efficient.

QUESTION ONECargo crane · work and power

A crane lifts a 500 kg crate 12 m vertically at constant speed in 20 s.

(a) Calculate the work done on the crate. A

(b) Calculate the useful power output of the crane. A

(c) The motor draws 4.2 kW. Calculate the efficiency and explain where the rest of the energy goes. M

Show worked answer · Question One

(a) At constant speed the lifting force equals the weight. W = Fd = mgh = 500 × 9.8 × 12 = 5.9 × 10⁴ J (58 800 J).

(b) P = W ÷ t = 58 800 ÷ 20 = 2.9 × 10³ W (2940 W).

(c) Efficiency = 2940 ÷ 4200 = 0.70 = 70%. The other 30% is transformed into heat in the motor and cables, sound, and heat from friction in pulleys and gears.

QUESTION TWOLaunch track · conservation with friction

A 450 kg test car starts from rest at the top of a 32 m drop on a launch track.

(a) Ignoring friction, calculate its speed at the bottom. M

(b) Ignoring friction, calculate its speed as it passes over a 12 m high hump after the drop. M

(c) Its measured speed at the bottom is actually 22 m s⁻¹. The track from the top to the bottom is 80 m long. Calculate the energy transformed by friction and the average friction force. E

Show worked answer · Question Two

(a) mgh = ½mv², so v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 9.8 × 32) = 25 m s⁻¹.

(b) Over the hump the car is 32 − 12 = 20 m below the start: v = √(2 × 9.8 × 20) = 19.8 m s⁻¹.

(c) Ep at the top: 450 × 9.8 × 32 = 141 120 J. Ek at the bottom: ½ × 450 × 22² = 108 900 J. Energy transformed to heat and sound = 3.2 × 10⁴ J. That equals the work done against friction: F = W ÷ d = 32 220 ÷ 80 = 400 N (403 N) on average.

QUESTION THREESpring launcher · elastic energy

A toy launcher has a spring with k = 400 N m⁻¹, compressed by 5.0 cm. It fires a 20 g ball straight up. Ignore air resistance and the small height gained while the spring extends.

(a) Calculate the elastic potential energy stored. A

(b) Calculate the ball's launch speed. M

(c) Calculate the maximum height and explain how compressing the spring twice as far would change it. E

Show worked answer · Question Three

(a) Convert first: x = 0.050 m, m = 0.020 kg. Ep = ½kx² = ½ × 400 × 0.050² = 0.50 J

(b) ½mv² = 0.50, so v = √(2 × 0.50 ÷ 0.020) = 7.1 m s⁻¹.

(c) mgh = 0.50, so h = 0.50 ÷ (0.020 × 9.8) = 2.6 m. Doubling x quadruples the stored energy (it depends on x²), so the ball reaches four times the height, about 10 m, and leaves at twice the speed.

QUESTION FOURHill climb · power output

A cyclist and bike with a total mass of 85 kg ride at constant speed up a hill that rises 40 m vertically. The climb takes 2.0 minutes.

(a) Calculate the gain in gravitational potential energy. A

(b) Calculate the minimum average power output. M

(c) Explain why the cyclist's actual power output must be greater than this. M

Show worked answer · Question Four

(a) ΔEp = mgΔh = 85 × 9.8 × 40 = 3.3 × 10⁴ J (33 320 J).

(b) Convert time: 2.0 min = 120 s. P = 33 320 ÷ 120 = 280 W (278 W).

(c) At constant speed, the cyclist must also do work against air resistance and rolling friction all the way up. That energy becomes heat and sound rather than potential energy, so the total power output is the 280 W gained as Ep plus the power used against resistance. (The body's own efficiency means even more chemical energy is used.)
