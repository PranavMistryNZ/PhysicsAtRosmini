01

Electric fields

Textbook ch. 17
  • An electric field is a region where a charge feels a force. Field lines point the way a positive charge would be pushed: from + to −.
  • Between two parallel plates the field is uniform: evenly spaced, parallel lines, the same strength everywhere.
  • A charge in the field feels \(F = Eq\). A negative charge (an electron) is pushed the opposite way to the field.
  • Moving a charge through the field changes its electric potential energy by \(\Delta E_{p} = Eqd\). Released from rest, it turns that into kinetic energy.
\(E = \frac{V}{d}\)\(F = Eq\)\(\Delta E_{p} = Eqd\)\(E_{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^{2}\)

Watch out: d is the distance between the plates in metres. A separation of 4.0 mm is \(4.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m}\).

02

DC electricity

Textbook ch. 18
  • Current is the rate of flow of charge. Conventional current flows from + to − round the circuit; electrons actually flow the other way.
  • Voltage is the energy given to (or used by) each coulomb of charge.
  • Series: the same current everywhere, the voltages add up, resistances add. Parallel: the same voltage across each branch, the currents add up, and the total resistance is less than the smallest branch.
  • Power is the rate of energy transfer: \(P = IV\). A brighter lamp has a bigger power.
\(I = \frac{q}{t}\)\(V = \frac{\Delta E}{q}\)\(V = IR\)\(P = IV\)\(P = \frac{\Delta E}{t}\)\(R_{T} = R_{1} + R_{2} + \ldots\)\(\frac{1}{R_{T}} = \frac{1}{R_{1}} + \frac{1}{R_{2}} + \ldots\)

Watch out: after adding the reciprocals for a parallel combination, flip the answer: \(\frac{1}{R_{T}}\) is not \(R_{T}\).

03

Magnetism

Textbook ch. 19
  • Magnetic field lines go from N to S outside a magnet. Field strength B is measured in tesla (T).
  • A current makes a magnetic field: circles round a straight wire, and a bar-magnet shape round a coil (solenoid). Use the right-hand grip rule for the direction.
  • A current-carrying wire across a field feels a force \(F = BIL\). A moving charge feels \(F = Bqv\). Use the right-hand slap rule for the direction.
  • The force is at right angles to the motion, so a charge moving across a uniform field follows a circle at constant speed.
\(F = BIL\)\(F = Bqv\)

Watch out: the force is zero when the wire or the charge moves parallel to the field. For an electron, find the direction for a positive charge and reverse it.

04

Electromagnetic induction

Textbook ch. 20
  • A conductor moving across a magnetic field has a voltage induced across its ends: \(V = BvL\).
  • The magnetic force on the charges inside the moving rod (\(F = Bqv\)) pushes them along it. That is where the voltage comes from.
  • If the circuit is complete, a current flows. The current in the field makes a force on the rod that opposes its motion (Lenz's law).
  • So you must keep pushing to keep the rod moving: the work you do becomes electrical energy. Generators work this way.
\(V = BvL\)\(I = \frac{V}{R}\)\(F = BIL\)\(P = IV\)

Watch out: the induced current always opposes the change that made it. If it helped, you'd get energy for nothing.

The big idea that links everything: charges, fields and energy

Every part of this standard is about forces on charges and the energy they carry. An electric field pushes on a charge (\(F = Eq\)) and gives it energy (\(\Delta E_{p} = Eqd\)). In a circuit, the battery does the same: each coulomb gains \(V = \frac{\Delta E}{q}\) joules and spends them in the resistors. A magnetic field pushes on moving charges (\(F = Bqv\)), which gives the force on a current (\(F = BIL\)) and, when the conductor itself moves, the induced voltage (\(V = BvL\)).

Phase 0.3 · Exam skills: answers that earn marks

EXAM SKILLS

Knowing the physics is half the job. The other half is showing it the way the marking schedule rewards. The grade depends on how deep your answer goes.

ACHIEVEMENT

Describe, identify, calculate (one step). Draw field lines, state a direction, or get a one-step answer such as \(V = IR\) with the right unit.

"The electron is pushed towards the positive plate, opposite to the field."

MERIT

Explain, multi-step calculate. Give the physics reason. Calculations where you need to find something first, such as the total resistance before the current.

"Adding a branch in parallel lowers the total resistance, so the total current from the supply increases."

EXCELLENCE

Connect concepts. Link two or more ideas into one complete argument, often with energy, and use it to reach a conclusion.

"The induced current makes a force \(F = BIL\) that opposes the rod's motion, so work must be done to keep it moving. That work is the electrical energy delivered to the resistor: \(Fv = VI\)."

A structure for "explain" questions

Name itName the physics: the field, the force, the rule, conservation of energy or charge.
Apply itSay what it means here: which way the force acts, what stays the same (V in parallel, I in series).
Link itConnect cause to effect with "so", "therefore" or "because".
ConcludeAnswer the exact question: brighter or dimmer, which direction, bigger or smaller.

Lay out every calculation the same way

Equation

Write the equation you are using first, in symbols.

Substitute

Put the numbers in with SI units. Show conversions (mm → m, mA → A, kV → V).

Answer

Give the answer with a unit, a sensible number of significant figures, and a direction for forces and fields.

A wrong final answer can still gain credit if the marker can follow a correct method. A bare number cannot.

Do this · avoid this

Do this

  • Draw field lines from + to −, evenly spaced and parallel between plates, with arrows.
  • Find the direction of a magnetic force for a positive charge first, then reverse it for an electron.
  • In combination circuits, work out the parallel part first, then add the series part.
  • Say what is the same: current in series, voltage in parallel.
  • In induction questions, say where the energy comes from (the work done pushing).

Avoid this

  • Saying current "gets used up" in a lamp. Charge is conserved; it's the energy that is transferred.
  • Using the plate separation in mm or cm in \(E = \frac{V}{d}\).
  • Saying a magnetic force speeds up a charge. It only changes the direction.
  • Forgetting that the induced current makes its own force on the rod.
  • Writing "voltage flows through" a resistor. Current flows; voltage is across.

Direction rules

Right-hand grip rule

Thumb along the current; your fingers curl the way the magnetic field circles the wire. For a coil: fingers along the current, thumb points to the N end.

Right-hand slap rule

Fingers along the field B, thumb along the current (or the velocity of a positive charge). Your palm pushes the way the force acts.

Dots and crosses

⊙ means pointing out of the page (the tip of an arrow coming at you). ⊗ means into the page (the tail feathers).

Phase 1.1 · Electric field foundations

FOUNDATIONS

Charges push and pull on each other without touching. We describe that with an electric field: a map of the force a charge would feel at each point.

Charge

Charge q is measured in coulombs (C). Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. An electron carries \(-1.60 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\) and a proton \(+1.60 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\). Objects become charged by gaining electrons (negative) or losing them (positive). Charge is conserved: it is never made or destroyed, only moved.

Electric fields and field lines

An electric field is a region where a charge feels a force. Field lines show it:

  • They point the way a positive test charge would be pushed: out of + charges, into − charges.
  • Closer lines mean a stronger field. Lines never cross.
  • Round a single point charge they spread out radially, so the field gets weaker further away.
  • Between two oppositely charged parallel plates they are straight, parallel and evenly spaced: a uniform field, the same strength everywhere except near the edges.
Field strength between plates: \(E = \frac{V}{d}\)

For two parallel plates with a voltage V across them, d metres apart:

\(E = \frac{V}{d}\)

E is in volts per metre (V m⁻¹), which is the same as newtons per coulomb (N C⁻¹). Example. 240 V across plates 6.0 mm apart: \(E = \frac{240}{6.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 4.0 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\). Bigger voltage or closer plates mean a stronger field.

Force on a charge: \(F = Eq\)

\(F = Eq\)

A positive charge is pushed along the field lines; a negative charge is pushed against them, towards the positive plate. In a uniform field the force is the same everywhere, so the charge has a constant acceleration \(a = \frac{F}{m}\), just like an object falling in gravity.

Example. An electron in the field above: \(F = 4.0 \times 10^{4} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 6.4 \times 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{N}\), towards the positive plate. Its weight (\(9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 9.8 \approx 9 \times 10^{-30}\,\mathrm{N}\)) is far too small to matter.

Electric potential energy: \(\Delta E_{p} = Eqd\)

Moving a charge through a field changes its electric potential energy, just as lifting a mass changes its gravitational potential energy. Work done = force × distance along the field:

\(\Delta E_{p} = Eqd\)

Here d is the distance moved along the field lines. Moving at right angles to the field lines needs no work. Across the whole gap, \(Eqd = \frac{V}{d}qd = qV\).

A charge released from rest gains kinetic energy equal to the potential energy it loses: \(Eqd = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^{2}\). Example. An electron accelerated across 240 V gains \(1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 240 = 3.84 \times 10^{-17}\,\mathrm{J}\), so \(v = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 3.84 \times 10^{-17}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}}} = 9.2 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

Phase 1.4 · Exam-style problems: electric fields

EXAM PRACTICE

Three original practice questions in the style of NCEA Level 2 AS91173 papers. Charge on an electron = \(-1.60 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\); mass of an electron = \(9.11 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\).

QUESTION ONEElectron gun · accelerating an electron

Two parallel plates 4.0 mm apart are connected to a 12 V supply. An electron starts from rest at the negative plate.

(a) Calculate the electric field strength between the plates. A

(b) Calculate the force on the electron and state its direction. A

(c) Calculate the speed of the electron when it reaches the positive plate. M

(d) The plates are moved to 8.0 mm apart with the same 12 V. Explain what happens to the force on the electron and to its final speed. E

Show worked answer · Question One

(a) \(E = \frac{V}{d} = \frac{12}{4.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 3.0 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\)

(b) \(F = Eq = 3.0 \times 10^{3} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 4.8 \times 10^{-16}\,\mathrm{N}\), towards the positive plate (opposite to the field, because the electron is negative).

(c) Energy gained \(= Eqd = 3.0 \times 10^{3} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 4.0 \times 10^{-3} = 1.92 \times 10^{-18}\,\mathrm{J}\). This becomes kinetic energy: \(v = \sqrt{\frac{2E_{k}}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 1.92 \times 10^{-18}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}}} = 2.1 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

(d) Doubling d with the same V halves the field (\(E = \frac{V}{d} = 1.5 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\)), so the force \(F = Eq\) halves too. But the electron is now pushed over twice the distance, so the work done, \(Eqd\), is unchanged (it equals \(qV\), and V is still 12 V). It gains the same kinetic energy, so it reaches the same final speed, \(2.1 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\); it just takes longer to get there.

QUESTION TWOFloating dust · balanced forces

A charged dust particle of mass 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ kg carries three extra electrons. It floats at rest between horizontal plates 5.0 mm apart.

(a) Calculate the charge on the particle. A

(b) State which plate must be positive, and explain why. M

(c) Calculate the voltage across the plates. E

Show worked answer · Question Two

(a) \(q = 3 \times (-1.60 \times 10^{-19}) = -4.8 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\)

(b) The particle is at rest, so the forces balance: the electric force must point up to balance its weight. A negative charge is pushed towards the positive plate, so the top plate is positive (the field points down).

(c) Balanced forces: \(Eq = mg\), so \(E = \frac{mg}{q} = \frac{3.2 \times 10^{-15} \times 9.8}{4.8 \times 10^{-19}} = 6.5 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\). Then \(V = Ed = 6.53 \times 10^{4} \times 5.0 \times 10^{-3} = 330\,\mathrm{V}\) (327 V).

QUESTION THREEMoving a proton · work in a field

Between two plates the uniform field is 5.0 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹, pointing straight down.

(a) Draw the field lines between the plates, and label which plate is positive. A

(b) A proton is moved 2.0 cm straight up, against the field. Calculate the change in its electric potential energy. M

(c) The proton is then moved 2.0 cm sideways, parallel to the plates. Explain why its electric potential energy does not change. E

Show worked answer · Question Three

(a) Straight, parallel, evenly spaced lines from the top plate (+) to the bottom plate (−), with arrows pointing down. The lines bulge outwards slightly at the edges.

(b) \(\Delta E_{p} = Eqd = 5.0 \times 10^{4} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 0.020 = 1.6 \times 10^{-16}\,\mathrm{J}\), an increase: the field pushes the proton down, so work has to be done to move it up.

(c) The force on the proton (\(F = Eq\)) acts straight down, along the field lines. Moving sideways, the displacement is at right angles to the force, so no work is done by or against the field. In \(\Delta E_{p} = Eqd\), d is the distance moved along the field, which is zero here, so \(\Delta E_{p} = 0\).

Phase 2.1 · DC electricity foundations

FOUNDATIONS

A circuit is an energy delivery system. Charge goes round and round and is never used up. The supply gives each coulomb energy, and the components take it away.

Current: \(I = \frac{q}{t}\)

Current is the rate of flow of charge, in amperes (A): one coulomb per second. Conventional current flows from the + terminal round to the −; in metal wires it is really electrons drifting the other way.

Example. 0.50 A for 2.0 minutes: \(q = It = 0.50 \times 120 = 60\,\mathrm{C}\). That is \(\frac{60}{1.60 \times 10^{-19}} = 3.8 \times 10^{20}\) electrons.

Current is measured with an ammeter in series: the current has to flow through it.

Voltage: \(V = \frac{\Delta E}{q}\)

Voltage (potential difference) is the energy transferred per coulomb, in volts (J C⁻¹). A 9.0 V battery gives each coulomb 9.0 J; across a lamp, the voltage is the energy each coulomb gives to the lamp. It is measured with a voltmeter in parallel (across the component).

Example. 60 C passes through a 12 V lamp: \(\Delta E = qV = 60 \times 12 = 720\,\mathrm{J}\).

Resistance and Ohm's law

\(V = IR\)

Resistance (Ω) is how hard it is to push current through something. A resistor at constant temperature is ohmic: I is proportional to V, so its V–I graph is a straight line through the origin and R is constant.

A filament lamp is non-ohmic: as the current increases, the filament heats up and its resistance increases, so the graph curves (the current rises more slowly). Calculate R at any point with \(R = \frac{V}{I}\), using the values at that point, not the gradient.

Series circuits
Current

The same everywhere: there is only one path.

Voltage

The supply voltage is shared: \(V = V_{1} + V_{2} + \ldots\), and the bigger resistor gets the bigger share.

Resistance

\(R_{T} = R_{1} + R_{2} + \ldots\). Adding a resistor in series increases the total and lowers the current.

Parallel circuits
Voltage

The same across every branch (each branch connects to the same two points).

Current

The supply current splits: \(I = I_{1} + I_{2} + \ldots\). The smaller resistance takes the bigger current.

Resistance

\(\frac{1}{R_{T}} = \frac{1}{R_{1}} + \frac{1}{R_{2}} + \ldots\). Adding a branch gives the current another path, so the total resistance goes down and the supply current goes up.

Example. 6.0 Ω and 3.0 Ω in parallel: \(\frac{1}{R_{T}} = \frac{1}{6.0} + \frac{1}{3.0} = 0.50\), so \(R_{T} = 2.0\,\Omega\), less than either branch. Houses are wired in parallel so every appliance gets the full voltage and can be switched separately.

Power and energy

\(P = IV \qquad P = \frac{\Delta E}{t}\)

Substituting \(V = IR\) gives \(P = I^{2}R\) and \(P = \frac{V^{2}}{R}\). The brightness of a lamp depends on its power. Example. A 2.0 kW heater on 230 V draws \(I = \frac{2000}{230} = 8.7\,\mathrm{A}\) and uses \(\Delta E = Pt = 2000 \times 3600 = 7.2 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{J}\) in an hour.

Phase 2.5 · Exam-style problems: DC electricity

EXAM PRACTICE

QUESTION ONEPhone charging · charge and energy

A phone charger delivers a current of 2.0 A at 5.0 V for 45 minutes.

(a) Calculate the charge that flows. A

(b) Calculate the number of electrons this represents. M

(c) Calculate the energy delivered and the power of the charger. M

Show worked answer · Question One

(a) 45 min = 2700 s. \(q = It = 2.0 \times 2700 = 5400\,\mathrm{C}\)

(b) \(n = \frac{5400}{1.60 \times 10^{-19}} = 3.4 \times 10^{22}\) electrons.

(c) \(\Delta E = qV = 5400 \times 5.0 = 2.7 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{J}\). \(P = IV = 2.0 \times 5.0 = 10\,\mathrm{W}\) (check: \(\frac{27\,000}{2700} = 10\,\mathrm{W}\)).

QUESTION TWOLamp circuit · combination

A 12 V supply is connected to resistor \(R_{1} = 4.0\,\Omega\) in series with two lamps in parallel, \(R_{2} = 6.0\,\Omega\) and \(R_{3} = 12\,\Omega\). Treat the lamps as having constant resistance.

(a) Calculate the total resistance. M

(b) Calculate the current through each lamp. M

(c) Lamp \(R_{3}\) blows. Explain, with calculations, what happens to the brightness of lamp \(R_{2}\). E

Show worked answer · Question Two

(a) \(\frac{1}{R_{\parallel}} = \frac{1}{6.0} + \frac{1}{12} = 0.25\), so \(R_{\parallel} = 4.0\,\Omega\). \(R_{T} = 4.0 + 4.0 = 8.0\,\Omega\)

(b) \(I = \frac{12}{8.0} = 1.5\,\mathrm{A}\). \(V_{1} = 1.5 \times 4.0 = 6.0\,\mathrm{V}\), so the lamps have 6.0 V across them. \(I_{2} = \frac{6.0}{6.0} = 1.0\,\mathrm{A}\), \(I_{3} = \frac{6.0}{12} = 0.50\,\mathrm{A}\) (they add to 1.5 A ✓).

(c) With \(R_{3}\) gone, \(R_{1}\) and \(R_{2}\) are in series: \(R_{T} = 10\,\Omega\), \(I = 1.2\,\mathrm{A}\). Now \(V_{1} = 4.8\,\mathrm{V}\) and \(V_{2} = 7.2\,\mathrm{V}\). The total resistance went up, so the supply current fell, so \(R_{1}\) takes less of the voltage and lamp \(R_{2}\) gets more (7.2 V instead of 6.0 V). Its power rises from \(1.0 \times 6.0 = 6.0\,\mathrm{W}\) to \(1.2 \times 7.2 = 8.6\,\mathrm{W}\), so lamp \(R_{2}\) gets brighter.

(b) Describe the shape of the lamp's V–I graph and explain why it is not a straight line. M

(c) Explain why the lamp is most likely to blow just as it is switched on. E

Show worked answer · Question Three

(a) \(R = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{2.0}{0.20} = 10\,\Omega\) and \(\frac{6.0}{0.30} = 20\,\Omega\).

(b) With I on the y-axis against V, the graph starts as a straight-ish line through the origin and then curves over, becoming less steep. A bigger current heats the filament more; at a higher temperature the metal's atoms vibrate more and get in the way of the electrons more, so the resistance increases. The current doesn't rise in proportion to the voltage, so the lamp is non-ohmic.

(c) When the lamp is first switched on the filament is cold, so its resistance is low. The current is therefore much bigger than normal for an instant, and the power \(P = \frac{V^{2}}{R}\) delivered to the filament is much bigger, heating it very quickly. That surge stresses any thin, weak spot in the filament, which can melt and break.

Phase 3.1 · Magnetism foundations

FOUNDATIONS

Magnetism and electricity are two sides of the same thing: moving charges make magnetic fields, and magnetic fields push on moving charges.

Magnetic fields

A magnetic field is a region where a magnet, a moving charge or a current feels a force. Field lines go from the N pole to the S pole outside a magnet; closer lines mean a stronger field. Field strength B is measured in tesla (T). Earth's field is about \(5 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{T}\); a strong school magnet about 0.1 T. Between the flat poles of a horseshoe magnet the field is roughly uniform.

The field around a current
Straight wire

Concentric circles round the wire, weaker further away. Right-hand grip rule: thumb along the current, fingers curl the way the field goes.

Coil (solenoid)

Like a bar magnet: nearly uniform inside, N at one end. Grip the coil with your fingers along the current: your thumb points to the N end. More turns or more current make a stronger electromagnet.

Two parallel wires with currents in the same direction attract; opposite directions repel. Each wire sits in the other's field and feels a force \(F = BIL\).

Force on a current: \(F = BIL\)

A wire carrying current I across a magnetic field B, with length L inside the field and at right angles to it, feels

\(F = BIL\)

The force is at right angles to both the wire and the field. If the wire is parallel to the field there is no force. Right-hand slap rule: fingers along B, thumb along I: the force comes out of your palm.

Example. 0.20 m of wire carrying 5.0 A at right angles to a 0.30 T field: \(F = 0.30 \times 5.0 \times 0.20 = 0.30\,\mathrm{N}\). This is the force that turns an electric motor.

Force on a moving charge: \(F = Bqv\)

A current is just moving charges, so a single charge moving at right angles to a field feels

\(F = Bqv\)

Use the slap rule with your thumb along the velocity of a positive charge. For an electron, the force is the opposite way.

The force is always at right angles to the velocity, so it changes the charge's direction but not its speed. The magnetic force does no work, and the kinetic energy stays the same. In a uniform field, a constant-size force always at 90° to the motion is exactly what circular motion needs: the charge moves in a circle, with \(F = Bqv\) as the centripetal force.

QUESTION ONECurrent balance · F = BIL

A horizontal wire 0.25 m long lies at right angles to a horizontal magnetic field of 0.40 T that points into the page. The wire carries 3.0 A to the right.

(a) Calculate the magnetic force on the wire. A

(b) State the direction of the force. A

(c) The wire has a mass of 20 g. Calculate the current needed for the magnetic force to support its weight exactly, and state which way it must flow. E

Show worked answer · Question One

(a) \(F = BIL = 0.40 \times 3.0 \times 0.25 = 0.30\,\mathrm{N}\)

(b) Fingers into the page (B), thumb to the right (I): the palm faces up. The force is up the page.

(c) Balanced forces: \(BIL = mg\), so \(I = \frac{mg}{BL} = \frac{0.020 \times 9.8}{0.40 \times 0.25} = 2.0\,\mathrm{A}\) (1.96 A). The magnetic force must point up, so the current must flow to the right, as in (b).

QUESTION TWOElectron beam · F = Bqv

An electron moves at 2.0 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹ to the right into a uniform magnetic field of 5.0 mT pointing out of the page.

(a) Calculate the magnetic force on the electron. A

(b) State the direction of the force as the electron enters the field. M

(c) Explain why the electron moves in a circle at constant speed. E

Show worked answer · Question Two

(a) \(F = Bqv = 5.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.0 \times 10^{6} = 1.6 \times 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{N}\)

(b) For a positive charge: fingers out of the page, thumb to the right, palm faces down. The electron is negative, so the force is the opposite way: up the page.

(c) The magnetic force is always at right angles to the electron's velocity. A force at 90° to the motion does no work, so the electron's kinetic energy and speed stay constant; it only changes the direction. As the direction changes, the force turns with it, always pointing sideways to the motion. The field and speed are constant, so the force \(Bqv\) has a constant size. A force of constant size that always acts at right angles to the velocity, towards one point, is a centripetal force, so the electron moves in a circle.

QUESTION THREEPower cables · fields round wires

Two long parallel cables carry large currents in the same direction.

(a) Describe the shape of the magnetic field around one cable, and how you find its direction. A

(b) Explain why the two cables are pulled towards each other. E

Show worked answer · Question Three

(a) Concentric circles centred on the cable, further apart (weaker) further from it. Right-hand grip rule: thumb along the current, the fingers curl in the direction of the field.

(b) Cable A is in the magnetic field made by cable B. At cable A, B's field points at right angles to cable A (it is part of a circle round B). Cable A carries a current across that field, so it feels a force \(F = BIL\). Using the grip rule for B's field and then the slap rule for A's current, the force on A points towards B. By the same argument (or Newton's third law), cable B is pulled towards A. If one current were reversed, the forces would reverse and the cables would repel.

Phase 4.1 · Electromagnetic induction foundations

FOUNDATIONS

Move a wire across a magnetic field and you get a voltage. This is how almost all of New Zealand's electricity is made, in the generators at hydro, geothermal and wind stations.

Where the voltage comes from

A metal rod contains free electrons. Moving the rod across a magnetic field moves those electrons across the field too, so each feels a force \(F = Bqv\), along the length of the rod. Electrons pile up at one end, leaving the other end positive: there is now a voltage across the rod.

\(V = BvL\)

L is the length of rod in the field, v its speed at right angles to the field. Faster, a stronger field or a longer rod gives a bigger voltage. A rod moving parallel to the field (not cutting field lines) has no voltage induced.

Example. A 0.50 m rod moving at 4.0 m s⁻¹ across a 0.20 T field: \(V = 0.20 \times 4.0 \times 0.50 = 0.40\,\mathrm{V}\).

Induced current

If the rod is part of a complete circuit (for example, sliding along metal rails joined by a resistor), the induced voltage drives a current \(I = \frac{V}{R}\). The moving rod acts like a battery. Inside the rod, conventional current flows the way the magnetic force pushes positive charges (slap rule with your thumb along the velocity).

Lenz's law: the induced current opposes the change

The induced current flows through the rod, which is in the magnetic field, so the rod now feels a force \(F = BIL\). That force always opposes the motion that caused it. This is Lenz's law.

It has to be this way because energy is conserved. If the force helped the motion, the rod would speed up and make more current, which would speed it up more: energy from nothing. Instead, you must keep pushing to keep the rod moving at constant speed, and the work you do becomes electrical energy:

\(P_{\text{mechanical}} = Fv = BILv = (BvL)I = VI = P_{\text{electrical}}\)

The same idea explains magnetic braking: a magnet falling through a copper pipe induces currents that oppose its fall, so it drifts down slowly.

Magnets and coils, generators

Pushing a magnet into a coil connected to a meter gives a reading while the magnet moves. Faster movement, a stronger magnet or more turns give a bigger voltage. Holding the magnet still gives nothing; pulling it out gives a reading in the opposite direction. The induced current makes the coil an electromagnet that repels the approaching magnet (or attracts the leaving one): Lenz's law again.

A generator spins a coil in a magnetic field. Each side of the coil cuts the field lines, first one way and then the other, so the induced voltage reverses every half turn: alternating current (AC). The turbine does work against the opposing force, and that work becomes electrical energy.

EXAM PRACTICE

QUESTION ONESliding rod · voltage, current, force

A metal rod 0.50 m long slides at a constant 3.0 m s⁻¹ along rails in a 0.20 T field pointing into the page. The rails are joined by a 2.0 Ω resistor. The rod moves to the right.

(a) Calculate the voltage induced across the rod. A

(b) Calculate the current and state its direction in the rod. M

(c) Calculate the force needed to keep the rod moving at constant speed, and show that the power supplied by this force equals the electrical power in the resistor. E

Show worked answer · Question One

(a) \(V = BvL = 0.20 \times 3.0 \times 0.50 = 0.30\,\mathrm{V}\)

(b) \(I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{0.30}{2.0} = 0.15\,\mathrm{A}\). Slap rule for positive charges in the rod: fingers into the page, thumb to the right (their velocity), palm up. So the current flows up the rod (and round through the resistor).

(c) The current in the rod feels \(F = BIL = 0.20 \times 0.15 \times 0.50 = 0.015\,\mathrm{N}\). Slap rule (fingers in, thumb up): palm faces left, opposing the motion (Lenz's law). At constant speed the net force is zero, so the push must be 0.015 N to the right. Mechanical power: \(P = Fv = 0.015 \times 3.0 = 0.045\,\mathrm{W}\). Electrical power: \(P = VI = 0.30 \times 0.15 = 0.045\,\mathrm{W}\). They are equal: all the work done pushing the rod becomes electrical energy, which the resistor turns into heat.

QUESTION TWOAircraft wing · induced voltage

An aircraft with a wingspan of 30 m flies horizontally at 250 m s⁻¹. The vertical part of Earth's magnetic field is 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ T.

(a) Calculate the voltage induced between the wing tips. A

(b) Explain how this voltage arises. M

(c) A passenger suggests using this voltage to power a lamp, by running a wire from one wing tip to the other through the cabin. Explain why no current would flow. E

Show worked answer · Question Two

(a) \(V = BvL = 5.0 \times 10^{-5} \times 250 \times 30 = 0.38\,\mathrm{V}\) (0.375 V).

(b) The metal wing is a conductor full of free electrons. As the plane flies, the electrons move with it across the vertical field lines, so each feels a magnetic force \(F = Bqv\) along the wing. Electrons gather at one tip, making it negative and leaving the other positive, until the voltage between the tips is BvL.

(c) The connecting wire moves with the plane at the same speed through the same field, so exactly the same voltage is induced along it, pushing its electrons towards the same side. The two voltages oppose each other round the loop and cancel, so there is no net voltage to drive a current. (A current needs the loop to cut field lines differently, which happens when part of a circuit moves relative to the rest.)

QUESTION THREEMagnet and coil · Lenz's law

The N pole of a bar magnet is pushed into a coil connected to a sensitive meter.

(a) Describe what the meter shows while the magnet moves in, while it is held still inside, and while it is pulled out. A

(b) State two ways to get a bigger reading. A

(c) Use Lenz's law and conservation of energy to explain why it is harder to push the magnet in when the coil is connected than when the coil is disconnected. E

Show worked answer · Question Three

(a) Moving in: the needle deflects one way. Held still: zero (no change, no induced voltage). Pulled out: it deflects the opposite way.

(b) Any two of: move the magnet faster; use a stronger magnet; use a coil with more turns.

(c) When the coil is connected, the induced current turns it into an electromagnet. By Lenz's law the current flows so that it opposes the change that causes it: the end of the coil facing the magnet becomes a N pole, which repels the approaching N pole. So you have to push harder, and the extra work you do is the electrical energy delivered to the circuit. With the coil disconnected a voltage is still induced, but no current flows, so there is no opposing magnetic force and no energy transferred, and the magnet goes in easily.
