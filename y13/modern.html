The big ideas

Modern physics starts where classical physics breaks down. Energy comes in packets: light arrives as photons of energy \(hf\), and electrons in atoms can only have certain energies. Mass is a form of energy: when a nucleus becomes more tightly bound, a little mass disappears and an enormous amount of energy appears, \(\Delta E = \Delta mc^{2}\). Both ideas are tested with careful unit conversions: eV ↔ J, u ↔ kg ↔ MeV, nm ↔ m.

Three chains cover most explanations:

Light is photons, \(E = hf\)→one photon gives all its energy to one electron→escapes only if \(hf \ge \phi\)→leftover energy is \(E_{k(\text{max})} = hf - \phi\)

CEO worked example · photoelectric effect

Question: Ultraviolet light of wavelength 250 nm falls on zinc, which has a work function of 4.3 eV. Calculate the maximum kinetic energy of the ejected electrons, in joules, and the stopping voltage.

C · Conceptualise: one photon gives its energy to one electron; \(\phi\) of it is needed to escape and the rest is kinetic energy: \(E_{k(\text{max})} = hf - \phi\). Knowns: \(\lambda = 250 \times 10^{-9}\) m, \(\phi = 4.3 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 6.88 \times 10^{-19}\) J.

E · Execute: \(E = hc/\lambda = 6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}/250 \times 10^{-9} = 7.96 \times 10^{-19}\) J. \(E_{k(\text{max})} = 7.96 \times 10^{-19} - 6.88 \times 10^{-19}\) = 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. \(V_{s} = E_{k}/e = 1.08 \times 10^{-19}/1.60 \times 10^{-19}\) = 0.68 V.

O · Outcomes: J and V ✓. The photon energy (4.97 eV) is just above the work function, so a small \(E_{k}\) is sensible ✓. Check in eV: 4.97 − 4.3 = 0.67 eV ✓.

CEO worked example · mass defect

Question: Calculate the energy released, in MeV and in joules, when a deuterium nucleus (2.014102 u) fuses with a tritium nucleus (3.016049 u) to form helium-4 (4.002603 u) and a neutron (1.008665 u). (Atomic masses.)

O · Outcomes: about 20 MeV, typical for fusion ✓. Keep all six decimal places in the masses: the difference is in the third decimal place, so rounding the masses early destroys the answer.

Common traps

Intensity vs frequencyBrighter light means more photons per second: more electrons, more current. It does not change the energy of each photon or the maximum kinetic energy.
eV and J1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Work functions and levels are often given in eV; \(h\) is in J s. Convert before you substitute.
"Maximum" kinetic energyElectrons from deeper in the metal lose energy on the way out. \(hf - \phi\) is the maximum kinetic energy.
S and L in Rydberg\(S\) is the lower (final for emission) level and \(L\) the higher. Swapping them gives a negative wavelength.
Negative energiesLevel energies are negative; the photon energy is the difference, always positive. Ionisation from the ground state needs 13.6 eV.
Binding energy is not "stored"Binding energy is the energy you must supply to pull a nucleus apart. More binding energy per nucleon = more stable, not more energy to release.
Rounding masses earlyMass defects are tiny differences between big numbers. Keep every decimal place until the end.
Balancing equationsCheck both the top numbers (nucleons) and the bottom numbers (charge), and count the neutrons released in fission.
Fission vs fusionFission splits heavy nuclei (right of iron); fusion joins light nuclei (left of iron). Both move towards iron.

Key terms · every definition for AS91525

52 terms in three groups. Use these as model definitions, then practise writing each one in your own words. Type in the box to find a term quickly.

The photoelectric effect

Quantum

The smallest possible packet of energy that can be emitted or absorbed. Energy comes in whole numbers of quanta.

Photon

A quantum of light or other electromagnetic radiation. It has no mass, travels at the speed of light and carries energy \(E = hf\).

Planck's constant (h)

The constant that links the energy of a photon to its frequency: \(6.63\times10^{-34}\ \text{J s}\).

Electronvolt (eV)

A unit of energy: the energy gained by one electron accelerated through a potential difference of one volt. \(1\ \text{eV} = 1.60\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}\).

Photoelectric effect

The emission of electrons from the surface of a material, usually a metal, when light of high enough frequency shines on it.

Photoelectron

An electron released from a surface by the photoelectric effect. It is an ordinary electron.

Work function (φ)

The minimum energy needed to remove an electron from the surface of a particular metal. Each metal has its own value.

Threshold frequency (f₀)

The minimum frequency of light that can release electrons from a particular metal: \(hf_0 = \phi\). Below it, no electrons are emitted, however bright the light.

Maximum kinetic energy

The kinetic energy of the fastest photoelectrons: the photon energy left over after the work function is paid, \(E_{k(\text{max})} = hf - \phi\).

Stopping voltage (Vs)

The opposing voltage that only just stops the fastest photoelectrons from reaching the collector, so the current falls to zero: \(eV_s = E_{k(\text{max})}\).

Intensity

The brightness of the light: the energy arriving on each square metre each second. For one frequency, a higher intensity means more photons per second, not more energy per photon.

Photocurrent

The current carried by photoelectrons crossing a photocell. It is proportional to the number of electrons released each second.

Saturation current

The largest photocurrent for a given light intensity, reached when every electron released is collected. Raising the voltage further does not increase it; raising the intensity does.

Photocell

An evacuated tube containing a metal emitter plate and a collector. Light on the emitter releases electrons, which cross to the collector and give a current.

Wave–particle duality

Light (and matter such as electrons) shows wave properties in some experiments and particle properties in others. Both models are needed.

Atoms and spectra

Line spectrum

A spectrum made of separate lines at particular wavelengths, not a continuous band of colour. Each element has its own pattern.

Emission spectrum

Bright lines on a dark background, produced when a hot or electrically excited gas emits light at its own particular wavelengths.

Absorption spectrum

Dark lines on a continuous spectrum, produced when white light passes through a cool gas, which absorbs its own particular wavelengths.

Bohr model

A model of the hydrogen atom in which the electron can only occupy certain allowed orbits, each with a fixed energy. It does not radiate while it stays in one of them.

Energy levels

The fixed energies an electron can have in an atom. For hydrogen, \(E_n = -13.6/n^{2}\ \text{eV}\).

Ground state

The lowest energy level of an atom (n = 1 for hydrogen), where the electron normally is.

Excited state

Any energy level above the ground state. An electron reaches one by absorbing exactly the right amount of energy.

Transition

A jump by an electron from one energy level to another. One photon is emitted (a fall) or absorbed (a rise), with \(hf = \Delta E\).

Ionisation

Removing an electron from an atom completely (to n = ∞). The ionisation energy of hydrogen from its ground state is 13.6 eV.

Rydberg formula

\(\dfrac{1}{\lambda} = R\left(\dfrac{1}{S^{2}} - \dfrac{1}{L^{2}}\right)\): gives the wavelength of every line in the hydrogen spectrum. \(R = 1.097\times10^{7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\).

Lyman series

The hydrogen lines from transitions that end on (or start from) n = 1. They are in the ultraviolet.

Balmer series

The hydrogen lines from transitions that end on n = 2. Four of them are visible (656, 486, 434 and 410 nm); the rest are near-ultraviolet.

Paschen series

The hydrogen lines from transitions that end on n = 3. They are in the infrared.

Brackett series

The hydrogen lines from transitions that end on n = 4. They are in the infrared.

Pfund series

The hydrogen lines from transitions that end on n = 5. They are in the infrared.

Series limit

The shortest wavelength in a spectral series, from a transition starting at n = ∞. The lines crowd together as they approach it.

Nuclear reactions

Nucleon

A particle in the nucleus: a proton or a neutron.

Atomic number (Z)

The number of protons in the nucleus. It decides which element the atom is.

Nucleon (mass) number (A)

The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.

Isotopes

Atoms of the same element (same Z) with different numbers of neutrons (different A). Some isotopes are stable and others are radioactive.

Strong nuclear force

The attractive force that holds nucleons together. It acts between all nucleons but only over a very short range, about 10⁻¹⁵ m.

Quark

A fundamental particle. Protons (up, up, down) and neutrons (up, down, down) are each made of three quarks.

Atomic mass unit (u)

One twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom: \(1.66\times10^{-27}\ \text{kg}\), equivalent to 931.5 MeV of energy.

Mass–energy equivalence

Mass and energy are two forms of the same thing: \(E = mc^{2}\). A loss of mass \(\Delta m\) releases energy \(\Delta mc^{2}\).

Mass defect

The difference between the total mass of the separate protons and neutrons and the (smaller) mass of the nucleus they form.

Binding energy

The energy needed to separate a nucleus completely into its nucleons. It is the energy equivalent of the mass defect: \(E = \Delta mc^{2}\).

Binding energy per nucleon

The binding energy divided by A. The larger it is, the more stable the nucleus. It peaks near iron-56.

Average mass per nucleon

The mass of a nucleus divided by A. It is lowest for the most stable nuclei, near iron.

Radiation

Energy sent out as particles or waves. Unstable nuclei emit alpha, beta or gamma radiation.

Alpha particle (α)

A helium nucleus, \(^{4}_{2}\text{He}\), emitted by a heavy unstable nucleus. A falls by 4 and Z falls by 2.

Beta particle (β⁻)

A fast electron emitted from the nucleus when a neutron changes into a proton. A is unchanged and Z rises by 1.

Positron (β⁺)

A particle with the mass of an electron and a charge of +1, emitted when a proton changes into a neutron. Z falls by 1.

Gamma ray (γ)

A high-energy photon emitted by a nucleus with excess energy. A and Z do not change.

Fission reaction

A heavy nucleus splits into two medium-sized nuclei, usually after absorbing a neutron, releasing energy and more neutrons.

Fusion reaction

Two light nuclei join to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy. It needs very high temperatures to overcome the repulsion between the nuclei.

Chain reaction

A fission reaction that keeps itself going, because the neutrons released by one fission cause further fissions.

Conservation laws

In every nuclear reaction the nucleon number, the charge, the momentum and the total mass–energy are the same before and after.

01 · The photoelectric effect

Energy comes in packets. Light arrives as photons, and one photon can free one electron from a metal.

Quanta, photons and the photoelectric effect

Six ideas. 1.1.1 and 1.1.2 introduce quantised energy; 1.1.3 to 1.1.6 cover the photoelectric effect. See what classical physics expected in 1.2, then test it in the lab in 1.3.

1.1.1 · Black-body radiation and the quantisation of energy

All hot bodies radiate. An ideal radiator is called a black body. Classical physics assumed the thermal energy of a hot body is shared among its atoms, making them vibrate and emit electromagnetic waves of every frequency.

  • At long wavelengths the classical prediction matched experiment.
  • At short wavelengths the predicted intensity rose towards infinity, which would break conservation of energy. This failure is called the ultraviolet catastrophe.

In 1900 Max Planck showed the measured spectrum could be explained if the oscillators that emit the radiation can only have discrete energies: zero, or a whole-number multiple of a fixed amount, a quantum, that depends on the frequency:

\[E=nhf\qquad h=6.63\times10^{-34}\ \text{J s}\]
  • At high frequency one quantum \(hf\) is large, so an oscillator needs a lot of energy just to reach its first vibration state.
  • Thermal energy is shared out randomly, so a high-frequency oscillator is very unlikely to receive that much. These oscillators are effectively "switched off".
  • The intensity at high frequencies therefore falls to zero, exactly as observed.
1.1.2 · Photons and the electronvolt

Light spreads out like a wave (it reflects, refracts, diffracts and interferes), but it delivers its energy in packets called photons. Each photon is one quantum of energy:

\[E=hf=\dfrac{hc}{\lambda}\]
  • Higher frequency (shorter wavelength) means more energy per photon: a UV photon carries more energy than a red one.
  • Brighter light of the same colour means more photons per second, not more energetic ones. A beam gets dimmer as it spreads out, but the energy of each photon does not change.

Photon and particle energies are tiny, so we use the electronvolt: the work done on an electron when it moves through a potential difference of one volt.

\[E=QV\qquad 1\ \text{eV}=1.60\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}\]
  • 1 J = 6.25 × 10¹⁸ eV.
  • Visible photons are about 1.8 eV (red) to 3.1 eV (violet).
1.1.3 · The photoelectric effect: discovery and observations

When some metals are illuminated by visible or ultraviolet light, electrons are emitted from the surface. This is the photoelectric effect.

  • 1887, Hertz: a spark passes more easily between two electrodes when they are lit with ultraviolet radiation.
  • 1888, Hallwachs: experiments with charged electroscopes showed that the illumination releases negatively charged particles.
  • After 1897, Lenard: once J. J. Thomson had discovered the electron, Lenard showed that the particles are electrons and measured their energies.

The experiments show:

  • No electrons are emitted below a threshold frequency \(f_{0}\), however bright the light. \(f_{0}\) depends on the metal.
  • Above \(f_{0}\), emission starts the instant the surface is lit, even with very dim light.
  • The electrons leave with a range of kinetic energies, from zero up to a maximum. The maximum kinetic energy increases with frequency and does not depend on intensity.
  • Above \(f_{0}\), the number of electrons emitted per second (the current) is proportional to the intensity.

1.1.5 · Einstein's photon model and the work function

In 1905 Einstein used Planck's idea: light is made of photons, each delivering one quantum \(E = hf\). When a photon meets an electron it is either reflected with no loss of energy, or it gives up all its energy to that one electron.

  • One photon frees at most one electron, so the number of electrons is proportional to the number of photons (the intensity).
  • An electron can leave as soon as a photon arrives, so emission is instant.

Some of the photon's energy is used to overcome the forces binding the electron to the metal. The minimum energy needed is the work function \(\phi\). At the threshold frequency the photon has only just enough:

\[hf_0=\phi\]

If \(f > f_{0}\), the energy left over becomes kinetic energy. This is Einstein's photoelectric equation:

\[E_{k(\text{max})}=hf-\phi\qquad \tfrac{1}{2}mv_{\text{max}}^{2}=hf-\phi=h(f-f_0)\]
  • It is a maximum because most electrons lose some energy in collisions with atoms on the way to the surface.
  • Threshold wavelength: \(\lambda_{0} = hc/\phi\).
1.1.6 · Stopping potential

In the experiment, light of known frequency falls on the metal plate. Emitted electrons cross to a collector, and a sensitive meter shows the current. An opposing voltage \(V\) is then applied, so the electrons are pulled back towards the plate they came from. The work done on each electron is \(W = QV = eV\), so only electrons with enough kinetic energy still reach the collector.

The stopping voltage \(V_{s}\) is the voltage that only just reduces the current to zero. It stops even the fastest electrons:

\[eV_s=\tfrac{1}{2}mv_{\text{max}}^{2}=hf-\phi\]

Measuring \(V_{s}\) for light of several frequencies gives a straight-line graph:

\[V_s=\dfrac{h}{e}f-\dfrac{\phi}{e}\]
  • Gradient = \(h/e\), the same for every metal.
  • \(x\)-intercept = threshold frequency \(f_{0}\); \(y\)-intercept = −\(\phi /e\).
  • Plotting \(E_{k(\text{max})}\) versus \(f\) instead gives gradient \(h\) and \(y\)-intercept −\(\phi\). Build this graph in 1.3.
1.1.7 · Wave–particle duality

Light cannot be described by one model alone. Some experiments can only be explained if light is a wave; others only if it arrives as particles (photons). Using whichever model fits the situation is called wave–particle duality.

EvidenceWhat is observedModel needed
Interference and diffraction (double slits, gratings)Bright and dark fringes where light from different paths overlapsWave: only waves can superpose and cancel
Photoelectric effectA threshold frequency, no time delay, and \(E_{k(\text{max})}\) that depends on frequency but not intensityParticle: each electron absorbs one photon of energy \(hf\)
Line spectraAtoms emit and absorb only certain frequenciesParticle: one photon per electron transition, \(hf = \Delta E\)
  • The two models are linked by \(E = hf\): the photon energy (a particle idea) is set by the frequency (a wave idea).
  • Light travels and spreads like a wave, but it transfers energy to matter in whole photons.
  • Duality is not just for light. Electrons, usually thought of as particles, also diffract and interfere like waves.

Say it like this: “The photoelectric effect shows light behaving as particles, because one photon gives all of its energy to one electron. Interference shows light behaving as a wave. Light has both wave and particle properties.”

Graph 1 · Maximum kinetic energy versus frequency

From \(E_{k(\text{max})} = hf - \phi\), which has the form \(y = mx + c\):

  • Gradient = \(h\), Planck's constant. It is the same for every metal.

Common misconceptions
  • "The line goes through the origin." It does not: nothing is emitted until \(f\) reaches \(f_{0}\).
  • "A different metal changes the gradient." The gradient is always \(h\); only the intercepts change.
  • "The dashed part is real data." Kinetic energy cannot be negative. The dashed line is only a construction to find \(\phi\).
  • "Brighter light moves the line up." Intensity does not appear in the equation, so the line does not move.
Graph 2 · Stopping voltage versus frequency

The stopping voltage measures the maximum kinetic energy, \(eV_{s} = E_{k(\text{max})}\), so \(V_{s} = \dfrac{h}{e}f - \dfrac{\phi}{e}\). It is Graph 1 with the vertical axis divided by \(e\).

  • Gradient = \(h/e\) = 4.14 × 10⁻¹⁵ V s. Multiply the gradient by \(e\) to get \(h\).
  • \(x\)-intercept = \(f_{0}\), exactly as in Graph 1.
  • \(y\)-intercept = −\(\phi /e\). Its size in volts equals the work function in eV.
Common misconceptions
  • "The gradient is \(h\)." On a voltage axis it is \(h/e\). Check the axis label before you quote a gradient.
  • "A stopping voltage of 1.5 V means 1.5 J." It means 1.5 eV = 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  • "The stopping voltage stops the photons." It stops the electrons. The light still reaches the metal and electrons are still freed; they are turned back before the collector.
Graph 3 · Current versus voltage, different intensities

The frequency is fixed and only the brightness changes.

  • Positive voltage: every emitted electron is collected, so the current levels off at a saturation current. Brighter light means more photons per second, more electrons per second and a higher saturation current.
  • Negative voltage: the electrons are slowed, and only the faster ones arrive, so the current falls. It falls gradually because the electrons have a range of kinetic energies.
  • Stopping voltage: all three curves reach zero at the same \(-V_{s}\), because the maximum kinetic energy depends on the frequency, not the intensity.
Common misconceptions
  • "Brighter light needs a bigger stopping voltage." The photons are more numerous, not more energetic, so \(V_{s}\) is unchanged.
  • "Increasing the positive voltage keeps increasing the current." Once every electron is collected, a larger voltage cannot collect more. Only brighter light can.
  • "The current is zero at zero voltage." Electrons leave with kinetic energy, so they reach the collector with no help.
Graph 4 · Current versus voltage, different frequencies

Now the frequency changes, with the same number of photons arriving per second.

  • Higher frequency means more energy per photon, a larger \(E_{k(\text{max})}\) and so a larger stopping voltage: the curve reaches zero further to the left.
  • The saturation current is the same, because the same number of photons frees the same number of electrons each second.
Common misconceptions
  • "Higher frequency gives more current." Frequency sets the energy of each electron; the number of photons sets how many electrons there are.
  • "Same intensity means same saturation current." Intensity is energy per second. At a higher frequency each photon carries more energy, so the same intensity contains fewer photons and would give a slightly smaller current. Graphs like this one assume equal photon numbers.
Graph 5 · Current versus intensity
  • Above the threshold frequency the line is straight and passes through the origin: doubling the intensity doubles the number of photons per second, and so doubles the current.
  • Below the threshold frequency the current is zero at every intensity. No photon has enough energy to free an electron.
Common misconceptions
  • "Bright enough light will always give a current." Not below \(f_{0}\): one electron absorbs one photon, and energy from several photons does not add up.
  • "Every photon frees an electron." Only a fraction do, but that fraction is constant, so the current is still proportional to intensity.

EXPLAIN ITWhy a photon model? · Merit / Excellence-style

Dim ultraviolet light ejects electrons from a zinc plate immediately, but very bright red light never does. Explain why this supports the photon model of light rather than the wave model.

Show model answer

In the wave model, the energy of light depends on its intensity, and it is spread across the surface. Bright red light would deliver plenty of energy, so it should eventually eject electrons, and dim UV light should take time to build up enough energy in one electron. The observations are the opposite. In the photon model, light arrives as photons of energy \(hf\), and each electron absorbs one whole photon. A UV photon has a high frequency, so \(hf\) is greater than zinc's work function and the electron escapes straight away, even if there are only a few photons (dim light). A red photon has \(hf\) less than the work function, so no electron ever gets enough energy to escape; making the light brighter only adds more photons that are each too weak. So the threshold frequency and the instant emission can only be explained by light being made of photons.

1.7 · Advanced understanding

How the photoelectric effect was discovered, debated and accepted.

The history of the photoelectric effect

Advanced. This section goes beyond the AS91525 standard and is not usually assessed as a specific question. It is here to deepen your understanding of how science develops.

The history: rival ideas and slow acceptance

Einstein's equation is now standard, but it was not accepted quickly. Historians of science who study how textbooks present this episode (Niaz et al., 2010) argue that a textbook which leaves out the points below makes the idea look simpler than it was.

  • There was a rival explanation. Lenard's trigger hypothesis (1902) said that electrons already hold their energy inside the atom, and light only triggers their release. Einstein's photon model competed with this idea, and both had to explain the same measurements.
  • Acceptance was slow. Einstein's light-quantum idea was largely set aside for about fifteen years. Planck, who had started the quantum idea, opposed Einstein's version until about 1913.
  • The experiments came first, and the interpretation was contested. Millikan confirmed Einstein's equation with great precision, and measured \(h\), in papers published between 1914 and 1916. He still called the light-quantum hypothesis reckless, because it seemed to conflict with the wave evidence for interference.
  • Data alone do not decide a theory. Millikan's measurements were accepted, but his rejection of the photon was not, so the same result can support different interpretations.

Einstein received the 1921 Nobel Prize in physics, mainly for explaining the photoelectric effect. Millikan received the 1923 prize, partly for his experiments on this effect.

02 · Models of the atom and spectra

Line spectra show that electrons in atoms can only have certain energies.

Line spectra, Rutherford, Bohr and energy levels

Six ideas. 2.1.1 to 2.1.3 describe the spectra; 2.1.4 to 2.1.6 are the models that explain them. Try them in the lab in 2.2.

2.1.1 · Atomic line spectra

A spectrum is the mixture of wavelengths in light, spread out by a prism or diffraction grating. It is not always a continuous range.

  • A line spectrum comes from any gas whose atoms do not interact. An electric discharge through hydrogen at low pressure emits only particular wavelengths, so the spectrum is a set of separate lines in the infrared, visible and ultraviolet.
  • A continuous spectrum comes from tightly packed atoms or molecules (a hot solid, liquid or dense gas). They vibrate, rotate, collide and exert forces on each other, which creates so many possible energy states that the colours merge into a continuous range.

Every element has its own pattern of lines, like a fingerprint.

2.1.2 · Emission and absorption spectra

Emission spectrum: light radiated directly from its source and passed through a prism or grating. For a low-pressure gas it is bright lines on a dark background. Hydrogen has four visible lines: red (656 nm), blue-green (486 nm), blue-violet (434 nm) and violet (410 nm).

Absorption spectrum: when white light shines through a cooler gas, the atoms absorb photons whose energies match those in their emission spectrum. They re-emit photons of these energies, but in all directions, so the intensity in the forward direction is reduced at those wavelengths. The result is dark lines on a continuous spectrum.

  • The Sun's spectrum is crossed by many faint dark lines. Some wavelengths emitted from the hot interior are absorbed by cooler gases, such as hydrogen, in its outer layers.
  • The absorption lines of an element are at exactly the same wavelengths as its emission lines.
2.1.3 · Rydberg's formula and spectral series

In 1885 J. J. Balmer, a Swiss mathematics teacher, found a formula for the positions of the four visible hydrogen lines. Rydberg suggested it could be generalised, with lines in other regions of the electromagnetic spectrum. Lyman (ultraviolet) and Paschen (infrared) later found those lines and confirmed the formula:

\[\dfrac{1}{\lambda}=R\left(\dfrac{1}{S^2}-\dfrac{1}{L^2}\right)\qquad R=1.097\times10^{7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\]

\(S\) is the number of the series and \(L\) = \(S\) + 1, \(S\) + 2, \(S\) + 3, …

  • Lyman (\(S\) = 1): ultraviolet, 91–122 nm.
  • Balmer (\(S\) = 2): visible and near-UV, 365–656 nm. Balmer saw the lines for \(L\) = 3, 4, 5 and 6.
  • Paschen (\(S\) = 3): infrared, 820–1875 nm.
  • Brackett (\(S\) = 4): infrared, 1458–4051 nm.
  • Pfund (\(S\) = 5): infrared, 2279–7460 nm.
Worked example · first line of the Brackett series

The first line has \(S = 4\) and \(L = 5\): \(\dfrac{1}{\lambda} = 1.097\times10^{7}\left(\dfrac{1}{16} - \dfrac{1}{25}\right) = 2.468\times10^{5}\ \text{m}^{-1}\).

So \(\lambda = 4.05\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}\) = 4050 nm, in the infrared. In the Bohr model this is an electron falling from n = 5 to n = 4.

Lines in a series crowd together towards a series limit, the line for \(L = \infty\). The formula described the lines, but nobody yet knew why it worked.

2.1.4 · Rutherford's nuclear model and its problem

Rutherford proposed a nuclear model: electrons orbit a small, positively charged nucleus that contains 99.9% of the atom's mass.

  • An electron moving in a circle has a centripetal acceleration.
  • Electromagnetic theory says an accelerating charge emits electromagnetic radiation.
  • So the electron should steadily lose energy and spiral into the nucleus.

Atoms do not collapse, so the model cannot be the full picture. It also cannot explain why atoms emit only certain frequencies of light, as seen in emission spectra.

2.1.5 · The Bohr model of the hydrogen atom

In 1913 Bohr, a student of Rutherford, modified the model with two conditions:

  • The electron can move around the nucleus in certain allowed orbits only.
  • The electron has a different, fixed amount of energy (kinetic + potential) in each orbit.

An electron can be raised to a higher orbit by giving it energy: by heating, by a high voltage across the gas, by a collision with an electron from another atom, or by absorbing electromagnetic radiation. When it jumps back to a lower orbit, the energy it loses is emitted as one photon:

\[hf=E_2-E_1\]
  • An electron jump is called a transition.
  • The greater the energy change, the higher the frequency of the photon.
  • Each possible transition gives a different spectral line.
2.1.6 · Discrete energy levels and ionisation

Bohr's allowed orbits only work for hydrogen. They have been replaced by a wave-mechanics model in which allowed orbits become allowed energy levels, but \(hf = E_{L} - E_{S}\) still applies. Combining Rydberg's formula with \(c = f\lambda\) and \(E = hf\):

\[f=cR\left(\dfrac{1}{S^2}-\dfrac{1}{L^2}\right)\qquad hf=\dfrac{hcR}{S^2}-\dfrac{hcR}{L^2}=E_L-E_S\]

So the energy of level \(n\) in hydrogen is:

\[E_n=-\dfrac{hcR}{n^2}=-\dfrac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{n^2}\]
  • Work must be done to remove an electron completely (to ionise the atom). A free electron is taken to have zero energy, so all bound levels are negative.
  • \(n\) = 1 is the ground state, with the largest negative energy (−13.6 eV). In the ground state there is no unoccupied level beneath the electron.
  • In an excited state the electron has been raised to a higher level, leaving an unoccupied level beneath it.
  • The minimum energy needed to ionise a hydrogen atom from its ground state is 13.6 eV.
  • Limitations of the Bohr model: it only works for one-electron atoms, cannot explain the different brightness of lines, and does not explain why only those orbits are allowed.

The hot, dense interior of the Sun emits a continuous spectrum, because its tightly packed atoms have a huge number of possible energy states. This light then passes through the cooler, low-density gas of the Sun's outer layers, which includes hydrogen. A hydrogen atom can only have certain energies, so it absorbs a photon only if the photon's energy exactly equals the gap between two of its levels, \(hf = E_{L} - E_{S}\). The excited atom soon drops back and re-emits a photon of the same energy, but in a random direction, so much less light of that wavelength continues towards Earth: a dark line. Emission from a discharge tube uses the same pairs of levels, with the electron dropping instead of rising, so the photon energies, and therefore the wavelengths, are identical. The pattern of dark lines identifies the elements in the Sun's outer layers.

Beyond Bohr: waves, clouds and orbitals

Advanced. This section goes beyond the AS91525 standard and is not usually assessed as a specific question. It explains why Bohr's rule works, and what replaced the orbits.

De Broglie's idea

In 1924 de Broglie suggested that particles, including electrons, have a wavelength given by the same kind of relation as light's:

\( \lambda = \dfrac{h}{p} = \dfrac{h}{mv} \)

Electron diffraction in 1927 (Davisson and Germer) showed that electrons do produce interference patterns, which supports the idea.

Why it explains Bohr's orbits

Think of the electron as a wave that must join up with itself around the orbit. That only happens if the circumference holds a whole number of wavelengths:

\( 2\pi r = n\lambda \)

Substituting \( \lambda = h/mv \) and rearranging gives \( mvr = \dfrac{nh}{2\pi} = n\hbar \). This is Bohr's rule for angular momentum, so it is not an extra assumption. It follows from the wave picture.

Where the electron cloud came from
  • 1926, Schrödinger: he wrote a wave equation for the electron in an atom. Its solutions \(\psi\) are allowed wave patterns, each with a set of quantum numbers. Bohr's orbit is replaced by a wave pattern around the nucleus.

How the model shrank the building blocks

Before 1964, physicists had found hundreds of hadrons (particles that feel the strong force, such as protons, neutrons and pions), and many seemed equally fundamental. Gell-Mann and Zweig showed that this pattern could be explained by a small set of building blocks. Three quark flavours, u, d and s, and their antiquarks were enough to build the known hadrons.

This also made the proton and neutron less fundamental. Instead of being simple particles, they became combinations of three quarks. The proton is about \(10^{-15}\) m across, and in experiments so far each quark has looked point-like.

In 1968, experiments at SLAC fired electrons at protons and found hard scattering from point-like particles inside, which were identified with quarks. Gell-Mann received the 1969 Nobel Prize in Physics for his work on the classification of elementary particles.

03 · Nuclear reactions

Mass defect, binding energy, and why splitting heavy nuclei and joining light ones both release energy.

Nuclear reactions

Seven ideas. The binding energy per nucleon curve (3.1.4) ties everything together.

3.1.1 · The nucleus

A nucleus is written \(^{A}_{Z}\text{X}\): \(A\) is the nucleon (mass) number, protons + neutrons; \(Z\) is the atomic (proton) number. The number of neutrons is \(N = A - Z\). Isotopes have the same \(Z\) but different \(N\).

The protons repel each other electrically (Coulomb repulsion), and this force acts across the whole nucleus. The nucleus is held together by the strong nuclear force, which attracts all nucleons, protons and neutrons alike, but only acts over about 10⁻¹⁵ m, between near neighbours.

3.1.2 · Einstein's mass–energy relation and the atomic mass unit

In 1905 Einstein proposed that energy and mass are equivalent. Any reaction that gives out energy must also lose mass:

\[E=mc^2\]

\(E\) is the energy in J, \(m\) the mass in kg and \(c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}\) m s⁻¹.

  • In physical changes (heating water) and chemical changes (burning hydrogen in oxygen), the change in mass is far too small to measure.
  • In nuclear reactions the energy released is so large that the change in mass is significant.

Nuclear masses are measured in unified atomic mass units. One carbon-12 atom has a mass of 19.92 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, and 1 u is defined as one-twelfth of this: 1 u = 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. Its energy equivalent is:

\[E=1.661\times10^{-27}\times(3.00\times10^{8})^2=1.49\times10^{-10}\ \text{J}\equiv931.5\ \text{MeV}\]

So the mass of a particle can be quoted as an energy. The mass of an electron is about 0.5 MeV.

3.1.3 · Mass defect and binding energy

The nucleons in a nucleus are bound together by the strong force. Work must be done against this force to separate them, so they have less potential energy when bound together than as separate particles, and therefore less mass. The mass of a nucleus is less than the total mass of its separate protons and neutrons. The difference is the mass defect (or mass deficit):

\[\Delta m=Zm_p+(A-Z)m_n-m_{\text{nucleus}}\]

Energy and mass are equivalent. The mass defect corresponds to the binding energy, the energy needed to pull the nucleus completely apart (and the energy released when it formed):

\[\Delta E=\Delta m\,c^2\qquad 1\ \text{u}=1.661\times10^{-27}\ \text{kg}\equiv931.5\ \text{MeV}\]
  • In words: mass defect = mass of separate nucleons − mass of nucleus, and binding energy = mass defect × \(c^{2}\).
  • If you are given the mass of the whole atom, include the mass of its electrons with the nucleons.
3.1.4 · Binding energy per nucleon

Dividing the binding energy by \(A\) gives the average energy needed to remove one nucleon, which measures how stable the nucleus is. The curve rises steeply for light nuclei, peaks at about 8.8 MeV per nucleon near iron-56 and nickel-62, then falls slowly for heavy nuclei.

  • Nuclei near the peak are the most stable. Medium-mass nuclei have more than 8 MeV per nucleon.
  • Light nuclei have less binding energy per nucleon, so they are less stable and can be joined to make more stable nuclei (fusion).
  • The heaviest nuclei also have less binding energy per nucleon, so they can be split to make more stable nuclei (fission).
  • Any reaction that moves nuclei towards the peak increases the total binding energy, lowers the total mass, and releases energy.
  • Light nuclei release energy by fusing; heavy nuclei release energy by splitting. Explore the curve in 3.2.
3.1.5 · Nuclear equations and conservation laws

In a nuclear reaction, mass and energy considered separately are not conserved, but the total mass–energy is. In every nuclear reaction:

  • Nucleon number is conserved: the top numbers add up to the same total on each side.
  • Charge (atomic number) is conserved: the bottom numbers add up too.
  • Mass–energy is conserved: any decrease in mass appears as kinetic energy of the products (and gamma rays).
  • Momentum is conserved too.

Alpha particle \(^{4}_{2}\)He; beta-minus electron \(^{0}_{-1}\)e; neutron \(^{1}_{0}\)n; gamma ray (no change in \(A\) or \(Z\)).

3.1.6 · Fission

A slow neutron is absorbed by a uranium-235 nucleus, which becomes unstable and splits into two medium-sized nuclei, two or three neutrons and about 200 MeV of energy, for example:

\[{}^{1}_{0}\text{n}+{}^{235}_{92}\text{U}\rightarrow{}^{141}_{56}\text{Ba}+{}^{92}_{36}\text{Kr}+3\,{}^{1}_{0}\text{n}\]
  • The neutrons released can cause more fissions: a chain reaction.
  • In a reactor, a moderator (water, graphite) slows the neutrons so they are more easily absorbed, and control rods (boron, cadmium) absorb neutrons so that, on average, exactly one neutron from each fission causes another.
  • The products are closer to the peak of the curve, so they are more tightly bound and have less total mass.
3.1.7 · Fusion

Two light nuclei join to make a heavier one. The fusion reaction planned for power stations is deuterium–tritium:

\[{}^{2}_{1}\text{H}+{}^{3}_{1}\text{H}\rightarrow{}^{4}_{2}\text{He}+{}^{1}_{0}\text{n}+17.6\ \text{MeV}\]
  • Nuclei are positive and repel. They must be moving very fast (temperatures of millions of degrees) to get close enough for the strong force to take over.
  • Per kilogram of fuel, fusion releases several times more energy than fission, because the binding energy curve is so steep for light nuclei.
  • The Sun fuses hydrogen into helium; its gravity provides the pressure and temperature needed.

  • More binding energy per nucleon means less mass per nucleon.
  • A graph of average mass per nucleon against nucleon number is the binding energy curve turned upside down: it falls steeply for light nuclei, reaches a minimum near iron, then rises slowly for heavy nuclei.
  • The most stable nuclei are at the bottom of this curve.

Show model answer

Binding energy is the energy needed to separate a nucleus into its nucleons; it equals the mass defect times \(c^{2}\). Quarks: the Gell-Mann model

Advanced. This section goes beyond the AS91525 standard and is not usually assessed as a specific question. It shows how the particle zoo was organised.

Where the model came from

In 1964 Murray Gell-Mann proposed that many particles are built from smaller particles, which he called quarks. George Zweig made a similar proposal at the same time. Gell-Mann took the name from James Joyce's Finnegans Wake.

Three quarks were enough to explain the particles known at the time. Charm (1974), bottom (1977) and top (1995) were found later, and each one was predicted by the same pattern.

Why quarks are never seen alone

Quarks are held together by the strong force, which is carried by particles called gluons. The force does not weaken as quarks move apart, so a single quark is never observed. Only combinations are seen. These combinations are called hadrons: particles made of quarks and held together by the strong force. Protons and neutrons are hadrons made of three quarks; mesons such as the pion are a quark and an antiquark.
