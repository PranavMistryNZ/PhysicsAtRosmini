The big ideas

Modern physics starts where classical physics breaks down. Energy comes in packets: light arrives as photons of energy \(hf\), and electrons in atoms can only have certain energies. Mass is a form of energy: when a nucleus becomes more tightly bound, a little mass disappears and an enormous amount of energy appears, \(\Delta E = \Delta mc^{2}\). Both ideas are tested with careful unit conversions: eV ↔ J, u ↔ kg ↔ MeV, nm ↔ m.

Assessment skills · writing modern physics answers

AS91525 is internally assessed, so your teacher sets the task and the conditions. The criteria are the same as for the other Level 3 physics standards: demonstrate understanding (Achievement), in-depth understanding (Merit) and comprehensive understanding (Excellence).

Achievement

Describe the observations and models; one-step calculations such as the energy of a photon, a threshold frequency, the energy of a level or the energy from a mass defect.

Merit

Explain why: why the photoelectric effect needs photons, why spectra are lines, why energy is released. Multi-step calculations with unit conversions (eV ↔ J, u ↔ MeV).

Excellence

Comprehensive, linked explanations that bring several ideas together, for example the binding energy curve, mass defect and \(\Delta E = \Delta mc^{2}\) to explain why both fission and fusion release energy, supported by correct calculations.

A structure for "explain" questions

1Name the model

Photon model of light, Bohr model, mass–energy equivalence, binding energy…

2Apply it to this situation

"Each photon of UV light has energy \(hf\) = 4.1 eV…"

3Link with physics words or an equation

"…which is more than the work function of zinc (3.6 eV), so one photon gives one electron enough energy to escape."

4Answer the question asked

"So UV light ejects electrons from zinc, but red light does not, however bright it is."

Three chains cover most explanations:

Light is photons, \(E = hf\)→one photon gives all its energy to one electron→escapes only if \(hf \ge \phi\)→leftover energy is \(E_{k(\text{max})} = hf - \phi\)
Electrons have fixed energy levels→a jump changes the energy by a fixed \(\Delta E\)→photon with \(hf = \Delta E\)→only certain wavelengths: a line spectrum
Products have more binding energy per nucleon→more tightly bound→total mass is less (mass defect)→energy released, \(\Delta E = \Delta mc^{2}\)

CEO worked example · photoelectric effect

Question: Ultraviolet light of wavelength 250 nm falls on zinc, which has a work function of 4.3 eV. Calculate the maximum kinetic energy of the ejected electrons, in joules, and the stopping voltage.

C · Conceptualise: one photon gives its energy to one electron; \(\phi\) of it is needed to escape and the rest is kinetic energy: \(E_{k(\text{max})} = hf - \phi\). Knowns: \(\lambda = 250 \times 10^{-9}\) m, \(\phi = 4.3 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 6.88 \times 10^{-19}\) J.

E · Execute: \(E = hc/\lambda = 6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}/250 \times 10^{-9} = 7.96 \times 10^{-19}\) J. \(E_{k(\text{max})} = 7.96 \times 10^{-19} - 6.88 \times 10^{-19}\) = 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. \(V_{s} = E_{k}/e = 1.08 \times 10^{-19}/1.60 \times 10^{-19}\) = 0.68 V.

O · Outcomes: J and V ✓. The photon energy (4.97 eV) is just above the work function, so a small \(E_{k}\) is sensible ✓. Check in eV: 4.97 − 4.3 = 0.67 eV ✓.

CEO worked example · mass defect

Question: Calculate the energy released, in MeV and in joules, when a deuterium nucleus (2.014102 u) fuses with a tritium nucleus (3.016049 u) to form helium-4 (4.002603 u) and a neutron (1.008665 u). (Atomic masses.)

C · Conceptualise: the products are more tightly bound, so their total mass is less. The missing mass is released as energy, \(\Delta E = \Delta mc^{2}\).

E · Execute: mass before = 5.030151 u; after = 5.011268 u; \(\Delta m\) = 0.018883 u. \(\Delta E = 0.018883 \times 931.5\) = 17.6 MeV. In joules: \(0.018883 \times 1.661 \times 10^{-27} \times (3.00 \times 10^{8})^{2}\) = 2.82 × 10⁻¹² J.

O · Outcomes: about 20 MeV, typical for fusion ✓. Keep all six decimal places in the masses: the difference is in the third decimal place, so rounding the masses early destroys the answer.

Common traps

Intensity vs frequencyBrighter light means more photons per second: more electrons, more current. It does not change the energy of each photon or the maximum kinetic energy.
eV and J1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Work functions and levels are often given in eV; \(h\) is in J s. Convert before you substitute.
"Maximum" kinetic energyElectrons from deeper in the metal lose energy on the way out. \(hf - \phi\) is the maximum kinetic energy.
S and L in Rydberg\(S\) is the lower (final for emission) level and \(L\) the higher. Swapping them gives a negative wavelength.
Negative energiesLevel energies are negative; the photon energy is the difference, always positive. Ionisation from the ground state needs 13.6 eV.
Binding energy is not "stored"Binding energy is the energy you must supply to pull a nucleus apart. More binding energy per nucleon = more stable, not more energy to release.
Rounding masses earlyMass defects are tiny differences between big numbers. Keep every decimal place until the end.
Balancing equationsCheck both the top numbers (nucleons) and the bottom numbers (charge), and count the neutrons released in fission.
Fission vs fusionFission splits heavy nuclei (right of iron); fusion joins light nuclei (left of iron). Both move towards iron.

Significant figures and units

  • nm → × 10⁻⁹ m; MeV → × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J; 1 u = 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 931.5 MeV.
  • Give the final answer to the same number of significant figures as the data, with a unit. In mass-defect questions, keep all the decimal places of the masses.
  • Always show the equation, then the substitution, then the answer with a unit.

01 · The photoelectric effect and atomic energy levels

Light behaves as particles called photons, and electrons in atoms have only certain energies.

Photons, electrons and energy levels

Six ideas. 1.1.2 and 1.1.3 explain the photoelectric effect; 1.1.4 to 1.1.6 explain line spectra.

1.1.1 · Photons

Light spreads out like a wave (it diffracts and interferes), but it delivers its energy in packets called photons. Each photon carries energy:

\[E=hf=\dfrac{hc}{\lambda}\]
  • \(h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\) J s is Planck's constant.
  • Higher frequency (shorter wavelength) means more energy per photon: a UV photon carries more energy than a red one.
  • Photon energies are tiny, so we often use the electronvolt: 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Visible photons are about 1.8 eV (red) to 3.1 eV (violet).
  • Brighter light of the same colour means more photons per second, not more energetic ones.
1.1.2 · The photoelectric effect

Light shining on a clean metal surface can knock electrons out of it. Experiments show:

  • No electrons are emitted below a threshold frequency \(f_{0}\), however bright the light.
  • Above \(f_{0}\), electrons are emitted instantly, even with very dim light.
  • The maximum kinetic energy of the electrons depends on the frequency, not the intensity.
  • The number of electrons per second (the current) is proportional to the intensity.

The wave model predicts the opposite: any frequency should work if the light is bright enough, dim light should need time to build up energy, and brighter light should give faster electrons. The photon model explains every observation.

1.1.3 · Einstein's photoelectric equation

One photon gives all its energy to one electron. The electron needs a minimum energy, the work function \(\phi\), to escape the metal. Whatever is left over becomes kinetic energy:

\[hf=\phi+E_{k(\text{max})}\qquad E_{k(\text{max})}=hf-\phi\]
  • Threshold frequency: \(hf_{0} = \phi\), so \(f_{0} = \phi /h\) and threshold wavelength \(\lambda_{0} = hc/\phi\).
  • A reverse voltage can stop even the fastest electrons. The stopping voltage \(V_{s}\) satisfies \(eV_{s} = E_{k(\text{max})}\).
  • A graph of \(E_{k(\text{max})}\) against \(f\) is a straight line: gradient \(h\), \(x\)-intercept \(f_{0}\), \(y\)-intercept −\(\phi\). Its gradient is the same for every metal.
1.1.4 · Line spectra

A hot, low-pressure gas gives out light at only certain wavelengths: an emission line spectrum. White light passed through a cool gas has dark lines at exactly the same wavelengths: an absorption spectrum.

Every element has its own pattern of lines, like a fingerprint. That is how we know what distant stars are made of. Lines mean the atoms can only give out or take in certain fixed amounts of energy.

1.1.5 · The Bohr model and energy levels

Bohr proposed that the electron in a hydrogen atom can only occupy certain orbits, each with a fixed energy:

\[E_n=-\dfrac{hcR}{n^2}=-\dfrac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{n^2}\]
  • \(n\) = 1 is the ground state (−13.6 eV); higher \(n\) are excited states.
  • Energies are negative because zero is the energy of a free electron. Removing the electron from the ground state (ionisation) needs 13.6 eV.
  • When the electron drops from a higher level to a lower one, it emits a single photon with \(hf = E_{L} - E_{S}\). To move up, it must absorb a photon of exactly that energy (or get it in a collision).
  • Limitations: the Bohr model only works for hydrogen (one electron), cannot explain the different brightness of lines, and does not explain why the orbits are allowed. It was replaced by quantum mechanics.
1.1.6 · The Rydberg formula and spectral series
\[\dfrac{1}{\lambda}=R\left(\dfrac{1}{S^2}-\dfrac{1}{L^2}\right)\qquad R=1.097\times10^{7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\]

\(S\) is the lower level and \(L\) the higher one. All the lines that end on the same lower level form a series:

  • Lyman (\(S\) = 1): ultraviolet, 91–122 nm.
  • Balmer (\(S\) = 2): visible and near-UV, 365–656 nm. The red line is 3 → 2.
  • Paschen (\(S\) = 3): infrared, 820–1875 nm.

Lines in a series crowd together towards a series limit, the line for an electron falling from \(L = \infty\). Try it in 1.3.

Photoelectric effect & energy levels · practice problems

Original practice questions in the style of AS91525. These are not NZQA questions. \(h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\) J s, \(c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}\) m s⁻¹, \(e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\) C, \(R = 1.097 \times 10^{7}\) m⁻¹.

QUESTION ONESodium photocell

Violet light of wavelength 400 nm falls on sodium, which has a work function of 2.28 eV.

(a) Calculate the energy of one photon in joules and in eV. Achievement

(b) Calculate the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons and the stopping voltage. Merit

(c) Calculate the threshold wavelength for sodium. Explain why red light of 650 nm cannot eject electrons from sodium, however bright it is. Excellence

Show worked answer · Question One

(a) \(E = hc/\lambda = 6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}/400 \times 10^{-9} = 4.97 \times 10^{-19}\) J. In eV: \(4.97 \times 10^{-19}/1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 3.11\) eV.

(b) \(E_{k(\text{max})} = hf - \phi = 3.11 - 2.28 = 0.83\) eV = \(1.3 \times 10^{-19}\) J. Stopping voltage \(V_{s} = E_{k}/e\) = 0.83 V.

(c) \(\phi = 2.28 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 3.65 \times 10^{-19}\) J. \(\lambda_{0} = hc/\phi = 1.989 \times 10^{-25}/3.65 \times 10^{-19} = 5.45 \times 10^{-7}\) m = 545 nm. A 650 nm photon has energy \(hc/\lambda\) = 1.91 eV, less than the work function. One electron absorbs one photon, so no single electron ever gets enough energy to escape. Brighter light only means more of these too-weak photons each second, so still no electrons are emitted.

QUESTION TWOMeasuring Planck's constant

A student measures the stopping voltage for a metal at two frequencies: 1.65 V at \(1.50 \times 10^{15}\) Hz and 3.72 V at \(2.00 \times 10^{15}\) Hz.

(a) Convert both stopping voltages into maximum kinetic energies in joules. Achievement

(b) Use the results to find a value for Planck's constant. Merit

(c) Find the work function in eV and the threshold frequency. Explain why using brighter light would not change these results. Excellence

Show worked answer · Question Two

(a) \(E_{k} = eV_{s}\): \(1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 1.65 = 2.64 \times 10^{-19}\) J and \(1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 3.72 = 5.95 \times 10^{-19}\) J.

(b) The gradient of \(E_{k}\) against \(f\) is \(h\): \(h = (5.95 - 2.64) \times 10^{-19}/(0.50 \times 10^{15}) = 6.62 \times 10^{-34}\) J s.

(c) \(\phi = hf - E_{k} = 6.62 \times 10^{-34} \times 1.50 \times 10^{15} - 2.64 \times 10^{-19} = 7.29 \times 10^{-19}\) J = 4.6 eV. \(f_{0} = \phi /h = 1.10 \times 10^{15}\) Hz. Brighter light delivers more photons per second, so more electrons are emitted and the current rises, but each photon still has energy \(hf\), so the maximum kinetic energy, and hence the stopping voltage, is unchanged.

QUESTION THREEThe red hydrogen line

Hydrogen gas in a discharge tube glows with a red line.

(a) Calculate the energy of the \(n\) = 3 level of hydrogen in eV. Achievement

(b) Calculate the wavelength of the photon emitted when an electron drops from \(n\) = 3 to \(n\) = 2, and name the series it belongs to. Merit

(c) Explain why hydrogen gives a line spectrum rather than a continuous one, and why a cool hydrogen cloud in front of a star produces dark lines at the same wavelengths. Excellence

Show worked answer · Question Three

(a) \(E_{3} = -13.6/3^{2} = -1.51\) eV (\(-2.42 \times 10^{-19}\) J).

(b) \(1/\lambda = 1.097 \times 10^{7} \times (1/2^{2} - 1/3^{2}) = 1.524 \times 10^{6}\) m⁻¹, so \(\lambda = 6.56 \times 10^{-7}\) m = 656 nm. It ends on \(n\) = 2, so it is in the Balmer series.

(c) The electron can only have certain energies. When it drops between two levels it emits one photon whose energy equals the gap, \(hf = \Delta E\). There are only a limited number of possible gaps, so only certain frequencies and wavelengths appear, giving bright lines. In the cool cloud, most electrons are in low levels. A photon from the star is absorbed only if its energy exactly matches a gap, lifting an electron up. The electron soon drops back, but re-emits the photon in a random direction, so very little of that wavelength continues towards us: those wavelengths appear as dark lines at exactly the emission wavelengths.

1.6 · Applications

Photons and energy levels in technology and astronomy.

Where do we use these ideas?

Assessment tasks are often set in contexts like these, with the information you need supplied.

Light sensors and photomultipliersPhotoelectric

Photomultiplier tubes in medical scanners and telescopes turn single photons into measurable pulses of current.

Photon hits photocathode → one electron emitted → accelerated and multiplied → measurable current

Exam angle: the photocathode must have a work function lower than the photon energy.

Solar cellsPhoton energy

A photon absorbed in a silicon solar cell frees an electron to carry current. Photons below about 1.1 eV (infrared) can't do it.

Photon energy ≥ threshold → electron freed → current; extra energy above threshold → heat

Exam angle: brighter sunlight gives more current, not higher-energy electrons.

What stars are made ofAbsorption spectra

Dark lines in sunlight (Fraunhofer lines) match the emission lines of hydrogen, helium, sodium, iron and more. Helium was discovered in the Sun this way.

Cooler gas in the star's atmosphere absorbs exact photon energies → dark lines → identify the element

Exam angle: absorption and emission lines have the same wavelengths.

Street lights and neon signsEmission spectra

Sodium street lamps glow yellow-orange; neon signs glow red. Each colour comes from the energy levels of the gas.

Electric current excites atoms → electrons drop back → photons of fixed energies → characteristic colour

Exam angle: the colour depends on the gas, not the voltage.

Fluorescent lightsEnergy levels

Mercury vapour emits ultraviolet photons; a phosphor coating absorbs them and re-emits several lower-energy visible photons.

One high-energy UV photon absorbed → electron climbs high → falls in smaller steps → visible photons

Exam angle: a big drop can be made in several smaller steps, each giving a longer wavelength.

LasersEnergy levels

All the photons in a laser beam come from the same transition, so they share one wavelength.

Electrons pumped to a higher level → stimulated to drop together → identical photons → single colour, coherent beam

Exam angle: the laser's wavelength is set by the energy gap, \(\lambda = hc/\Delta E\).

EXPLAIN ITWhy a photon model? · Merit / Excellence-style

Dim ultraviolet light ejects electrons from a zinc plate immediately, but very bright red light never does. Explain why this supports the photon model of light rather than the wave model.

Show model answer

In the wave model, the energy of light depends on its intensity, and it is spread across the surface. Bright red light would deliver plenty of energy, so it should eventually eject electrons, and dim UV light should take time to build up enough energy in one electron. The observations are the opposite. In the photon model, light arrives as photons of energy \(hf\), and each electron absorbs one whole photon. A UV photon has a high frequency, so \(hf\) is greater than zinc's work function and the electron escapes straight away, even if there are only a few photons (dim light). A red photon has \(hf\) less than the work function, so no electron ever gets enough energy to escape; making the light brighter only adds more photons that are each too weak. So the threshold frequency and the instant emission can only be explained by light being made of photons.

02 · Nuclear reactions

Mass defect, binding energy, and why splitting heavy nuclei and joining light ones both release energy.

Nuclear reactions

Six ideas. The binding energy per nucleon curve (2.1.3) ties everything together.

2.1.1 · The nucleus

A nucleus is written \(^{A}_{Z}\text{X}\): \(A\) is the nucleon (mass) number, protons + neutrons; \(Z\) is the atomic (proton) number. The number of neutrons is \(N = A - Z\). Isotopes have the same \(Z\) but different \(N\).

The protons repel each other electrically. The nucleus is held together by the strong nuclear force, which attracts all nucleons but only acts over about 10⁻¹⁵ m.

2.1.2 · Mass defect and binding energy

The mass of a nucleus is less than the total mass of its separate protons and neutrons. The difference is the mass defect:

\[\Delta m=Zm_p+(A-Z)m_n-m_{\text{nucleus}}\]

Energy and mass are equivalent. The mass defect corresponds to the binding energy, the energy needed to pull the nucleus completely apart (and the energy released when it formed):

\[\Delta E=\Delta m\,c^2\qquad 1\ \text{u}=1.661\times10^{-27}\ \text{kg}\equiv931.5\ \text{MeV}\]
2.1.3 · Binding energy per nucleon

Dividing the binding energy by \(A\) shows how tightly bound each nucleon is. The curve rises steeply for light nuclei, peaks at about 8.8 MeV per nucleon near iron-56 and nickel-62, then falls slowly for heavy nuclei.

  • Nuclei near the peak are the most stable.
  • Any reaction that moves nuclei towards the peak increases the total binding energy, lowers the total mass, and releases energy.
  • Light nuclei release energy by fusing; heavy nuclei release energy by splitting. Explore the curve in 2.2.
2.1.4 · Nuclear equations

In every nuclear reaction:

  • Nucleon number is conserved: the top numbers add up to the same total on each side.
  • Charge is conserved: the bottom numbers add up too.
  • Mass–energy is conserved: any decrease in mass appears as kinetic energy of the products (and gamma rays).
  • Momentum is conserved too.

Alpha particle \(^{4}_{2}\)He; beta-minus electron \(^{0}_{-1}\)e; neutron \(^{1}_{0}\)n; gamma ray (no change in \(A\) or \(Z\)).

2.1.5 · Fission

A slow neutron is absorbed by a uranium-235 nucleus, which becomes unstable and splits into two medium-sized nuclei, two or three neutrons and about 200 MeV of energy, for example:

\[{}^{1}_{0}\text{n}+{}^{235}_{92}\text{U}\rightarrow{}^{141}_{56}\text{Ba}+{}^{92}_{36}\text{Kr}+3\,{}^{1}_{0}\text{n}\]
  • The neutrons released can cause more fissions: a chain reaction.
  • In a reactor, a moderator (water, graphite) slows the neutrons so they are more easily absorbed, and control rods (boron, cadmium) absorb neutrons so that, on average, exactly one neutron from each fission causes another.
  • The products are closer to the peak of the curve, so they are more tightly bound and have less total mass.
2.1.6 · Fusion

Two light nuclei join to make a heavier one. The fusion reaction planned for power stations is deuterium–tritium:

\[{}^{2}_{1}\text{H}+{}^{3}_{1}\text{H}\rightarrow{}^{4}_{2}\text{He}+{}^{1}_{0}\text{n}+17.6\ \text{MeV}\]
  • Nuclei are positive and repel. They must be moving very fast (temperatures of millions of degrees) to get close enough for the strong force to take over.
  • Per kilogram of fuel, fusion releases several times more energy than fission, because the binding energy curve is so steep for light nuclei.
  • The Sun fuses hydrogen into helium; its gravity provides the pressure and temperature needed.

Look for these ideas

  • Mass defects are tiny, often in the third or fourth decimal place of the mass in u, so keep every decimal place until the end.
  • Fission releases about 170–200 MeV per reaction; fusion about 3–18 MeV; alpha decay about 4–5 MeV; beta decay less than 1 MeV.
  • Per nucleon, D–T fusion (17.6 MeV ÷ 5) beats U-235 fission (≈ 173 MeV ÷ 236) by about five times.
  • These are atomic masses. They include the electrons, which cancel on each side for these reactions (for beta-minus decay, the new atom's extra electron accounts for the emitted one).

Nuclear reactions · practice problems

Original practice questions in the style of AS91525. These are not NZQA questions. \(m_{p}\) = 1.00728 u, \(m_{n}\) = 1.00867 u, 1 u = 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 931.5 MeV, \(c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}\) m s⁻¹.

QUESTION ONEBinding energy of helium-4

The mass of a helium-4 nucleus is 4.00151 u.

(a) State the number of protons and neutrons in a helium-4 nucleus. Achievement

(b) Calculate the mass defect, and the binding energy in MeV and in joules. Merit

(c) Calculate the binding energy per nucleon, and explain what it tells you about the stability of helium-4 compared with lithium-6 (5.3 MeV per nucleon). Excellence

Show worked answer · Question One

(a) 2 protons and 2 neutrons.

(b) Mass of separate nucleons = \(2 \times 1.00728 + 2 \times 1.00867 = 4.03190\) u. \(\Delta m = 4.03190 - 4.00151 = 0.03039\) u. Binding energy = \(0.03039 \times 931.5 = 28.3\) MeV. In joules: \(\Delta m = 0.03039 \times 1.661 \times 10^{-27} = 5.048 \times 10^{-29}\) kg; \(\Delta E = \Delta mc^{2} = 4.54 \times 10^{-12}\) J.

(c) 28.3/4 = 7.08 MeV per nucleon. That is much higher than lithium-6, so on average each nucleon in helium-4 needs more energy to remove: helium-4 is more tightly bound and more stable. It is unusually stable for a light nucleus, which is why alpha particles are emitted as a unit.

QUESTION TWOUranium fission

\(^{1}_{0}\)n + \(^{235}_{92}\)U → \(^{141}_{56}\)Ba + \(^{92}_{36}\)Kr + x \(^{1}_{0}\)n. Atomic masses: U-235 = 235.043930 u, Ba-141 = 140.914411 u, Kr-92 = 91.926156 u, neutron = 1.008665 u.

(a) Find x and show that charge is conserved. Achievement

(b) Calculate the energy released per fission in MeV and in joules. Merit

(c) Explain, using binding energy per nucleon, why this reaction releases energy, and explain how a reactor keeps the chain reaction steady. Excellence

Show worked answer · Question Two

(a) Nucleons: 1 + 235 = 141 + 92 + x, so x = 3. Charge: 0 + 92 = 56 + 36 + 0 ✓.

(b) Before: 1.008665 + 235.043930 = 236.052595 u. After: 140.914411 + 91.926156 + 3 × 1.008665 = 235.866562 u. \(\Delta m\) = 0.186033 u. \(\Delta E = 0.186033 \times 931.5 = 173\) MeV = \(173 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-13} = 2.77 \times 10^{-11}\) J.

(c) Uranium-235 has a binding energy per nucleon of about 7.6 MeV; barium and krypton are about 8.3 and 8.5 MeV, closer to the peak of the curve. The products are more tightly bound, so their total binding energy is greater and their total mass is less. The missing mass is released as energy, mostly kinetic energy of the fragments, \(\Delta E = \Delta mc^{2}\). Each fission releases about three neutrons. A moderator slows them so they can be absorbed by more U-235, and control rods absorb the excess so that, on average, exactly one neutron from each fission causes another fission, keeping the power steady.

QUESTION THREEFusion fuel

\(^{2}_{1}\)H + \(^{3}_{1}\)H → \(^{4}_{2}\)He + \(^{1}_{0}\)n. Atomic masses: H-2 = 2.014102 u, H-3 = 3.016049 u, He-4 = 4.002603 u, neutron = 1.008665 u.

(a) Calculate the mass defect for one reaction. Achievement

(b) Calculate the energy released in one reaction, in joules. Merit

(c) Compare the energy released per nucleon with the fission in Question Two, and explain why fusion is so hard to achieve on Earth. Excellence

Show worked answer · Question Three

(a) Before: 5.030151 u. After: 5.011268 u. \(\Delta m\) = 0.018883 u.

(b) \(\Delta m = 0.018883 \times 1.661 \times 10^{-27} = 3.136 \times 10^{-29}\) kg. \(\Delta E = \Delta mc^{2} = 3.136 \times 10^{-29} \times 9.00 \times 10^{16} = 2.82 \times 10^{-12}\) J (17.6 MeV).

(c) Fusion: 17.6 MeV ÷ 5 nucleons = 3.5 MeV per nucleon. Fission: 173 MeV ÷ 236 nucleons = 0.73 MeV per nucleon. So fusion releases about five times more energy per nucleon (per kilogram of fuel). But both nuclei are positively charged and repel strongly. To get close enough for the short-range strong force to act, they must collide at enormous speeds, which needs temperatures of over 100 million °C. No material container can hold such a hot plasma, so it has to be confined with magnetic fields, which is very difficult to keep stable.

2.6 · Applications

Nuclear energy in stars, power stations, medicine and history.

Where do we use nuclear reactions?

Each application turns a small mass defect into a large amount of energy, or uses the radiation that comes with it.

The SunFusion

The Sun turns about 4 million tonnes of mass into energy every second by fusing hydrogen into helium in its core.

Huge gravity → high pressure and temperature (15 million K) → nuclei collide fast enough to fuse → mass defect → sunlight

Exam angle: four protons have more mass than one helium-4 nucleus; the difference is radiated.

Nuclear power stationsFission

About 10% of the world's electricity comes from fission reactors. Aotearoa New Zealand has none, by law.

Fission → fast fragments → heat in the fuel → steam → turbine → generator

Exam angle: explain the roles of the moderator, control rods and coolant.

Fusion research (ITER, tokamaks)Fusion

Tokamaks hold a plasma of deuterium and tritium hotter than the Sun's core in a magnetic "bottle".

Magnetic fields confine plasma → 150 million °C → D–T fusion → fast neutrons carry the energy out

Exam angle: why the temperature must be so high (electrostatic repulsion).

Smoke detectorsAlpha decay

A tiny americium-241 source ionises the air between two plates, letting a small current flow. Smoke absorbs the alpha particles and the current drops.

Alpha decay → ionised air → current; smoke → fewer ions → current falls → alarm

Exam angle: balance the decay equation: \(^{241}_{95}\)Am → \(^{237}_{93}\)Np + \(^{4}_{2}\)He.

PET scansE = mc²

A tracer emits positrons, which annihilate with electrons. All their mass becomes two gamma rays of 0.511 MeV each, travelling in opposite directions.

Positron + electron → mass completely converted → two gamma photons → detectors locate the source

Exam angle: the electron's rest mass, 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg, is equivalent to 0.511 MeV.

Rutherford and the nucleusHistory

New Zealander Ernest Rutherford discovered the nucleus (1911) and carried out the first artificial nuclear reaction (1917), turning nitrogen into oxygen.

\(^{14}_{7}\)N + \(^{4}_{2}\)He → \(^{17}_{8}\)O + \(^{1}_{1}\)H: nucleon number and charge both balance

Exam angle: a balancing exercise with a famous context.

EXPLAIN ITBoth release energy · Excellence-style

Explain how both the fission of heavy nuclei and the fusion of light nuclei can release energy. Refer to binding energy per nucleon and mass defect.

Show model answer

Binding energy is the energy needed to separate a nucleus into its nucleons; it equals the mass defect times \(c^{2}\). The binding energy per nucleon rises from light nuclei to a peak near iron-56 and then falls for heavier nuclei. In fusion, light nuclei such as deuterium and tritium (low on the curve) join to form helium-4, which has a much higher binding energy per nucleon. In fission, a heavy nucleus such as uranium-235 splits into two medium nuclei that are nearer the peak. In both cases the products have more binding energy in total than the reactants, so they are more tightly bound. A greater binding energy means a greater mass defect, so the products have less total mass than the reactants. The mass that disappears is released as energy, \(\Delta E = \Delta mc^{2}\), mostly as kinetic energy of the products. Neither process can release energy from iron, because it is already at the peak.
