The big ideas

Two conservation laws and one rule run through the whole standard. Energy is conserved (Kirchhoff's loop rule, Lenz's law, power in = power out). Exam skills · writing electrical systems answers

What the examiners look for, built from the NZQA 2025 assessment report for AS91526 and the achievement criteria.

Use an explanation chain

Merit and Excellence explanations link ideas in order and name the physics principle behind each step. Two chains cover most questions:

Change in current or motion→change in flux→induced emf (Faraday)→opposes the change (Lenz)→effect on current, time or energy
Charge builds on plates→\(V_{C} = Q/C\) rises→\(V_{R} = \varepsilon - V_{C}\) falls→current \(i = V_{R}/R\) falls→charging slows (exponential)

Achievement

Single ideas and one-step calculations: terminal voltage, a time constant, maximum current, identifying resonance when \(X_{L} = X_{C}\); sketching the basic shape of a charge or decay curve.

Merit

Link two ideas: explain why internal resistance lowers terminal voltage; graph exponential decay accurately; explain that at resonance \(X_{L}\) and \(X_{C}\) cancel so \(Z = R\).

Excellence

Complete chains using two concepts: how series/parallel changes voltage, capacitance and \(\tau\); resonance as minimum impedance and maximum current; matching energy stored in a capacitor and an inductor.

Traps the 2025 examiners reported

"Path of least resistance"Current does not "choose" one path. It divides between branches according to their resistances, and every closed path carries some current.
emf vs terminal voltageMany could not explain emf outside induction. emf is the energy given to each coulomb by the source; terminal voltage is what's left after the "lost volts" \(ir\).
Capacitors in parallel with a switchStudents misread what parallel capacitors do when a switch is opened or closed. Track where the charge can go, and remember parallel capacitors share the same voltage.
Inductor ≠ capacitorSome said a capacitor opposes the flow of current. An inductor opposes changes in current; a capacitor stores charge and opposes changes in voltage.
Peak from rmsMany wrote \(V_{\text{max}} = \sqrt{2V_{\text{rms}}}\). Correct: \(V_{\text{max}} = \sqrt{2} \times V_{\text{rms}}\). For 230 V mains, that's 325 V, not 21 V.

Basic definitionsLearn exact meanings: capacitance (charge stored per volt), time constant, emf, reactance, impedance, resonant frequency.
Not attempting every partSome candidates answered only the first parts of a question. Every part is a chance to show evidence, so write something for each.

Graph sketching checklist

  • Label both axes with quantity and unit.
  • Mark the starting value and draw the final value (asymptote) as a dashed line.
  • Mark \(\tau\) on the time axis and show the 63% (growth) or 37% (decay) point. Charging and discharging curves are steepest at the start.

01 · DC circuits

emf, internal resistance and Kirchhoff's laws: the energy and charge bookkeeping of any circuit.

emf, internal resistance and Kirchhoff's laws

Six ideas. The 2025 examiners reported many candidates couldn't separate emf from terminal voltage, so read 1.1.1 and 1.1.2 carefully.

1.1.1 · emf and potential difference

A battery does work on charges, moving them to its higher-potential terminal. The electromotive force (emf, \(\varepsilon\)) is the energy given to each coulomb of charge by the source:

\[\varepsilon=\dfrac{\Delta E}{Q}\]

The potential difference (\(V\)) across a component is the energy each coulomb loses as it passes through it. Both are measured in volts (J C⁻¹), but they describe opposite energy changes:

  • emf: chemical (or other) energy → electrical energy, in the source.
  • pd: electrical energy → heat, light or other energy, in the load.
1.1.2 · Internal resistance and terminal voltage

The chemicals inside a real battery resist the current. This internal resistance, \(r\), is drawn as a resistor in series with an ideal emf, inside a dashed box. Some of the emf is "used up" pushing current through \(r\):

\[\varepsilon=V+ir\quad \Rightarrow\quad V=\varepsilon-ir\]

\(V\) is the terminal voltage, the pd across the battery's terminals and so across the external circuit. \(ir\) is often called the "lost volts": energy per coulomb turned into heat inside the battery.

  • No current (open circuit): \(V = \varepsilon\). A high-resistance voltmeter alone reads the emf.
  • More current (smaller load resistance): more lost volts, so lower terminal voltage.
  • Short circuit \((R \approx 0)\): \(i = \varepsilon /r\), the maximum possible current, and all the energy heats the battery.
1.1.3 · The \(V\text{–}i\) graph for a battery

Rearranging gives \(V = -ri + \varepsilon\), a straight line, like \(y = mx + c\).

Vary the load, record \(V\) and \(i\), and fit a straight line: the intercept gives \(\varepsilon\) and the gradient gives −\(r\). Try it in 1.2.

1.1.4 · Kirchhoff's junction rule (charge is conserved)

At any junction, the total current flowing in equals the total current flowing out. Charge cannot build up at a point in a wire, so every coulomb that arrives must leave.

Use signs, like one-dimensional vectors. Current isn't a true vector, but giving each current a sign based on its direction works the same way. Count currents flowing into the junction as positive and currents flowing out as negative. Then the rule becomes one tidy equation:

\[\Sigma i = 0\]

For example, if \(i_1\) flows into a junction and \(i_2\) and \(i_3\) flow out: \(i_1 - i_2 - i_3 = 0\).

If you guess a current's direction wrongly, the answer simply comes out negative. That still tells you the right size and the true direction.

1.1.5 · Kirchhoff's loop rule (energy is conserved)

A charge that goes all the way round a closed loop ends up back where it started, at the same potential. So the potential changes around any loop must add to zero:

\[\Sigma V = 0\]

Mark the + and − ends first. A cell's long plate is +. A resistor's + end is where the current goes in and its − end is where the current comes out, because current flows from higher to lower potential through a resistor. Then pick a direction to walk round the loop and add up each change:

  • Arrive at the − end and leave from the + end: the potential rises, so write it as positive (+ε or +iR).
  • Arrive at the + end and leave from the − end: the potential falls, so write it as negative (−ε or −iR).

For example, a loop containing a cell and two resistors, walked in the direction of the current: \(\varepsilon - i_1R_1 - i_3R_3 = 0\).

Which way should you walk? It doesn't matter. Walking the other way round the loop flips the sign of every term, which just multiplies the whole equation by −1. Since −0 = 0, you get the same answer. The same is true of \(\Sigma i = 0\): counting currents out as positive instead of in flips every sign but changes nothing. Pick a direction for each loop (clockwise is a good habit) and stay consistent.

Use \(\Sigma i = 0\) at the junctions to cut down the number of unknown currents, then write one \(\Sigma V = 0\) loop equation for each unknown left.

1.1.6 · How current really divides

Current does not "take the path of least resistance". Parallel branches share the same pd, so each branch carries \(i = V/R\). A low-resistance branch carries more current, but every branch carries some.

  • Series: \(R_{T} = R_{1} + R_{2}\). Same current through each; the pds add.
  • Parallel: \(1/R_{T} = 1/R_{1} + 1/R_{2}\). Same pd across each; the currents add.
  • Adding a branch in parallel lowers the total resistance, so the battery supplies more current, the lost volts rise and the terminal voltage falls.

Method for exam questions

  1. Draw a current arrow in each branch (any direction will do).
  2. Mark + and − on every component: the long plate of each cell is +; each resistor is + where your arrow goes in and − where it comes out.
  3. Junction X, \(\Sigma i = 0\) (in is +, out is −): \(i_1\) arrives, while \(i_2\) and \(i_3\) leave, so \(i_1 - i_2 - i_3 = 0\).
  4. Left loop, \(\Sigma V = 0\), walking clockwise from Y up through cell 1, across to X and down through \(R_3\): rise through the cell (− to +), fall through \(R_1\) and \(R_3\) (+ to −): \(\varepsilon_1 - i_1R_1 - i_3R_3 = 0\).
  5. Right loop, \(\Sigma V = 0\), also clockwise: from Y up through \(R_3\) (against \(i_3\), so − to +: a rise), across to the right, down through \(R_2\) (with \(i_2\), so + to −: a fall) and down through cell 2 (+ to −: a fall): \(i_3R_3 - i_2R_2 - \varepsilon_2 = 0\).
  6. The direction you walk doesn't matter. Going round the right loop anticlockwise instead gives \(\varepsilon_2 + i_2R_2 - i_3R_3 = 0\). That's the same equation with every sign flipped, which is the same as multiplying both sides by −1, and −0 = 0. The same goes for junctions: counting "out" as + instead of "in" just flips every sign in \(\Sigma i = 0\). Choose one direction and stick to it for each loop.
  7. Solve the three equations. A negative current means it really flows against your arrow, and the + and − on that resistor swap over. Here \(i_2\) flows into the + terminal of cell 2, so cell 2 is being charged.

QUESTION ONETorch battery · terminal voltage

A battery of emf 9.0 V and internal resistance 1.5 Ω is connected to a 6.0 Ω lamp.

(a) Calculate the current and the terminal voltage. Achievement

(b) Calculate the power wasted inside the battery. Merit

(c) A second identical lamp is connected in parallel with the first. Explain, with calculations, why each lamp is now dimmer than the single lamp was. Excellence

Show worked answer · Question One

(a) \(i = \varepsilon /(R + r) = 9.0/7.5 = 1.2 \,\mathrm{A};\qquad V = iR = 1.2 \times 6.0 = 7.2 \,\mathrm{V}\)

(b) \(P = i^{2}r = 1.2^{2} \times 1.5 = 2.2 \,\mathrm{W}\)

(c) Two 6.0 Ω lamps in parallel have a total resistance of 3.0 Ω, so the total resistance falls to 4.5 Ω and the current rises to \(9.0/4.5 = 2.0 \,\mathrm{A}\). The lost volts rise to \(2.0 \times 1.5 = 3.0 \,\mathrm{V}\), so the terminal voltage falls to \(6.0 \,\mathrm{V}\). Each lamp now has 6.0 V across it instead of 7.2 V, so its power \(V^{2}/R\) drops from 8.6 W to 6.0 W and it is dimmer. With an ideal battery (\(r\) = 0) the lamps would not dim.

QUESTION TWOMeasuring \(\varepsilon\) and \(r\)

A student varies the load on a battery. With a current of 0.40 A the terminal voltage is 5.6 V; with 1.20 A it is 4.4 V.

(a) Calculate the internal resistance of the battery. Merit

(b) Calculate the emf. Merit

(c) Calculate the short-circuit current and explain why short-circuiting a battery is dangerous. Excellence

Show worked answer · Question Two

(a) The gradient of \(V\) against \(i\) is −\(r\): \(r = (5.6 - 4.4)/(1.20 - 0.40) = 1.5 \,\mathrm{\Omega}\)

(b) \(\varepsilon = V + ir = 5.6 + 0.40 \times 1.5 = 6.2 \,\mathrm{V}\)

(c) \(i = \varepsilon /r = 6.2/1.5 = 4.1 \,\mathrm{A}\). With no external resistance, all the energy supplied is converted to heat in the internal resistance (\(P = i^{2}r \approx 26 \,\mathrm{W}\) in a small battery). The battery heats rapidly and can leak, swell or catch fire.

QUESTION THREEKirchhoff · two cells

Two cells and three resistors form three branches between junctions X and Y, as in lab 1.3. Branch 1: a 12 V cell in series with 4.0 Ω. Branch 2: a 6.0 V cell in series with 2.0 Ω. Branch 3: a 6.0 Ω resistor. Both cells have their positive terminal towards X. Ignore internal resistance.

(a) Write the junction equation and two loop equations. Merit

(b) Calculate the current in each branch. Excellence

(c) Explain what is happening to the 6.0 V cell. Excellence

Show worked answer · Question Three

(a) Take \(i_{1}\) upwards through the 12 V cell (into X), and \(i_{2}\) and \(i_{3}\) downwards through the 2.0 Ω and 6.0 Ω branches (out of X). Each resistor is + where its current enters.

Junction X, \(\Sigma i = 0\) (in +, out −): \(i_{1} - i_{2} - i_{3} = 0\)

Left loop, \(\Sigma V = 0\) (up through the 12 V cell, − to +, then through 4.0 Ω and 6.0 Ω, + to −): \(12 - 4.0\,i_{1} - 6.0\,i_{3} = 0\)

Right loop, \(\Sigma V = 0\), also walked clockwise (up through the 6.0 Ω against \(i_3\): a rise; down through the 2.0 Ω with \(i_2\): a fall; then down through the 6.0 V cell from + to −: a fall): \(6.0\,i_{3} - 2.0\,i_{2} - 6.0 = 0\)

(b) Let \(V\) be the potential of X above Y. Then \(i_{1} = (12 - V)/4.0\), \(i_{2} = (V - 6.0)/2.0\) and \(i_{3} = V/6.0\). Substituting into the junction rule: \((12 - V)/4 - (V - 6)/2 - V/6 = 0\), which gives \(V = 6.55 \,\mathrm{V}\). So \(i_{1} = 1.36\) A, \(i_{2} = 0.27\) A, \(i_{3} = 1.09\) A, all positive, so every arrow was the right way round. Check \(\Sigma i = 0\) at X: \(1.36 - 0.27 - 1.09 = 0\) ✓.

(c) \(i_{2}\) flows downwards, so 0.27 A flows into the positive terminal of the 6.0 V cell, against its emf. The 12 V cell is forcing current backwards through it, so the 6.0 V cell is being charged: electrical energy is converted into chemical energy in it (if it is rechargeable).

1.6 · Applications of DC circuits

Internal resistance and Kirchhoff's laws in everyday technology.

Where do we use these ideas?

Internal resistance explains why batteries sag, heat up and wear out. Kirchhoff's rules underlie every circuit, from house wiring to battery packs.

Starting a carLost volts

The starter motor draws a very large current, over 100 A, from the 12 V battery. If the headlights are on, they dim while the engine cranks.

Huge current → large lost volts \(ir\) → terminal voltage drops → lights get less voltage

Exam angle: car batteries are built with a very small \(r\) so they can deliver big currents. On a cold morning \(r\) rises, which is why flat batteries show up in winter.

Battery health testers\(V\text{–}i\) graph

A battery tester puts a known load on the battery and measures how far the terminal voltage drops. An old battery can still read its full emf with no load.

Battery ages → internal resistance increases → bigger voltage drop under load → tester shows "replace"

Exam angle: an open-circuit voltmeter reading shows \(\varepsilon\), not \(r\). You need a current flowing to reveal \(r\).

Fast charging and heat\(i^{2}r\)

Phones and power banks warm up during fast charging and heavy use.

Large current through internal resistance → power wasted as heat \(P = i^{2}r\) → battery temperature rises

Exam angle: doubling the current makes four times the heat. Chargers slow down near full charge partly to limit this.

Battery packsSeries & parallel

Power tools, e-bikes and electric cars use packs of many small cells. Cells in series add their emfs for a higher voltage; parallel groups share the current.

Series: emfs add, and so do internal resistances. Parallel: same emf, but the current is shared so each cell's lost volts are smaller.

Exam angle: Kirchhoff's loop rule explains why mismatched cells in parallel push current into each other.

Jump-startingKirchhoff

Jumper leads connect a good battery in parallel with a flat one: positive to positive, negative to negative.

Good battery's emf is larger → net current flows into the flat battery → it charges, and helps power the starter

Exam angle: exactly the two-cell circuit in 1.3. Connecting the leads the wrong way puts the emfs in series around a near-zero resistance loop, giving a huge, dangerous current.

House wiringJunction rule

Appliances in a house are wired in parallel, so each gets the full mains voltage and can be switched on independently.

Each appliance switched on → another parallel branch → total current from the switchboard rises → a circuit breaker trips if the total gets too high

Exam angle: the current in the supply cable is the sum of all the branch currents (junction rule).

EXPLAIN ITDimming headlights · Merit / Excellence-style

A car's headlights dim noticeably while the starter motor is turning the engine. Explain why, using the idea of internal resistance.

Show model answer

The headlights and starter motor are connected in parallel across the battery. When the starter motor runs, it adds a very low-resistance branch, so the total external resistance drops and the current drawn from the battery becomes very large. The battery has internal resistance \(r\), so the lost volts \(ir\) become large. Since the terminal voltage is \(V = \varepsilon - ir\), the terminal voltage falls, and the headlights, connected across the terminals, receive less voltage. Their power \((V^{2}/R)\) drops, so they dim. When the starter stops, the current falls, \(ir\) falls, and the lights brighten again.

02 · Capacitors

Storing charge and energy in an electric field, and the exponential curves of charging and discharging.

Capacitors in DC circuits

Six ideas. Compare 2.1.5 with inductors (4.1.2): the curves look alike, but the physics and the quantity that's delayed are different.

2.1.1 · Capacitance: \(Q = CV\)

A capacitor is two conducting plates separated by an insulator. Connected to a battery, electrons are pushed onto one plate and pulled off the other, so the plates carry equal and opposite charges, +\(Q\) and −\(Q\). No charge crosses the gap.

\[Q=CV\]

Capacitance (\(C\)) is the charge stored per volt across the plates. Unit: farad (F) = C V⁻¹. Practical capacitors are usually µF (10⁻⁶), nF (10⁻⁹) or pF (10⁻¹²). A graph of \(V\) against \(Q\) is a straight line through the origin with gradient 1/\(C\).

2.1.2 · Parallel-plate capacitors
\[C=\dfrac{{\varepsilon}_0{\varepsilon}_rA}{d}\]
  • Bigger plate area A: more room for charge, so a bigger \(C\).
  • Smaller gap \(d\): the opposite charges attract each other more strongly, so more charge is held for the same voltage, giving a bigger \(C\).
  • \(\varepsilon _{0} = 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \,\mathrm{F} \,\mathrm{m}^{-1}\) is the permittivity of free space. \(\varepsilon _{r}\) is the dielectric constant of the material between the plates.

Between the plates the electric field is uniform, with strength \(E = V/d\) (Level 2 background).

2.1.3 · Dielectrics

A dielectric is an insulator placed between the plates. The field polarises its molecules: their electrons shift slightly towards the + plate. This creates a small opposing field inside the dielectric, so the overall field and the voltage for a given charge are smaller.

Dielectric polarised→opposing field→lower \(V\) for the same \(Q\)→\(C = Q/V\) is larger, by a factor of \(\varepsilon _{r}\)

Dielectrics also keep the plates apart and can withstand higher voltages than air before breaking down.

2.1.4 · Energy stored: \(\tfrac{1}{2}QV\)
\[E=\dfrac{1}{2}QV=\dfrac{1}{2}CV^2\]

The energy stored is the area under the \(V\text{–}Q\) graph. The first charges move onto the plates easily (\(V\) is small); later charges have to be pushed against a larger voltage. On average each coulomb is moved through \(V\)/2, so \(E = Q \times V/2\).

The battery supplies energy QV, but only half ends up stored. The other half is converted to heat in the resistance of the circuit while charging, whatever the value of \(R\).

2.1.5 · Charging and discharging: \(\tau = RC\)
Switch closed→charge flows onto plates→\(V_{C}\) rises→\(V_{R} = \varepsilon - V_{C}\) falls→\(i = V_{R}/R\) falls→charging slows
  • Charging: current starts at its maximum, \(\varepsilon /R\), and decays to zero; \(V_{C}\) rises from 0 to \(\varepsilon\). After \(\tau\), \(V_{C} = 63\%\) of \(\varepsilon\).
  • Discharging: \(V_{C}\), \(Q\) and \(i\) all decay to zero, falling to 37% after \(\tau\). The current flows the opposite way to the charging current.
  • \(\tau = RC\). A bigger \(R\) slows the flow of charge; a bigger \(C\) needs more charge to reach the same voltage. Both make the circuit slower, the opposite of an inductor, where a bigger \(R\) makes it faster.

Charging: \(V_{C}\) grows while \(i\) decays. For an inductor (4.1.2) the roles are swapped: current grows while the inductor's voltage decays.

2.1.6 · Capacitors in series and parallel
  • Parallel: \(C_{T} = C_{1} + C_{2}\). Same voltage across each; like making the plate area bigger. Charges add.
  • Series: \(1/C_{T} = 1/C_{1} + 1/C_{2}\). Same charge on each; like making the gap bigger. Voltages add, and the smaller capacitor gets the larger share \((V = Q/C)\).

These are the opposite way round to the rules for resistors.

2.7 · Applications of capacitors

Quick bursts of energy, timing, smoothing and sensing.

Where do we use capacitors?

Capacitors can release their energy far faster than a battery, their RC time constant makes a built-in timer, and changing their plates or dielectric makes them sensors.

Camera flashEnergy \(\tfrac{1}{2}CV^{2}\)

A battery slowly charges a capacitor to a few hundred volts. Pressing the shutter discharges it through the flash tube in about a millisecond.

Slow charge (large \(R\), long \(\tau\)) → energy stored → very fast discharge (tiny \(R\), tiny \(\tau\)) → high power flash

Exam angle: the same energy released in a much shorter time means a much larger power. The "whine" you hear is the charging circuit.

DefibrillatorsEnergy \(\tfrac{1}{2}CV^{2}\)

An AED charges a capacitor to store around 150–200 J, then delivers it through the patient's chest in a few milliseconds to restart a normal heart rhythm.

Charge for several seconds → large \(\tfrac{1}{2}CV^{2}\) → rapid discharge through the body's resistance

Exam angle: the discharge time depends on \(\tau = RC\), with \(R\) the resistance of the patient's chest.

TouchscreensChanging \(C\)

Phone screens have a grid of transparent electrodes forming tiny capacitors. A finger is a conductor and changes the capacitance where it touches.

Finger near electrodes → capacitance changes → charging time changes → chip locates the touch

Exam angle: gloves are insulators, so they don't change \(C\). That's why touchscreens ignore them.

Timing circuits\(\tau = RC\)

Flashing indicators, delay timers for hallway lights and many electronic oscillators use an RC circuit as a clock.

Capacitor charges through \(R\) → reaches a trigger voltage after a time set by RC → circuit switches → repeat

Exam angle: change \(R\) or \(C\) to change the delay. A bigger RC gives a slower flash rate.

Smoothing power suppliesStores charge

Chargers convert AC to a bumpy DC. A large capacitor across the output fills the gaps between the bumps.

Voltage peaks → capacitor charges → voltage dips → capacitor discharges into the load → a much steadier output

Exam angle: a larger \(C\) or a smaller load current (larger \(R\)) means a longer \(\tau\), so less drop between peaks.

Condenser microphones\(C = \varepsilon _{0}\)A/\(d\)

A thin, flexible plate sits very close to a fixed plate, forming a capacitor with a voltage across it.

Sound moves the thin plate → gap \(d\) changes → \(C\) changes → charge flows on and off → electrical signal

Exam angle: a smaller gap gives a bigger capacitance, the parallel-plate formula in action.

SupercapacitorsFast charge

Supercapacitors have capacitances of thousands of farads. They are used to catch regenerative braking energy in buses and trams, and as backup power.

Huge effective area and tiny gap → enormous \(C\) → large energy that can go in and out very quickly

Exam angle: they store less energy than a battery of the same size, but can charge and discharge much faster and for many more cycles.

03 · Flux & Faraday

Where induced emfs come from, how big they are, and which way they push.

Magnetic flux and electromagnetic induction

Six ideas underpin the whole topic. Open each panel for the detail.

3.1.1 · Flux density and magnetic flux

Magnetic flux density \((B)\) is the strength of a magnetic field at a point. Unit: tesla (T). On a diagram it is shown by how closely the field lines are packed.

Magnetic flux \((\Phi )\) is the total amount of field passing through an area. Unit: weber (Wb), where \(1 \,\mathrm{Wb} = 1 \,\mathrm{T} \,\mathrm{m}^{2}\).

\[\Phi=BA\]

Here \(A\) is the area perpendicular to the field. Tilt a loop and fewer field lines pass through it; turn it edge-on and the flux is zero even though \(B\) is unchanged.

Same field, same loop: only the area facing the field counts.

3.1.2 · Ways to change the flux

An emf is induced only while the flux through a circuit is changing. Since \(\Phi = BA\), there are three ways to change it:

  • Change \(B\): move a magnet towards or away from a coil, or change the current in a nearby electromagnet.
  • Change A: slide a rod along rails so the circuit area grows, or pull a loop out of the field.
  • Change the angle: rotate a coil in a field, as in a generator.

For a coil of \(N\) turns, the same flux passes through every turn. The flux linkage is \(N\Phi\), so more turns give a bigger emf for the same change.

3.1.3 · Faraday's law: emf = rate of change of flux

The induced emf equals the rate of change of flux linkage:

\[\varepsilon=-N\dfrac{\Delta \Phi}{\Delta t}\]

So on a \(\Phi\)–\(t\) graph, the emf is minus the gradient (times \(N\)). A large flux that isn't changing gives zero emf; a small flux that changes quickly can give a large one.

A faster magnet, a stronger magnet, or more turns all give a bigger emf. A faster magnet does not change the total \(\Delta \Phi\), only how quickly it happens.

3.1.4 · Lenz's law: the induced current opposes the change

Lenz's law: the induced current flows in the direction that creates a magnetic field opposing the change in flux that produced it. This is the minus sign in Faraday's law.

  • Push a N pole towards a coil: the near face of the coil becomes a N pole, repelling the magnet.
  • Pull the N pole away: the near face becomes a S pole, attracting the magnet back.

Why it must be so (conservation of energy): if the induced current helped the change, the magnet would be pulled in faster, inducing more current, pulling harder… electrical energy would appear from nothing. Because the induced current opposes the motion, you must do work to move the magnet, and that work becomes the electrical energy in the circuit.

3.1.5 · Induction in the real world

Generators, regenerative braking, eddy-current brakes, guitar pickups, microphones and induction cooktops all run on these two laws. A magnet dropped down a copper pipe falls slowly for the same reason: eddy currents induced in the pipe oppose its motion.

3.1.6 · Eddy currents

Induction doesn't need a coil. When a block of metal moves through a non-uniform field, or sits in a changing field, emfs are induced inside the metal itself. These drive loops of current called eddy currents that circulate within the metal.

  • By Lenz's law, eddy currents oppose the motion that causes them. This is the basis of magnetic brakes, and why a magnet falls slowly down a copper pipe.
  • Eddy currents heat the metal \((i^{2}R)\). This is useful in induction cooktops but wasteful in transformer cores, which is why cores are laminated (see 5.1.5).
  • Cutting slots in the metal breaks up the current loops and reduces the effect.

04 · Inductors

A coil that induces an emf in itself, and the delays, energy and sparks that follow.

Inductors in DC circuits

Six ideas. The comparison with capacitors (4.1.6) is worth learning carefully: the 2025 examiners reported many candidates mixing the two up.

4.1.1 · Self-inductance and back emf

An inductor is a coil of wire, often wound on an iron core. When the current in it changes, its own magnetic flux changes, and by Faraday's law an emf is induced in the coil itself. By Lenz's law this back emf opposes the change in current.

\[\varepsilon=-L\dfrac{\Delta i}{\Delta t}\]

\(L\) is the self-inductance, in henry (H). A 1 H inductor produces 1 V of back emf when its current changes at 1 A s⁻¹. More turns, a larger area and an iron core all increase \(L\), because each gives more flux for the same current.

An inductor opposes changes in current, not current itself. With a steady current, an ideal inductor has no voltage across it.

4.1.2 · Switching on: the current grows gradually
Switch closed→current starts to rise→flux in coil increases→back emf induced→opposes the rise, so current grows slowly

At the instant of switching on, \(i = 0\) but it is changing fastest, so the back emf equals the supply emf and the voltage across the resistor is zero. As the current rises, it changes more slowly, so the back emf shrinks and more voltage appears across the resistor. Eventually the current is steady at \(i_{\text{max}} = \varepsilon / R_{\text{total}}\).

Switching on: current grows (left) while the voltage across an ideal inductor decays (right).

4.1.3 · The time constant \(\tau = L/R\)
\[\tau=\dfrac{L}{R_{\text{total}}}\]

In one time constant the current rises to 63% of its maximum (or falls to 37% when decaying). After about \(5\tau\) it is effectively steady.

  • Bigger \(L\) → longer \(\tau\): a larger back emf for the same \(\Delta i/\Delta t\), so the current changes more slowly.
  • Bigger \(R\) → shorter \(\tau\): the final current is smaller, so it is reached sooner. \(R\) includes the inductor's internal resistance.

Note the contrast with capacitors, where \(\tau = RC\) and a bigger \(R\) makes the circuit slower.

4.1.4 · Energy stored and real inductors
\[E=\dfrac{1}{2}Li^2\]

While the current builds up, the supply does work against the back emf. That energy is stored in the inductor's magnetic field, and it is released when the current falls.

A real inductor is long, thin wire, so it has internal resistance \(r\). It is drawn as an ideal inductor in series with \(r\). Its terminal voltage is the back emf plus \(ir\), so once the current is steady the voltage across it is \(ir\), not zero.

4.1.5 · Switching off: large back emfs and sparks

If the switch is simply opened, the current tries to fall to zero almost instantly. \(\Delta i/\Delta t\) is enormous, so the back emf is enormous, often hundreds or thousands of volts. It can make a spark jump the switch gap or light a neon lamp connected across the coil. The spark is where the stored energy \(\tfrac{1}{2}Li^{2}\) goes.

If there is a closed path (for example a two-way switch that removes the battery but keeps the coil connected to a resistor), the current decays exponentially, \(i = i_{0}e^{-t/\tau}\). The inductor's voltage reverses polarity: it now acts like a source, trying to keep the current flowing in its original direction.

(c) At \(t = 0\), \(i = 0\), so there is no voltage across either resistance; the whole 12.0 V is back emf: \(\Delta i/\Delta t = \varepsilon /L = 12.0 / 2.4 = 5.0 \,\mathrm{A} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\).

(\(d\)) Steady: no back emf, so \(V = ir = 0.50 \times 6.0 = 3.0 \,\mathrm{V}\). Energy \(E = \tfrac{1}{2}Li^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 2.4 \times 0.50^{2} = 0.30 \,\mathrm{J}\).

QUESTION THREELamp and spark · explanation

A lamp is connected in series with a large iron-cored inductor, a switch and a battery. When the switch is closed, the lamp takes about a second to reach full brightness. When the switch is opened, a small spark is seen between the switch contacts.

(b) Explain why a spark is produced when the switch is opened, and where its energy comes from. Excellence

Show worked answer · Question Three

(a) When the switch closes, the current starts to increase, so the flux in the inductor increases. By Faraday's law this changing flux induces a back emf in the coil, and by Lenz's law it opposes the increase in current. The current therefore rises gradually (exponentially) and the lamp brightens gradually. As the current approaches its maximum, \(\Delta i/\Delta t\) falls, the back emf falls, and the lamp reaches full brightness.

(b) Opening the switch makes the current fall to zero in a very short time, so \(\Delta i/\Delta t\) is very large. The flux collapses rapidly, inducing a very large back emf \((\varepsilon = -L\Delta i/\Delta t)\) which opposes the fall in current. This voltage is large enough to ionise the air between the switch contacts, so a spark jumps the gap. The energy comes from the energy stored in the inductor's magnetic field, \(\tfrac{1}{2}Li^{2}\), which is released as the field collapses.

05 · Transformers

Two coils, one changing flux: how voltages are stepped up and down.

How a transformer works

Five ideas, from the induction chain to why the national grid runs at hundreds of kilovolts.

5.1.1 · The induction chain
AC in primary coil→changing magnetic field→changing flux in the iron core→same changing flux passes through secondary→alternating emf induced in secondary (Faraday)

The two coils are not connected electrically. Energy is carried from one to the other by the changing magnetic flux in the core.

5.1.2 · The turns ratio
\[\dfrac{V_s}{V_p}=\dfrac{N_s}{N_p}\]

The same changing flux passes through every turn of both coils, so each turn has the same emf induced in it. A coil with more turns therefore has a larger total emf.

  • Step-up: \(N_{s}\) > \(N_{p}\), so \(V_{s}\) > \(V_{p}\) (power stations to the grid).
  • Step-down: \(N_{s}\) < \(N_{p}\), so \(V_{s}\) < \(V_{p}\) (substations, phone chargers).
5.1.3 · Ideal transformers: power in = power out

An ideal transformer wastes no energy, so

\[V_pi_p=V_si_s\quad \Rightarrow\quad \dfrac{i_s}{i_p}=\dfrac{N_p}{N_s}\]

Stepping the voltage up steps the current down by the same factor. A transformer cannot create energy. The load on the secondary sets the secondary current, and that sets how much current the primary draws.

5.1.4 · Why transformers need AC

With a steady DC supply, the primary current is constant, so the flux in the core is constant. With no change in flux, no emf is induced in the secondary. There is only a brief pulse when the DC is switched on or off, while the current (and flux) is changing.

AC changes direction continuously, so the flux changes continuously and the secondary has an alternating emf at the same frequency as the supply.

5.1.5 · Energy losses and design
LossCauseDesign fix
Resistance of windings\(i^{2}R\) heating in the copper wireThick copper wire, especially on the high-current side
Eddy currentsThe changing flux induces circulating currents in the iron core itself, which heat itLaminated core: thin insulated sheets break up the eddy current paths

Well-designed large transformers are more than 99% efficient.

QUESTION TWODC and the core · explanation

(a) A student connects the charger transformer's primary to a 12 V car battery. Explain why there is no steady output from the secondary. Merit

(b) Explain why the core is made of thin, insulated layers of soft iron rather than a solid block. Excellence

Show worked answer · Question Two

(a) A steady DC current in the primary produces a constant magnetic field, so the flux in the core and through the secondary is constant. (b) The changing flux also passes through the iron core itself, which is a conductor, so emfs are induced in the core and drive eddy currents around it. These heat the core and waste energy. 3.7 · Applications of induction

Where Faraday's and Lenz's laws are working around you.

Where do we use induction?

Each device below uses a changing flux to induce an emf. Read the chain, then try the "Explain it" question at the bottom. Exam questions are often set in contexts like these, with the information you need supplied.

Power station generatorsFaraday

Most of New Zealand's electricity comes from hydro, geothermal and wind stations. In each, a turbine spins a large electromagnet inside fixed coils.

Turbine turns magnet → flux through the coils keeps changing → alternating emf induced

Exam angle: faster rotation gives a bigger \(\Delta \Phi /\Delta t\), so a bigger emf and a higher frequency.

Regenerative brakingLenz

When an electric car or e-bike slows down, its motor is switched to work as a generator, charging the battery.

Wheels turn the motor → induced current → by Lenz, its force opposes the rotation → car slows and kinetic energy becomes electrical energy

Exam angle: the braking force exists because energy is being taken out: conservation of energy.

Eddy-current brakesLenz

Roller coasters, drop-tower rides and some trains slow down by passing metal fins between strong magnets. There is no contact, so nothing wears out.

Fin moves through field → changing flux in the metal → eddy currents → their field opposes the motion

Exam angle: the faster the fin moves, the bigger the braking force, so the ride slows smoothly. The same effect slows a magnet falling down a copper pipe.

Electric guitar pickupsFaraday

A small magnet wrapped in thousands of turns of fine wire sits under each steel string. The magnet magnetises the string.

String vibrates → flux through the coil changes at the string's frequency → emf with the same frequency → amplifier

Exam angle: many turns (large \(N\)) make a usable emf from a tiny change in flux. Nylon strings don't work.

Dynamic microphonesFaraday

Sound waves push a thin diaphragm attached to a light coil that sits around a magnet.

Diaphragm and coil move → coil cuts through the magnet's flux → emf that copies the sound wave

Exam angle: a loudspeaker is the same device run backwards: a current in the coil makes it move.

Induction cooktopsFaraday + eddy currents

A coil under the glass carries a high-frequency alternating current. The pan, not the glass, gets hot. Pans must be magnetic (steel or cast iron).

Rapidly changing current → rapidly changing flux through the pan base → large eddy currents → heating

Exam angle: a high frequency means a large \(\Delta \Phi /\Delta t\), so large induced currents.

EXPLAIN ITRegenerative braking · Excellence-style

An electric car going downhill switches its motor to generator mode. Explain why this slows the car, and why more braking happens at higher speed.

Show model answer

The wheels turn the coils of the motor in its magnetic field, so the flux through the coils changes and an emf is induced (Faraday). This drives a current into the battery. By Lenz's law, the induced current produces a magnetic force that opposes the change causing it, so it opposes the rotation and slows the car. This must happen by conservation of energy, because the electrical energy stored in the battery comes from the car's kinetic energy. At higher speed the flux changes faster, so the induced emf and current are larger and the opposing force is larger.

4.6 · Applications of inductors

Using back emf, delays and stored magnetic energy.

Where do we use inductors?

Some devices use the large back emf when the current is switched off, some use the stored energy \(\tfrac{1}{2}Li^{2}\), and some use the way an inductor slows changes in current.

Car ignition coilsBack emf

A petrol engine needs a spark of tens of thousands of volts from a 12 V battery. The ignition coil has a primary winding on an iron core, wound together with a secondary of many more turns.

Primary current switched off suddenly → flux collapses very fast → large back emf in the primary, stepped up further by the secondary → spark at the plug

Exam angle: the faster the current is cut, the larger \(\Delta i/\Delta t\) and the larger the voltage. Compare 4.3.

Protecting electronicsBack emf

Relays, solenoid valves and motors are coils. Switching them off with a transistor could destroy it, so a diode is fitted across the coil.

Switch opens → back emf tries to keep the current flowing → current circulates through the diode → voltage limited to about 0.7 V

Exam angle: the stored energy \(\tfrac{1}{2}Li^{2}\) still has to go somewhere. With a diode, it becomes heat in the coil's resistance over a time of about \(L\)/\(r\).

Traffic light sensorsInductance

The rectangular cuts you see in the road at intersections hold loops of wire. A detector feeds a changing current through the loop.

Car's metal body stops over the loop → its eddy currents change the loop's flux → loop's inductance changes → detector senses a car is waiting

Exam angle: inductance depends on what is in or near the coil, just as an iron core increases \(L\).

Boost convertersEnergy \(\tfrac{1}{2}Li^{2}\)

LED torches running off one battery, solar chargers and electric cars all use circuits that step DC voltage up. They rely on an inductor switched on and off thousands of times a second.

Switch on: current grows, energy stored in the field → switch off: back emf adds to the supply → energy delivered at a higher voltage

Exam angle: the inductor stores energy as current flows and gives it back when the current falls.

Smoothing chokesOpposes \(\Delta i\)

Inductors called "chokes" are placed in power supplies and on cables (the lump on some laptop chargers' cables is a related idea) to stop sudden changes and spikes in current.

Current tries to change quickly → back emf opposes the change → current changes only slowly

Exam angle: an inductor opposes changes in current, unlike a capacitor, which opposes changes in voltage.

MRI scannersEnergy \(\tfrac{1}{2}Li^{2}\)

The magnet in a hospital MRI scanner is a huge superconducting coil carrying hundreds of amps. Once the current is set up, it keeps flowing with almost no power input.

Large \(L\) and large \(i\) → enormous energy stored in the magnetic field (megajoules)

Exam angle: with zero resistance, \(\tau = L/R\) is effectively infinite, so the current never decays.

EXPLAIN ITIgnition coil · Excellence-style

Explain how a car's ignition coil can produce a very high voltage from a 12 V battery when the primary current is switched off.

Show model answer

While the switch is closed, a steady current in the primary creates a strong magnetic field in the iron core, storing energy \(\tfrac{1}{2}Li^{2}\). When the switch opens, the current falls to zero in a very short time, so the flux in the core collapses very quickly. By Faraday's law the induced emf depends on the rate of change of flux, \(\Delta \Phi /\Delta t\), so a very large back emf is induced (\(\varepsilon = -L\Delta i/\Delta t\) with a very small \(\Delta t\)). The secondary has many more turns than the primary, and the same collapsing flux links all of them, so the emf is multiplied up to tens of kilovolts. That is enough to make a spark jump the plug gap, and the stored magnetic energy supplies the spark.

5.6 · Applications of transformers

From the national grid to the charger in your bag.

Where do we use transformers?

Every device here relies on a changing current in one coil inducing an emf in another. The turns ratio sets the voltage, and power in ≈ power out sets the current.

The national gridStep-up & step-down

Generators produce electricity at around 11 kV. Transformers at the power station step this up to 220 kV for the main transmission lines. Substations step it down to 33 kV and 11 kV, and the transformer on your street steps it down to 230 V for homes.

High voltage → low current → small \(i^{2}R\) losses in the lines → step down near users for safety

Exam angle: see 5.3. Raising \(V\) by a factor \(k\) cuts the loss by \(k^{2}\).

Phone and laptop chargersStep-down

A charger turns 230 V mains into about 5–20 V. Modern chargers first convert the mains to a very high frequency (tens of kilohertz), so a tiny transformer will do the job.

Higher frequency → faster-changing flux → enough emf per turn with a much smaller core

Exam angle: the output is then rectified to DC. The transformer itself only works on AC.

Electric fence energisersStep-up

On farms across New Zealand, energisers send short pulses of several thousand volts along fence wires. A capacitor dumps its charge through the primary of a step-up transformer about once a second.

Sudden pulse of current in the primary → rapidly changing flux → large emf in the many-turn secondary → high-voltage pulse on the fence

Exam angle: a pulse works because the current is changing. Steady DC would give no output.

Wireless chargingCoupled coils

Phone charging pads and electric toothbrushes have a coil in the base and a coil in the device, with no metal contacts.

AC in the pad's coil → changing flux passes into the phone's coil → emf induced → rectified to charge the battery

Exam angle: with no shared iron core, much of the flux leaks away. The phone has to sit close and centred, and efficiency is lower than a normal transformer.

Tap-to-pay cardsCoupled coils

A contactless bank card or school ID card has no battery. A coil of wire runs around the edge of the card.

Reader's coil makes an alternating field → changing flux through the card's coil → emf powers the chip → chip sends its data back

Exam angle: it only works within a few centimetres, because the field and the flux linkage fall off quickly with distance.

Welding transformersStep-down, high current

An arc welder steps mains voltage down to a low voltage so it can deliver a current of hundreds of amps, enough to melt steel.

Fewer secondary turns → lower \(V_{s}\) → (power in ≈ power out) much larger \(i_{s}\)

Exam angle: the secondary is wound with thick wire to keep \(i^{2}R\) heating in the winding down.

Microwave ovensStep-up

A microwave's magnetron needs around 2000 V. A heavy transformer inside the oven steps 230 V up to this.

Many more secondary turns → \(V_{s}\) almost ten times \(V_{p}\) → much smaller secondary current

Exam angle: this is why the inside of a microwave is dangerous even when unplugged: the high voltage is stored in a capacitor.

Bathroom shaver socketsIsolation (1 : 1)

The shaver socket in a bathroom contains an isolating transformer. Its output is not connected to earth or to the mains wiring.

Energy crosses only through the changing flux → no direct path from mains to the user through earth → safer near water

Exam angle: a transformer doesn't have to change the voltage to be useful. The coils are not electrically connected.

EXPLAIN ITWireless charging · Merit / Excellence-style

A phone on a wireless charging pad charges more slowly when it is placed off-centre. Explain how the pad charges the phone, and why position matters.

Show model answer

An alternating current in the pad's coil produces a magnetic field that keeps changing, so the flux through the phone's coil keeps changing. By Faraday's law, this changing flux induces an alternating emf in the phone's coil. That emf is rectified and used to charge the battery. There is no iron core joining the coils, so only part of the pad's flux passes through the phone's coil. When the phone is off-centre or further away, even less of the flux links the phone's coil, so \(\Delta \Phi /\Delta t\) through it is smaller. The induced emf and the power delivered are therefore smaller, and charging is slower.

06 · AC circuits

rms values, phase, reactance, impedance and resonance.

Alternating current circuits

Seven ideas. Watch for one point the 2025 examiners raised: peak voltage is \(\sqrt{2} \times V_{\text{rms}}\), not \(\sqrt{2V_{\text{rms}}}\).

6.1.1 · Describing AC

An alternating voltage varies sinusoidally: \(V = V_{\text{max}} \sin \omega t\), and similarly for current.

  • Frequency \(f\): cycles per second (Hz). NZ mains is 50 Hz.
  • Angular frequency \(\omega = 2\pi f\) (rad s⁻¹), as in circular motion and SHM. One cycle is \(2\pi\) rad.
  • Peak value \(V_{\text{max}}\) or \(i_{\text{max}}\): the amplitude of the waveform.
6.1.2 · rms values: the heating effect

The power in a resistor, \(P = i^{2}R\), is always positive but varies between 0 and its peak value twice each cycle. The average power is half the peak power.

The rms (root mean square) current is the steady DC current that would produce the same heating in the same resistor:

\[i_{\text{rms}}=\dfrac{i_{\text{max}}}{\sqrt{2}}\quad V_{\text{rms}}=\dfrac{V_{\text{max}}}{\sqrt{2}}\]

NZ mains is 230 V rms, so the peak is √2 × 230 = 325 V. Meter readings and appliance labels are rms values unless stated otherwise, so \(P = V_{\text{rms}}i_{\text{rms}}\) gives the average power.

6.1.3 · Resistors: in phase

For a resistor, \(V = iR\) at every instant, so voltage and current peak at the same time: they are in phase. Resistance does not depend on frequency.

6.1.4 · Capacitors: current leads voltage

Current flows onto a capacitor fastest when its voltage is changing fastest (as it passes zero), and the current is zero when the voltage is at its peak and momentarily not changing. So the current leads the voltage by ¼ cycle (90°).

\[X_C=\dfrac{1}{\omega C}\]

Capacitive reactance \(X_{C}\) (Ω) is the capacitor's opposition to AC. At high frequency the capacitor charges and discharges many times a second, so a large current flows: \(X_{C}\) is small. For DC (\(f\) = 0), \(X_{C}\) is infinite and no steady current flows.

6.1.5 · Inductors: voltage leads current

An inductor's back emf is greatest when the current is changing fastest (as it passes zero). So the voltage leads the current by ¼ cycle (90°).

\[X_L=\omega L\]

Inductive reactance \(X_{L}\) rises with frequency: faster changes in current mean a bigger back emf. For DC, \(X_{L} = 0\) and only the coil's resistance remains.

Memory aid, "CIVIL": in a Capacitor, I (the current, \(i\)) leads V; V leads I in an inductor (L). Reactances don't dissipate energy on average: the energy stored each quarter cycle is returned in the next.

6.1.6 · Phasors and impedance

A phasor is a rotating arrow whose length is a peak (or rms) value and whose angle shows its phase. In a series circuit the current is the same everywhere, so draw the current along the reference axis. Then:

  • \(V_{R}\) points along the current (in phase).
  • \(V_{L}\) points 90° ahead (up).
  • \(V_{C}\) points 90° behind (down).

The supply voltage is the vector sum, not the ordinary sum. Dividing each voltage by \(i\) gives the impedance triangle:

\[Z=\sqrt{R^2+{(X_L-X_C)}^2}\quad V=iZ\quad \tan \varphi=\dfrac{X_L-X_C}{R}\]

Impedance \(Z\) (Ω) is the total opposition to AC. \(\varphi\) is the phase angle between the supply voltage and the current.

6.1.7 · Resonance in LCR circuits

As frequency increases, \(X_{L}\) rises and \(X_{C}\) falls. At one frequency they are equal and cancel:

\[\omega L=\dfrac{1}{\omega C}\quad \Rightarrow\quad f_0=\dfrac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{LC}}\]

At the resonant frequency: \(Z = R\) (its minimum), so the current is a maximum; the supply voltage and current are in phase; and \(V_{L}\) and \(V_{C}\) are equal and opposite. Each can be much larger than the supply voltage.

QUESTION TWOReactance and frequency

A 4.7 µF capacitor and, separately, a 0.15 H inductor (negligible resistance) are each connected to a 12 V rms supply.

(a) Calculate the reactance of each at 50 Hz. Achievement

(b) Calculate the rms current in the capacitor at 50 Hz and at 500 Hz. Merit

(c) Explain why the current in the capacitor increases with frequency but the current in the inductor decreases. Excellence

Show worked answer · Question Two

(a) \(\omega = 2\pi \times 50 = 314 \,\mathrm{rad} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\). \(X_{C} = 1/(314 \times 4.7 \times 10^{-6}) = 677 \,\mathrm{\Omega}\); \(X_{L} = 314 \times 0.15 = 47 \,\mathrm{\Omega}\)

(b) At 50 Hz: \(i = 12/677 = 0.018 \,\mathrm{A}\). At 500 Hz, \(X_{C}\) is ten times smaller (67.7 Ω), so \(i = 0.18 \,\mathrm{A}\).

(c) At a higher frequency the capacitor is charged and discharged more times per second, so more charge flows each second for the same voltage: a larger current and smaller reactance \((X_{C} = 1/\omega C)\). In the inductor, a higher frequency means the current changes more rapidly, so the back emf \((\varepsilon = -L\Delta i/\Delta t)\) is larger and opposes the current more strongly: a larger reactance \((X_{L} = \omega L)\) and a smaller current.

QUESTION THREELCR resonance

A series circuit contains a 12 Ω resistor, a 50 mH inductor and a 20 µF capacitor, connected to a 6.0 V rms variable-frequency supply.

(a) Calculate the resonant frequency. Merit

(b) Calculate the current at resonance and the voltage across the inductor at resonance. Merit

(c) Explain why the voltage across the inductor can be larger than the supply voltage, and why the current is a maximum at resonance. Excellence

Show worked answer · Question Three

(a) \(f_{0} = 1/(2\pi \sqrt{0.050 \times 20 \times 10^{-6}}) = 1/(2\pi \times 1.0 \times 10^{-3}) = 159 \,\mathrm{Hz}\)

(b) At resonance \(Z = R\), so \(i = 6.0/12 = 0.50 \,\mathrm{A}\). \(X_{L} = 2\pi \times 159 \times 0.050 = 50 \,\mathrm{\Omega}\), so \(V_{L} = 0.50 \times 50 = 25 \,\mathrm{V}\).

(c) \(V_{L}\) and \(V_{C}\) are 180° out of phase (\(V_{L}\) leads the current by 90°, \(V_{C}\) lags by 90°). At resonance \(X_{L} = X_{C}\), so \(V_{L} = V_{C}\) and they cancel in the phasor sum. The supply only has to provide \(V_{R}\), so each of \(V_{L}\) and \(V_{C}\) can be much larger than the supply. Because the reactances cancel, the impedance \(Z = \sqrt{R^{2} + (X_{L} - X_{C})^{2}}\) is at its minimum value, \(R\), so the current \(i = V/Z\) is a maximum.

6.7 · Applications of AC circuits

Tuning, filtering and the electricity in your walls.

Where do we use AC circuit ideas?

Because reactance depends on frequency, capacitors and inductors can sort signals by frequency. Resonance picks out one frequency from many.

Radio tuningResonance

An aerial picks up signals from every station at once. Turning the tuning dial changes a capacitor in an LC circuit.

Changing \(C\) → changes \(f_{0}\) → only the station at \(f_{0}\) gives a large current → that station is selected

Exam angle: a low resistance gives a sharp peak, so neighbouring stations are rejected.

Speaker crossovers\(X_{L}\), \(X_{C}\) vs \(f\)

A speaker box has a small tweeter for high notes and a large woofer for low notes. A crossover circuit sends each the right frequencies.

Capacitor in series with tweeter → high \(X_{C}\) blocks bass. Inductor in series with woofer → high \(X_{L}\) blocks treble.

Exam angle: \(X_{C} = 1/\omega C\) falls with frequency; \(X_{L} = \omega L\) rises.

Mains electricityrms

New Zealand homes get 230 V rms at 50 Hz. Appliance power ratings and meter readings use rms values.

230 V rms → peak 325 V → insulation must withstand the peak, but heating depends on rms

Exam angle: \(V_{\text{max}} = \sqrt{2} \times V_{\text{rms}}\), not \(\sqrt{2V_{\text{rms}}}\).

Wireless chargingResonance

Many charging pads tune the transmitting and receiving coils with capacitors so that both resonate at the same frequency.

Both LC circuits share the same \(f_{0}\) → large currents at that frequency → more energy transferred across the gap

Exam angle: links transformers (5.6) with resonance.

Noise filtersReactance

Computers, chargers and power boards contain filters of inductors and capacitors that stop high-frequency electrical noise travelling along the mains wiring.

Inductor in series: high \(X_{L}\) at high frequency blocks noise. Capacitor across the supply: low \(X_{C}\) at high frequency short-circuits noise away.

Exam angle: at 50 Hz the same components barely affect the mains.

Metal detectorsLC oscillator

Many hobby metal detectors use a coil that forms part of an LC circuit oscillating at a set frequency.

Metal under the coil → eddy currents change the coil's effective inductance → \(f_{0} = 1/(2\pi \sqrt{LC})\) shifts → the detector beeps

Exam angle: changing \(L\) or \(C\) changes the resonant frequency.

EXPLAIN ITRadio tuning · Excellence-style

Explain how adjusting a variable capacitor in a series LC circuit allows a radio to select one station.

Show model answer

The aerial produces small emfs at many frequencies, one for each station. The LC circuit has impedance \(Z = \sqrt{R^{2} + (X_{L} - X_{C})^{2}}\). At its resonant frequency \(f_{0} = 1/(2\pi \sqrt{LC})\), \(X_{L} = X_{C}\) and they cancel, so \(Z = R\), its minimum value, and the current at that frequency is a maximum. At other frequencies \(X_{L}\) ≠ \(X_{C}\), so \(Z\) is larger and the current is much smaller. Adjusting the capacitor changes \(C\) and so moves \(f_{0}\); when \(f_{0}\) equals a station's frequency, that station's signal gives the largest current and is the one heard. A small resistance makes the peak sharp, so nearby stations are rejected.
