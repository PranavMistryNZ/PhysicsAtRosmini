The big ideas

Three ideas run through the whole standard. Newton's second law in all its forms: \(F = ma\), \(F_{c} = mv^{2}/r\), \(\tau = I\alpha\) and \(a = -\omega^{2}x\). Conservation laws: momentum (linear and angular) when there is no external force or torque, and energy, which must include every form, including rotational kinetic energy. And linear and rotational quantities mirror each other: \(x \leftrightarrow \theta\), \(v \leftrightarrow \omega\), \(m \leftrightarrow I\), \(F \leftrightarrow \tau\), \(p \leftrightarrow L\).

Exam skills · writing mechanical systems answers

What the examiners look for, built from the NZQA 2023 and 2024 assessment reports for AS91524 and the achievement criteria. Both recent exams had three questions: circular motion, rotational motion and simple harmonic motion.

Achievement

Single ideas and one-step calculations: centripetal force, angular velocity, a period. Correct free-body diagrams; stating the conditions for SHM; recognising that damping reduces amplitude.

Merit

Link two ideas or complete most steps of a calculation: use \(F_{c} = F_{g}\) for an orbit; recognise that the net force provides the centripetal force; link \(L = I\omega\) to a change in mass distribution; use the reference circle for a time.

Excellence

Complete multi-step chains laid out in order: conservation of energy that includes every form of energy; force diagrams at several points with relative sizes; explaining how a change in \(I\) changes \(\alpha\) or \(\omega\); full energy story for damping.

A structure for "explain" questions

1Name the principle

Newton's second law, conservation of momentum or energy, \(\tau = I\alpha\), conservation of angular momentum, \(a = -\omega^{2}x\)…

2Apply it to this situation

Say which forces act and where: weight and support at the top of the loop, tension in the string, a torque from friction.

3Link with physics words or an equation

"As she pulls her arms in, \(I\) decreases; \(L = I\omega\) is constant, so \(\omega\) increases."

4Answer the question asked

Finish with the conclusion: "so she spins faster."

Merit and Excellence explanations link ideas in order and name the physics principle behind each step. Three chains cover most questions:

Moving in a circle→velocity changes direction→acceleration towards the centre→net force towards the centre, \(F_{\text{net}} = mv^{2}/r\)→which real force(s) supply it?
Mass moves closer to the axis→\(I = \Sigma mr^{2}\) decreases→no external torque, so \(L = I\omega\) constant→\(\omega\) increases
Displaced from equilibrium→restoring force \(\propto -x\)→\(a = -\omega^{2}x\)→SHM with \(T\) independent of amplitude

The CEO framework for calculation questions

Use the four steps above when the question says explain. When it says calculate, determine or show that, work through Conceptualise, Execute and Outcomes.

CConceptualise

Understand the situation before you write any maths.

  • Core physics: momentum, energy, circular motion, torque, angular momentum, SHM?
  • Visualise: sketch it. Draw a free-body diagram with real forces only, and mark the direction of the centre of the circle.
  • Define the system: what is conserved? Is there an external force or torque? Which forms of energy are there at each point?
EExecute

Turn the concept into a solution.

  • Select equations that link the knowns to the unknown (see 0.3 Formula Sheet).
  • Solve symbolically first: masses often cancel.
  • Substitute in SI units: rpm → rad s⁻¹, km → m, g → kg, hours → s. Calculator in radians for SHM.
OOutcomes

Check the answer makes physical sense.

  • Units: rad s⁻¹, N m for torque, kg m² for \(I\), kg m² s⁻¹ for \(L\).
  • Reality check: a higher orbit should be slower; a rolling object should be slower than a sliding one; the support force at the bottom of a loop is more than \(mg\).
  • Cross-check: \(T = 2\pi /\omega\); energy before = energy after; momentum vectors close into a triangle.
CEO worked example · roller coaster loop

Question: A roller coaster car goes round a vertical loop of radius 9.0 m. It is just fast enough to stay on the track at the top. Calculate its speed at the top, and its speed at the bottom of the loop. Ignore friction.

C · Conceptualise: at the top, both weight and support force point down, towards the centre. "Just fast enough" means the support force has dropped to zero, so weight alone provides the centripetal force. Between top and bottom, mechanical energy is conserved, and the car still has kinetic energy at the top.

E · Execute: at the top, \(mg = mv_{t}^{2}/r\), so \(v_{t} = \sqrt{gr} = \sqrt{9.8 \times 9.0}\) = 9.4 m s⁻¹. Energy, top to bottom (height drop \(2r\) = 18 m): \(\tfrac{1}{2}mv_{b}^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_{t}^{2} + mg(2r)\), so \(v_{b} = \sqrt{v_{t}^{2} + 4gr} = \sqrt{5gr} = \sqrt{441}\) = 21 m s⁻¹.

O · Outcomes: m s⁻¹ ✓. The mass cancelled, as it should for a question that gives none ✓. Faster at the bottom ✓. The 2023 examiners saw many answers use \(E_{p}\)(top) = \(E_{k}\)(bottom), forgetting the kinetic energy at the top; that gives 19 m s⁻¹, which is too slow.

CEO worked example · flywheel and falling mass

Question: A 0.50 kg mass hangs from a string wound round the 0.040 m radius axle of a flywheel. Released from rest, it falls 1.2 m in 3.0 s. Calculate the angular acceleration of the flywheel and its rotational inertia.

C · Conceptualise: the mass accelerates uniformly. The string's tension provides the torque on the flywheel. Linear and angular quantities are linked by the axle radius: \(a = r\alpha\).

E · Execute: \(d = \tfrac{1}{2}at^{2}\), so \(a = 2d/t^{2} = 2 \times 1.2/9.0 = 0.267\) m s⁻². \(\alpha = a/r = 0.267/0.040\) = 6.7 rad s⁻². Tension: \(mg - T = ma\), so \(T = 0.50 \times (9.8 - 0.267) = 4.77\) N. Torque \(\tau = Tr = 0.191\) N m. \(I = \tau /\alpha = 0.191/6.67\) = 0.029 kg m².

O · Outcomes: rad s⁻² and kg m² ✓. The tension is a little less than the weight (4.9 N), as it must be for the mass to accelerate downwards ✓. The 2024 examiners noted answers that found the torque from the weight instead of the tension.

Diagram checklist

  • Free-body diagrams: draw real forces only (weight, support, tension, friction). Never add a "centripetal force" arrow as an extra force; it is the resultant.
  • Start every arrow at the object, label it, and make the lengths show relative sizes.
  • For circular motion, mark the centre of the circle and draw the net force towards it.
  • Momentum vector diagrams: draw to scale, nose to tail; the "after" vectors add to give the "before" vector.
  • SHM graphs: label axes with units, keep the period constant, and show the amplitude decaying for damping. A release from rest starts at maximum displacement.

Significant figures and units

  • rpm → rad s⁻¹: × 2π ÷ 60. Revolutions → radians: × 2π. km h⁻¹ → m s⁻¹: ÷ 3.6.
  • Orbits: \(r\) is measured from the centre of the planet. Add the planet's radius to the altitude.
  • Give the final answer to the same number of significant figures as the data (usually 2 or 3), with a unit. Carry unrounded values through and round at the end.
  • In a "show that" question, write the equation and your unrounded answer.

01 · Translational motion

Momentum, impulse and the centre of mass: tracking a whole system when the pieces move in two dimensions.

Momentum and the centre of mass

Five ideas. Level 2 did momentum in a straight line; Level 3 adds two dimensions and the centre of mass.

1.1.1 · Momentum is a vector

Momentum is mass times velocity, and it has the direction of the velocity:

\[p=mv\]

Units: kg m s⁻¹. Two objects with the same speed moving in different directions have different momenta. In two dimensions, add momenta like any other vectors: nose to tail, or by components.

1.1.2 · Impulse

A force acting for a time changes the momentum. The change is called the impulse:

\[\Delta p=F\Delta t\]
  • Impulse is the area under a force–time graph.
  • For a fixed change in momentum, a longer contact time means a smaller force: airbags, crumple zones, bending your knees on landing.
  • \(\Delta p\) is a vector subtraction: a ball bouncing back has \(\Delta p = m(v_f - v_i)\), which is bigger than \(mv\) because the direction reverses.
1.1.3 · Conservation of momentum in two dimensions

When no external net force acts on a system, its total momentum stays the same. Forces between the objects are internal: they come in Newton's third law pairs, so their impulses cancel.

\[\Sigma \vec{p}_{\text{before}}=\Sigma \vec{p}_{\text{after}}\]

In 2D, draw the momentum vectors after the collision nose to tail. They must add up to the momentum before. Either use a scale diagram, or resolve into components and conserve each direction separately.

The "after" momenta, placed nose to tail, close the triangle with the "before" momentum. Try it in 1.2 with Show momentum vectors ticked.

1.1.4 · Elastic and inelastic collisions

Momentum is conserved in every collision (if the system is isolated). Kinetic energy is not always:

  • Elastic: total kinetic energy after = before. Hard spheres, gas molecules, a good snooker shot.
  • Inelastic: some kinetic energy becomes heat, sound or deformation. Most real collisions.
  • Perfectly inelastic: the objects stick together. The largest possible loss of kinetic energy, but momentum is still conserved.
  • In an explosion, kinetic energy increases (from chemical or elastic potential energy), yet the total momentum is unchanged.
1.1.5 · Centre of mass

The centre of mass is the average position of the mass in a system. For two masses on a line:

\[x_{\text{cm}}=\dfrac{m_1x_1+m_2x_2}{m_1+m_2}\]

In 2D, use the same formula for the \(y\) coordinates. The total momentum of a system equals its total mass times the velocity of its centre of mass:

\[\Sigma p=(\Sigma m)\,v_{\text{cm}}\]

So if no external force acts, the centre of mass moves at constant velocity, however violently the parts collide or explode. A firework shell's fragments fly apart, but their centre of mass carries on along the original parabola (gravity is the only external force).

Translational motion · exam-style problems

Original practice questions in the style of AS91524. These are not NZQA questions. Attempt each part before opening the worked answer, and show formula, substitution, answer and unit.

QUESTION ONEIce hockey pucks · 2D collision

A 0.17 kg puck A slides east at 8.0 m s⁻¹ and hits an identical stationary puck B. Afterwards A moves at 4.0 m s⁻¹ at 60° north of east.

(a) Calculate the momentum of puck A before the collision. Achievement

(b) Use conservation of momentum to find the velocity of puck B after the collision. Merit

(c) Determine whether the collision is elastic. Excellence

Show worked answer · Question One

(a) \(p = mv = 0.17 \times 8.0 = 1.36\) kg m s⁻¹ east (1.4 kg m s⁻¹).

(b) A after: \(p_{A}' = 0.17 \times 4.0 = 0.68\) kg m s⁻¹ at 60° N of E. Components: east \(0.68\cos 60° = 0.34\), north \(0.68\sin 60° = 0.589\). B must make up the rest: east \(1.36 - 0.34 = 1.02\), north \(0 - 0.589 = -0.589\) (that is, south). \(p_{B}' = \sqrt{1.02^{2} + 0.589^{2}} = 1.18\) kg m s⁻¹, so \(v_{B} = 1.18/0.17 = 6.9\) m s⁻¹, at \(\tan^{-1}(0.589/1.02) = 30°\) south of east.

(c) \(E_{k}\) before \(= \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.17 \times 8.0^{2} = 5.44\) J. After: \(\tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.17 \times 4.0^{2} + \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.17 \times 6.93^{2} = 1.36 + 4.08 = 5.44\) J. Kinetic energy is conserved, so the collision is elastic. This fits the rule for equal masses: the pucks leave at 60° + 30° = 90° to each other.

QUESTION TWOSkaters push apart · centre of mass

A 60 kg skater and a 40 kg skater stand still together on smooth ice, then push apart. The 60 kg skater moves off at 2.0 m s⁻¹.

(a) Calculate the velocity of the 40 kg skater. Achievement

(b) Explain why the centre of mass of the two skaters does not move. Merit

(c) Calculate the kinetic energy produced and explain where it came from. Show that the centre of mass is still at the starting point 3.0 s later. Excellence

Show worked answer · Question Two

(a) Total momentum before = 0. So \(60 \times 2.0 = 40\,v\), giving \(v = 3.0\) m s⁻¹ in the opposite direction.

(b) The pushing forces are internal: equal and opposite (Newton's third law), so they give equal and opposite impulses. The ice is smooth, so there is no external horizontal force. Total momentum stays zero, and since \(\Sigma p = (\Sigma m)v_{\text{cm}}\), the centre of mass stays at rest.

(c) \(E_{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}(60)(2.0^{2}) + \tfrac{1}{2}(40)(3.0^{2}) = 120 + 180 = 300\) J, from chemical energy in the skaters' muscles. After 3.0 s they are at −6.0 m and +9.0 m: \(x_{\text{cm}} = (60 \times -6.0 + 40 \times 9.0)/100 = 0\) ✓.

QUESTION THREETennis serve · impulse

A 58 g tennis ball arrives at a racquet at 30 m s⁻¹ and leaves in the opposite direction at 40 m s⁻¹. The contact lasts 5.0 ms.

(a) Calculate the change in momentum of the ball. Merit

(b) Calculate the average force of the racquet on the ball. Merit

(c) Coaches say "follow through". Explain, using impulse, why keeping the racquet in contact with the ball for longer makes it leave faster. Excellence

Show worked answer · Question Three

(a) Take the outgoing direction as positive: \(\Delta p = m(v_f - v_i) = 0.058 \times (40 - (-30)) = 0.058 \times 70 = 4.06\) kg m s⁻¹ (4.1 kg m s⁻¹). Using 40 − 30 is the common mistake.

(b) \(F = \Delta p/\Delta t = 4.06/(5.0 \times 10^{-3}) = 812\) N (810 N).

(c) Impulse = \(F\Delta t\) = change in momentum. The player can only push with a limited force, so increasing the contact time \(\Delta t\) increases the impulse. A larger impulse means a larger change in momentum, so the ball leaves with a higher velocity.

1.5 · Applications of translational motion

Momentum, impulse and the centre of mass in sport, safety and space.

Where do we use these ideas?

Exam questions are often set in contexts like these, with the information you need supplied.

Car safetyImpulse

Crumple zones, airbags and seatbelts all stretch out the time a passenger takes to stop.

Same \(\Delta p\) → longer \(\Delta t\) → smaller \(F = \Delta p/\Delta t\) → less injury

Exam angle: the change in momentum is fixed by the speed and mass; only the time can be changed.

Snooker and pool2D collisions

A cue ball hitting an object ball off-centre sends them apart at close to 90°, because the balls have equal mass and the collision is nearly elastic.

Equal masses + elastic → momentum triangle is right-angled → 90° split

Exam angle: players use this "90° rule" to predict where the cue ball goes.

RocketsConservation

A rocket pushes exhaust gas backwards; the gas pushes the rocket forwards. No air is needed.

Gas gains backward momentum → rocket gains equal forward momentum → total momentum unchanged

Exam angle: the forces between rocket and gas are internal to the rocket–gas system.

High jump and gymnasticsCentre of mass

A Fosbury-flop high jumper arches over the bar so that their centre of mass can pass under it.

Body bends round the bar → centre of mass lies outside the body → less height gain needed

Exam angle: once airborne, the jumper's centre of mass follows a parabola, whatever their limbs do.

RecoilExplosions

A rifle kicks back when it fires; a cannon on wheels rolls backwards.

Total momentum zero before → bullet forward \(mv\) → rifle backward \(mv\) → heavier rifle recoils slower

Exam angle: the bullet gets most of the kinetic energy, because \(E_{k} = p^{2}/2m\) and its mass is small.

Crash investigationVector momentum

Police measure skid marks after a collision at an intersection and work backwards to the speeds before the crash.

Directions and speeds after → momentum vectors after → add them → momentum before → speed of each car

Exam angle: exactly a 2D momentum vector diagram, run backwards.

EXPLAIN ITBending your knees · Merit / Excellence-style

Explain why a person who jumps off a wall and bends their knees on landing is less likely to be injured than one who lands stiff-legged.

Show model answer

Both people hit the ground at the same speed, and both end up at rest, so their change in momentum \(\Delta p = mv\) is the same. The impulse from the ground equals this change in momentum: \(F\Delta t = \Delta p\). Bending the knees makes the stopping take longer, so \(\Delta t\) increases. Since \(\Delta p\) is fixed, a larger \(\Delta t\) means a smaller average force \(F\) from the ground on the legs, and so less chance of injury.

02 · Circular motion & gravity

Why anything moving in a circle needs a net force towards the centre, and how gravity supplies it for satellites.

Circular motion and gravitation

Six ideas. The examiners report that many answers lose marks on the direction of the net force, so read 2.1.2 carefully.

2.1.1 · Speed and period

For an object moving round a circle of radius \(r\) at constant speed, taking time \(T\) for one revolution:

\[v=\dfrac{2\pi r}{T}\qquad \omega=\dfrac{2\pi}{T}=2\pi f\qquad v=r\omega\]

The speed is constant, but the velocity is not: its direction changes all the time. Changing velocity means acceleration.

2.1.2 · Centripetal acceleration and force

The acceleration points towards the centre of the circle:

\[a_c=\dfrac{v^2}{r}\qquad F_c=\dfrac{mv^2}{r}\]
  • \(F_{c}\) is not a new kind of force. It is the net force, the resultant of the real forces (weight, support, tension, friction, gravity).
  • So on a free-body diagram, never add an extra "centripetal force" arrow. Draw the real forces; their vector sum points to the centre.
  • "Centrifugal force" is not a force on the object. You feel pushed outwards because your body tends to keep going in a straight line (inertia).
2.1.3 · Vertical circles

In a vertical circle, the speed changes (energy is conserved) and so does the force needed.

  • Top (inside a loop): weight and support force both point down, towards the centre: \(N + mg = mv^{2}/r\). As the speed drops, \(N\) drops. At the minimum speed \(N = 0\) and \(v_{\text{min}} = \sqrt{gr}\). The rider feels light.
  • Bottom: support force up, weight down: \(N - mg = mv^{2}/r\), so \(N = mg + mv^{2}/r\). The rider feels heavy.
  • Use conservation of energy to link the speeds at the top and bottom: \(\tfrac{1}{2}mv_{b}^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_{t}^{2} + mg(2r)\).
2.1.4 · Banked curves

A road banked at angle \(\theta\) tips the support force inwards. Without friction:

\[N\cos\theta=mg\qquad N\sin\theta=\dfrac{mv^2}{r}\qquad \Rightarrow\ \tan\theta=\dfrac{v^2}{rg}\]

The net force is horizontal, towards the centre of the curve, because the car moves in a horizontal circle. It is not along the slope. A car going faster than the design speed needs friction down the slope; slower, friction up the slope.

2.1.5 · Newton's law of gravitation

Every mass attracts every other mass:

\[F_g=\dfrac{GMm}{r^2}\qquad G=6.67\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N\,m^2\,kg^{-2}}\]
  • \(r\) is measured between the centres of the masses. For a satellite, \(r\) = planet radius + altitude.
  • Inverse square: double the distance, one quarter of the force.
  • The gravitational field strength at distance \(r\) is \(g = GM/r^{2}\). At the Earth's surface this is 9.8 N kg⁻¹.
2.1.6 · Satellites and orbits

For a circular orbit, gravity is the only force, so it provides the centripetal force:

\[\dfrac{GMm}{r^2}=\dfrac{mv^2}{r}\ \Rightarrow\ v=\sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{r}}\qquad T^2=\dfrac{4\pi^2}{GM}r^3\]
  • The satellite's mass cancels: every object at that radius orbits at the same speed.
  • Higher orbits are slower and have longer periods.
  • A geostationary satellite has \(T\) = 24 h (strictly 23 h 56 min) and orbits above the equator, about 36 000 km up, so it stays over the same spot.
  • Astronauts are "weightless" because they and the station fall together. Gravity still acts; there is just no support force.

Rides, roads and satellites: where the net force points to the centre.

Where do we use circular motion?

EXPLAIN ITLight at the top · Merit / Excellence-style

A rider on a Ferris wheel feels lighter at the top of the ride and heavier at the bottom. Explain why, using a force diagram for each position.

Show model answer

The rider moves in a circle at steady speed, so the net force on them must point to the centre of the wheel and have size \(mv^{2}/r\). At the top, the centre is below the rider: weight acts down and the seat's support force acts up, so \(mg - N = mv^{2}/r\) and \(N = mg - mv^{2}/r\), less than their weight. At the bottom, the centre is above: \(N - mg = mv^{2}/r\) and \(N = mg + mv^{2}/r\), more than their weight. What a person feels as their weight is the support force, so they feel lighter at the top and heavier at the bottom. Their actual weight \(mg\) is the same everywhere.

03 · Rotating systems

Torque, rotational inertia and angular momentum: Newton's laws for things that spin.

Rotational motion

Six ideas. Every rotational quantity has a linear twin, so you already know most of the equations.

3.1.1 · Angular quantities

Rotation is measured in radians: one revolution = 2π rad.

  • Angular displacement \(\theta\) (rad); angular velocity \(\omega = \Delta\theta/\Delta t\) (rad s⁻¹); angular acceleration \(\alpha = \Delta\omega/\Delta t\) (rad s⁻²).
  • A point at radius \(r\) has: \(d = r\theta\), \(v = r\omega\), \(a = r\alpha\). Every point on a rigid wheel has the same \(\omega\), but points further out move faster.
  • Convert rpm: \(\omega = \text{rpm} \times 2\pi /60\).
3.1.2 · Rotational kinematics

For constant angular acceleration, swap each linear symbol for its rotational twin:

LinearRotational
\(v_f = v_i + at\)\(\omega_f = \omega_i + \alpha t\)
\(d = v_it + \tfrac{1}{2}at^{2}\)\(\theta = \omega_it + \tfrac{1}{2}\alpha t^{2}\)
\(v_f^{2} = v_i^{2} + 2ad\)\(\omega_f^{2} = \omega_i^{2} + 2\alpha\theta\)
\(d = \tfrac{1}{2}(v_i + v_f)t\)\(\theta = \tfrac{1}{2}(\omega_i + \omega_f)t\)
3.1.3 · Torque

A torque is the turning effect of a force:

\[\tau=Fr\]

\(r\) is the perpendicular distance from the axis to the line of action of the force. Units: N m. A net torque is needed to change the rotation, just as a net force is needed to change linear motion. The 2024 examiners noted that the best answers linked the net torque to a net force acting off the axis.

3.1.4 · Rotational inertia and \(\tau = I\alpha\)

Rotational inertia \(I\) measures how hard it is to change an object's rotation. It depends on the mass and where the mass is:

\[I=\Sigma mr^2\qquad \tau=I\alpha\]
  • Mass far from the axis counts for much more (\(r^{2}\)). A hoop has a bigger \(I\) than a disc of the same mass and radius.
  • Common shapes (given in questions): hoop \(mr^{2}\), solid disc or cylinder \(\tfrac{1}{2}mr^{2}\), solid sphere \(\tfrac{2}{5}mr^{2}\), point mass \(mr^{2}\).
  • Units: kg m².
3.1.5 · Angular momentum
\[L=I\omega\qquad L=mvr\ \text{(point mass)}\]

If no external torque acts, angular momentum is conserved. When a spinning object pulls mass inwards, \(I\) falls, so \(\omega\) rises: skaters, divers, collapsing stars. Its rotational kinetic energy increases, because work is done pulling the mass in.

3.1.6 · Rotational kinetic energy and rolling
\[E_{k(\text{rot})}=\tfrac{1}{2}I\omega^2\]

An object that rolls without slipping has both kinds of kinetic energy, with \(v = r\omega\):

\[mgh=\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2+\tfrac{1}{2}I\omega^2\]

Objects with more of their mass near the rim put more energy into spinning, so they roll down a slope more slowly. Leaving out one of the two kinetic energy terms was a common mistake in 2023.

Flywheels, divers, doors and neutron stars.

Where do we use rotation?

Look for the linear twin in each one: torque ↔ force, rotational inertia ↔ mass, angular momentum ↔ momentum.

EXPLAIN ITA diver's tuck · Merit / Excellence-style

Show model answer

Once the diver leaves the board, no external torque acts on her about her centre of mass, so her angular momentum \(L = I\omega\) is conserved. Pulling into a tuck brings her mass closer to the axis of rotation, and since \(I = \Sigma mr^{2}\), her rotational inertia decreases. To keep \(L\) constant, her angular velocity must increase by the same factor, so she somersaults faster and fits more rotations into the time before she reaches the water. Opening out increases \(I\) again, which reduces \(\omega\), so she can enter the water in a controlled, nearly non-rotating, straight position.

04 · Oscillating systems

Simple harmonic motion: springs, pendulums, the reference circle and damping.

Simple harmonic motion

Six ideas. The reference circle (4.1.3) is the key tool: it turns SHM into circular motion you can draw.

4.1.1 · What makes motion simple harmonic?

An object moves with SHM when the restoring force is proportional to its displacement from equilibrium and directed back towards equilibrium:

\[F=-kx\qquad a=-\omega^2x\]

The minus sign says the force and acceleration always point towards equilibrium, opposite to the displacement. Examples: a mass on a spring, a pendulum with small swings, a floating object bobbing.

4.1.2 · Describing an oscillation
  • Amplitude \(A\): the maximum displacement from equilibrium (m).
  • Period \(T\): the time for one complete oscillation (s). Frequency \(f = 1/T\) (Hz).
  • Angular frequency \(\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi /T\) (rad s⁻¹).
  • In SHM the period does not depend on the amplitude.
4.1.3 · The reference circle

Imagine a point moving round a circle of radius \(A\) at angular velocity \(\omega\). Its shadow on a diameter moves with SHM. The angle swept, \(\omega t\), is the phase.

  • Displacement: \(x = A\sin \omega t\) (starting at equilibrium) or \(x = A\cos \omega t\) (starting at an extreme).
  • Velocity: the shadow of the point's velocity \(A\omega\); maximum at equilibrium.
  • Acceleration: the shadow of the centripetal acceleration \(A\omega^{2}\); maximum at the extremes, pointing to the centre.
  • Time from the circle: find the angle the point must sweep, then \(t = \theta/\omega\), or \(t = (\theta/2\pi) \times T\).

See it in action in 4.2.

4.1.4 · The SHM equations
\[x=A\sin\omega t\quad v=A\omega\cos\omega t\quad a=-A\omega^2\sin\omega t\]

\(v_{\text{max}} = A\omega\) at equilibrium; \(a_{\text{max}} = A\omega^{2}\) at the extremes. Set your calculator to radians. The 2024 examiners saw marks lost to degree mode, missing brackets and the wrong trig function.

4.1.5 · Springs and pendulums
\[T=2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{m}{k}}\qquad T=2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{l}{g}}\]
  • A stiffer spring (larger \(k\)) or a smaller mass gives a shorter period.
  • A pendulum's period depends only on its length and \(g\), not on its mass, as long as the swings are small (less than about 10°).
  • To find \(k\) or \(g\) from data, plot \(T^{2}\) against \(m\) or \(l\): a straight line through the origin with gradient \(4\pi^{2}/k\) or \(4\pi^{2}/g\). Try it in 4.3.
4.1.6 · Energy and damping

Energy swaps between kinetic and potential twice each cycle; the total stays constant without friction:

\[E_{\text{total}}=\tfrac{1}{2}kA^2=\tfrac{1}{2}mv_{\text{max}}^2\]

Damping is a resistive force (friction, air resistance, a shock absorber's oil) that does work against the motion. Mechanical energy is transferred to heat, so the amplitude decreases over time. Light damping barely changes the period. For Excellence, name the force, the energy transfer and its effect on the amplitude.

  • So \(k = 4\pi^{2}/\text{gradient}\) and \(g = 4\pi^{2}/\text{gradient}\). This is the step the 2023 examiners said many candidates missed.
  • Timing 10 oscillations divides the reaction-time error by 10. Several points and a line of best fit average out the rest.
  • A graph of \(T\) against \(m\) would curve. Squaring \(T\) makes it straight, which makes the gradient easy to use.

Oscillating systems · exam-style problems

Original practice questions in the style of AS91524. These are not NZQA questions. Calculators in radians.

QUESTION ONEMass on a spring

A 0.25 kg mass hangs on a spring with \(k\) = 16 N m⁻¹. It is pulled down 5.0 cm and released.

(a) Calculate the period. Achievement

(b) Calculate the maximum speed and maximum acceleration. Merit

(c) Use the reference circle to find the time after release when the mass is first 2.5 cm below equilibrium. Excellence

Show worked answer · Question One

(a) \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k} = 2\pi\sqrt{0.25/16} = 0.785\) s (0.79 s).

(b) \(\omega = 2\pi /T = 8.0\) rad s⁻¹. \(v_{\text{max}} = A\omega = 0.050 \times 8.0 = 0.40\) m s⁻¹. \(a_{\text{max}} = A\omega^{2} = 0.050 \times 64 = 3.2\) m s⁻².

(c) Released from the bottom extreme, so \(x = A\cos \omega t\) (taking down as positive). 2.5 cm is half the amplitude: \(\cos \omega t = 0.5\), so \(\omega t = \pi/3\) rad (60° round the reference circle). \(t = (\pi/3)/8.0 = 0.13\) s. Check: 60° is one sixth of a cycle, and \(T/6 = 0.131\) s ✓.

QUESTION TWOPendulum graph

Students time pendulums of different lengths. They plot \(T^{2}\) (s²) against length \(l\) (m) and get a straight line through the origin with a gradient of 4.02 s² m⁻¹.

(a) Explain why they timed 10 swings rather than one. Achievement

(b) Use the gradient to calculate \(g\). Merit

(c) The experiment is repeated on the Moon, where \(g\) = 1.6 m s⁻². Explain how the graph would change, and calculate the period of a 1.0 m pendulum there. Excellence

Show worked answer · Question Two

(a) The reaction-time error in starting and stopping the watch (about 0.2 s) is the same whatever is timed, so timing 10 swings and dividing by 10 makes the error in \(T\) ten times smaller.

(b) \(T^{2} = (4\pi^{2}/g)\,l\), so the gradient = \(4\pi^{2}/g\). \(g = 4\pi^{2}/4.02 = 9.82\) m s⁻².

(c) The gradient \(4\pi^{2}/g\) would be about six times steeper (24.7 s² m⁻¹), still a straight line through the origin. \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{1.0/1.6} = 5.0\) s, compared with 2.0 s on Earth.

QUESTION THREECar suspension · damping

A car's springs and shock absorbers make it bob up and down after it goes over a bump. A worn shock absorber lets it bounce many times.

(a) State what damping does to an oscillation. Achievement

(b) Sketch the displacement–time graph for a lightly damped car body, released from its highest point. Merit

(c) Explain, in terms of energy, how a shock absorber damps the oscillation, and why a good one is designed to stop the bouncing within about one cycle. Excellence

Show worked answer · Question Three

(a) Damping reduces the amplitude of the oscillation over time.

(b) A cosine-shaped curve starting at maximum displacement, with each peak smaller than the last (an exponentially decaying envelope) and the time between peaks staying the same. Axes labelled displacement (m) and time (s).

(c) The shock absorber pushes a piston through oil. The oil exerts a resistive force opposing the motion, so work is done against it and the car's mechanical energy (kinetic and elastic potential) is transferred to heat in the oil. Less mechanical energy means a smaller amplitude each cycle. A car that keeps bouncing is uncomfortable, and its tyres lose grip when the wheel lifts, so a good shock absorber removes the energy within about one cycle without making the suspension too stiff to absorb the bump.

4.6 · Applications of oscillating systems

Clocks, buildings, cars and musical instruments.

Where do we use oscillations?

Anything with a restoring force proportional to displacement oscillates with SHM. Damping decides how long it lasts.

Pendulum clocks\(T = 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}\)

A pendulum keeps time because its period doesn't depend on the size of the swing.

Period set by \(l\) only → small swings stay in time → clock adjusted by moving the bob up or down

Exam angle: a clock that runs slow needs a shorter pendulum.

Earthquake engineeringDamping

New Zealand buildings such as Te Papa sit on base isolators: lead-rubber bearings that let the building sway slowly and absorb energy.

Ground shakes → bearings flex → lead core deforms → energy becomes heat → building's amplitude is reduced

Exam angle: explain damping as an energy transfer, not just "it stops it".

Car suspensionSpring + damper

Springs let the wheels follow the road; shock absorbers stop the body bouncing afterwards.

Bump → spring compresses → oscillation → damper turns energy into heat → car settles quickly

Exam angle: a heavier load lowers the frequency, \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}\).

Quartz watchesNatural frequency

A tiny quartz tuning fork vibrates at exactly 32 768 Hz; the watch counts the vibrations.

Stiff crystal + tiny mass → very high natural frequency → very stable timing

Exam angle: the frequency depends on stiffness and mass, like a spring.

Bungy jumpingEnergy in SHM

Once the cord is stretched, the jumper bounces up and down with roughly SHM, with damping from air resistance and the cord itself.

\(E_{p}\) (gravity) → \(E_{k}\) → elastic \(E_{p}\) in the cord → repeated, losing a little each bounce

Exam angle: the lowest point has maximum acceleration, upwards.

Tuned mass dampersOscillators

Tall towers such as Taipei 101 hang a huge pendulum near the top that swings against the building's sway.

Building sways → pendulum swings out of phase → pulls the building back → dampers absorb the energy

Exam angle: the pendulum's length is chosen so its period matches the building's.

EXPLAIN ITWhy the period stays the same · Merit / Excellence-style

Explain why a mass on a spring takes the same time for each oscillation, whether it is pulled down 2 cm or 4 cm before release.

Show model answer

The spring's restoring force is proportional to the displacement, \(F = -kx\), so the acceleration is \(a = -(k/m)x\): this is SHM with \(\omega^{2} = k/m\). Pulling it down twice as far doubles the restoring force and so doubles the acceleration at every corresponding point. The mass has twice as far to travel, but it is accelerated twice as hard and reaches twice the speed, so it covers the larger distance in the same time. The period, \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}\), depends only on the mass and the spring constant, not on the amplitude.
