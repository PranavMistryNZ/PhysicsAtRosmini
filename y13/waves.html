Wave foundations

Use these five ideas as the foundation for the rest of the topic. Open each panel for the detail you need.

1.1.1 · Waves transfer energy

A progressive or travelling wave transfers energy from one point to another without transferring matter between the points.

Mechanical waves are produced by a disturbance in a material medium. Particles of the medium oscillate about fixed positions while energy is transmitted through the medium.

Electromagnetic waves are disturbances of varying electric and magnetic fields. No material medium is required, so they can travel through a vacuum.

1.1.2 · Transverse waves

Transverse waves: displacement is at right angles to the direction of travel.

particle motion ↑↓wave travels →

The particles oscillate perpendicular to the direction the wave travels.

Examples: water waves, electromagnetic waves, vibrating strings, secondary seismic waves and surface waves.

1.1.3 · Longitudinal waves

Longitudinal waves: displacement is parallel to the direction of travel.

particle motion ↔wave travels →

The particles oscillate parallel to the direction the wave travels. Regions where particles bunch together are compressions; spread-out regions are rarefactions.

Compression: a region in a longitudinal wave where the particles of the medium are closer together than normal.

Rarefaction: a region in a longitudinal wave where the particles of the medium are further apart than normal.

transverse representationcompression = peakrarefaction = trough

In Physics, we often represent a longitudinal wave using a transverse-style graph because it is easier to see the pattern. In this representation, a crest shows a compression and a trough shows a rarefaction. The particles are not actually moving up and down — the graph is only a way of representing the longitudinal wave.

Examples: sound waves, helical springs and primary seismic waves.

1.1.4 · Describe the wave

Six quantities help describe and compare waves. The graphs below use the same colours, axes and measurement style.

  • Wavelength (λ) — distance between two consecutive points in phase, such as crest to crest. Unit: m.
  • Amplitude (A) — maximum displacement from equilibrium. Unit: m.
  • Period (T) — time for one complete oscillation. Unit: s.
  • Frequency (f) — number of complete oscillations per second. Unit: Hz.
  • Wave speed (v) — distance travelled by the wave per second. Unit: m/s.
  • Phase (φ) — position within a cycle; phase difference describes how far one oscillation leads or lags another. Unit: degrees (°) or radians (rad).
01Displacement–distance

λ is one full cycle along the medium. A is measured from the equilibrium line to a crest, not from crest to trough.

02Displacement–time

T is the time for one cycle. Two cycles in 2 s means f = 1 Hz; frequency counts cycles per second.

f = 1/T    and    v = fλ   (v in m/s)

03Phase · comparing two waves

Phase describes where a wave is in its cycle. 0°: the waves match exactly. 45°: Wave 2 is shifted slightly relative to Wave 1. 180°: a crest of Wave 1 lines up with a trough of Wave 2. The vertical separation is for clarity only.

1.1.5 · Combine waves

When waves overlap, the resultant displacement is the sum of the individual displacements.

Constructive interference: the waves combine to give a larger resultant displacement.

Destructive interference: the waves combine to give a smaller resultant displacement.

Diffraction: when waves pass through a gap, they spread into the region beyond the gap. The amount of spreading increases as the gap narrows and becomes pronounced when the gap width is similar to the wavelength.

QUESTION ONEFixed string · calculation & explanation

See the original NZQA 2025 string-and-harmonics question (Question Two) ↗

A stretched guitar string is fixed at both ends. It has a vibrating length of 0.660 m and a transverse wave speed of 132 m s⁻¹.

(a) Calculate the fundamental frequency of the string. Achievement

(b) The string is excited at its third harmonic. Calculate the frequency and describe how many antinodes appear along the string. Merit

(c) Explain why increasing the string tension increases the fundamental frequency if the length and mass per unit length remain unchanged. Excellence-style explanation

Show worked answer · Question One

(a) For a string with both ends fixed, the fundamental fits half a wavelength along the length, so f1=v2L=1322×0.660=100 Hz.

(b) f3=3f1=300 Hz. The third harmonic has three displacement antinodes (and four nodes, counting both fixed ends).

(c) Increasing tension raises the speed of transverse waves along the string: v=τμ. The fixed length keeps the fundamental wavelength at 2L; therefore, by f = v/λ, frequency increases as wave speed increases. The wave speed here is along the string, not the speed of sound through air.

Marking focus: correct result and units; correct harmonic pattern; linked explanation using tension, wave speed, constant wavelength and frequency.

QUESTION TWOClosed pipe · resonance

See the original NZQA 2024 pan-flute diagram (Question One, page 2) ↗

A pan-flute tube is 8.50 cm long and closed at one end. Take the speed of sound in air as 343 m s⁻¹. Neglect end correction.

(a) State the type of displacement point found at the closed end and the open end. Achievement

(b) Calculate the frequency of the third harmonic. Merit

(c) Explain why the second harmonic cannot form in an ideal tube closed at one end. Excellence-style explanation

Show worked answer · Question Two

(a) The closed end is a displacement node: the wall prevents air from moving through it. The open end is approximately a displacement antinode: air can move freely there (and the pressure variation is small).

(b) The third harmonic of a closed pipe fits three quarter-wavelengths: 3λ4=L⇒λ=4L3 = 0.1133 m. Hence f=vλ=3430.1133≈3030 Hz (3 s.f.).

(c) A standing wave must simultaneously have a displacement node at the closed end and an antinode at the open end. That separation permits odd numbers of quarter-wavelengths: L = λ/4, 3λ/4, 5λ/4, …. The second harmonic would not satisfy both boundary conditions. Therefore, ideal closed pipes support odd-numbered harmonics only.

Marking focus: boundary conditions, sound-wave speed, unit conversion and a physical explanation of odd harmonics.

QUESTION THREEBeats · tuning

Two tuning forks are sounded together. One has frequency 523 Hz. The other has a lower frequency, and 9 beats per second are heard.

(a) Determine the frequency of the second fork. Achievement

(b) Calculate the approximate pitch (underlying tone frequency) heard while both forks sound. Merit

(c) Explain what happens to the beat rate as the lower-frequency fork is tuned up towards 523 Hz, and why. Excellence-style explanation

Show worked answer · Question Three

(a) fbeat=|f1−f2|. As the second fork is lower, 9 = 523 − f2, giving 514 Hz.

(b) fnote=f1+f22=523+5142=518.5 Hz.

(c) The frequency difference decreases as the second fork approaches 523 Hz, so the beat frequency falls and the time between loudness peaks increases. When both frequencies become equal, there is no fluctuating beat amplitude. Beats arise from the changing relative phase between the two similar-frequency waves, not from a difference in path length.

Marking focus: identify the correct branch of the frequency difference; calculate the average frequency; link relative phase to loudness variation.

QUESTION FOUROpen pipe · compare harmonics

An ideal organ pipe is open at both ends and has length 0.850 m. The sound speed is 340 m s⁻¹.

(a) State the displacement boundary condition at both open ends. Achievement

(b) Calculate the fundamental frequency and the first overtone frequency. Merit

(c) Compare the permitted harmonic frequencies in this pipe with those of an ideal pipe of the same length closed at one end. Excellence-style explanation

Show worked answer · Question Four

(a) Both open ends are approximately displacement antinodes (and pressure nodes).

(b) f1=v2L=3402×0.850=200 Hz. The first overtone is its second harmonic, 400 Hz.

(c) An open–open pipe allows all positive integer harmonics (200, 400, 600 Hz, …), because each mode maintains displacement antinodes at both ends. An equal-length closed–open pipe has a displacement node at the closed end and antinode at the open end. Its fundamental is v/(4L) = 100 Hz, and only odd harmonics are permitted (100, 300, 500 Hz, …). Thus the first overtone of the closed pipe is its third harmonic, not its second.

Marking focus: use correct boundary conditions to justify the frequency sequences rather than only quoting the formulas.

QUESTION FIVEReflection & standing-wave formation

A pulse travels to the right along a taut string and reaches a rigidly fixed end. Later, the string is driven continuously at a frequency that produces a stable standing wave.

(a) Describe the direction and orientation of the reflected upward pulse. Achievement

(b) Explain why the end of the string is a displacement node when incident and reflected waves overlap. Merit

(c) Explain how the superposition of oppositely travelling waves produces fixed nodes and antinodes, and distinguish a standing wave from two beats. Excellence-style explanation

Show worked answer · Question Five

(a) The reflected pulse travels left and is inverted (downward).

(b) A rigid fixed end cannot move, so its total displacement must remain zero. The reflected wave is inverted so its displacement at the end cancels that of the incident wave: yincident + yreflected = 0.

(c) Incident and reflected waves of equal frequency travelling in opposite directions superpose. At certain positions, their displacements always cancel, producing fixed nodes. Midway between adjacent nodes, the displacement oscillates between its largest positive and negative values, producing antinodes. Beats instead involve waves of slightly different frequencies producing a time-varying amplitude (periodic loudness changes), without a stationary pattern of nodes and antinodes.

Marking focus: connect phase, superposition, fixed boundaries and time-independent node positions; distinguish spatial standing-wave patterns from temporal beating.

Electromagnetic wave foundations

Review the knowledge needed for Level 3 light interference. Open each section to revise the idea, then use the formula sheet when you calculate.

2.1.1 · Electromagnetic waves and their properties

Electromagnetic waves are transverse waves: oscillating electric and magnetic fields are perpendicular to the direction of travel. Unlike sound, they can move through a vacuum.

Wavelength (λ) is the distance between points in phase; frequency (f) is the number of cycles per second; period (T) is the time for one cycle. These quantities are related through wave speed.

In a vacuum, all electromagnetic waves travel at approximately 3.00 × 108 m/s. When light changes medium, its speed and wavelength may change; frequency remains the same at the boundary.

Unit conversions: 1 nm = 10−9 m and 1 mm = 10−3 m. Convert all lengths to the same unit before substituting.

Visible light examples: red light has a relatively long wavelength (about 650 nm), while blue light has a shorter wavelength (about 450 nm). In a vacuum, shorter wavelengths mean higher frequencies. For this diagram, visible light is shown approximately from 380 to 700 nm; wavelengths beyond 700 nm are labelled infrared. The exact visible boundary varies between sources and observers.

  • Intensity describes the power transferred per unit area (W/m²). Greater intensity generally means brighter light; for the same wavelength, it does not mean a higher frequency.
  • Wavelength (λ, measured in m or nm) is the distance between successive crests of the electric field.
  • Frequency (f, measured in Hz) is the number of oscillations per second; light in a vacuum obeys c = fλ.

2.1.2 · Phase and Superposition

Superposition means that overlapping waves produce a resultant displacement equal to the sum of their individual displacements. For light, the electric fields add, and the observed brightness depends on the resulting intensity.

  • Constructive interference: waves arrive in phase, strengthening the resultant and producing a bright fringe.
  • Destructive interference: waves arrive in opposite phase (180° apart), cancelling when their amplitudes are equal and producing a dark fringe.
  • Path difference: the difference between distances travelled from each source to a point on the screen determines the phase difference.

2.1.3 · Coherent Waves and Interference

A clear, stable interference pattern requires coherent waves with a constant phase relationship and the same frequency. Monochromatic laser light is useful because it has a narrow wavelength range and can illuminate both slits coherently.

Each narrow slit diffracts the incoming light, allowing the two spreading wavefronts to overlap on a distant screen. Bright and dark bands form wherever their path difference causes constructive or destructive interference.

  • Increasing wavelength makes adjacent fringes farther apart.
  • Increasing the distance to the screen makes fringes farther apart.
  • Increasing separation between the slits moves adjacent fringes closer together.

The familiar fringe-spacing relationship assumes small angles and a screen much farther away than the slit separation. Use the Interference tab to test these trends.

2.1.4 · Wavefronts in Physics

A wavefront is a line joining points on a wave that are at the same phase. For example, every point on one crest can be represented by a single wavefront.

  • Direction of travel: the wave travels at right angles to the wavefront.
  • Wavelength: the perpendicular distance between neighbouring wavefronts is one wavelength, λ.
  • Plane wavefronts: far from a source, wavefronts can be treated as straight, parallel lines.
  • Circular wavefronts: a point source in two dimensions produces expanding circular wavefronts.
  • Important idea: when we sketch wavefronts for a transverse wave, we usually draw the lines through the crests only. Each line marks points that are in the same phase.

Wavefronts show where the wave has the same phase. In the transverse-wave sketch above, the blue wavefronts pass through the crests only. The arrows show the direction of propagation, which is perpendicular to the wavefronts.

Wavefront diagrams are useful because they let us describe reflection, refraction, diffraction and interference without drawing every individual oscillation of the wave.

2.1.5 · Diffraction gratings and multiple slits

A diffraction grating contains many closely spaced parallel slits or lines. Constructive interference from many slits produces narrow, sharply defined principal maxima.

The bright centre is the zero-order maximum. The first and second principal maxima occur at increasing angles on either side of the centre. Their positions depend on wavelength and slit spacing.

If there are N lines per metre, the distance between adjacent lines is d = 1/N in metres. If the grating is specified in lines per millimetre, convert that figure to lines per metre first.

As the number of illuminated slits increases, principal maxima become more sharply concentrated. This is why gratings can separate different wavelengths of light.

For an ideal multiple-slit arrangement, increasing the number of slits makes the principal bright maxima narrower and more intense. With N equally spaced slits there are N − 2 much weaker subsidiary maxima between neighbouring principal maxima (the 2025 exam compared the 2-slit and 3-slit patterns).

QUESTION 1Double-slit fringe spacing

A laser of wavelength 600 nm illuminates two slits separated by 0.250 mm. The screen is 2.00 m away.

  1. Calculate the separation of adjacent bright fringes in millimetres.
  2. Explain how the spacing changes if the slit separation is doubled while other quantities remain fixed.
  3. Explain why bright and dark fringes appear although the two slits are both illuminated.
Show worked answer

(a) Convert: λ = 600 × 10⁻⁹ m and d = 0.250 × 10⁻³ m. Using Δx = λL/d (nλ = dx/L with n = 1) gives (600 × 10⁻⁹ × 2.00)/(0.250 × 10⁻³) = 0.00480 m = 4.80 mm.

(b) Doubling d halves Δx, producing fringes 2.40 mm apart, because Δx is inversely proportional to d.

(c) The waves diffract through both slits and overlap. Where path difference is nλ they arrive in phase and reinforce (bright); at (n + ½)λ they cancel (dark).

QUESTION 2Grating angle and order

Monochromatic light of wavelength 520 nm strikes a diffraction grating with 600 lines per millimetre at normal incidence.

  1. Determine the distance between adjacent grating lines in metres.
  2. Calculate the angle of the first-order maximum.
  3. Determine the largest possible diffraction order and explain why higher orders are absent.
Show worked answer

(a) Line spacing d = 1/(600 × 10³) = 1.667 × 10⁻⁶ m.

(b) d sinθ = nλ. For n = 1, sinθ = (520 × 10⁻⁹)/(1.667 × 10⁻⁶) = 0.312; θ ≈ 18.2°.

(c) sinθ cannot exceed 1, so n ≤ d/λ ≈ 3.21. Thus orders 0, ±1, ±2 and ±3 are possible, but fourth order is not.

QUESTION 3Changing the wavelength

A double-slit arrangement produces bright-fringe separation of 3.00 mm with light of wavelength 500 nm.

  1. Find the spacing when the wavelength is increased to 650 nm, without changing the apparatus.
  2. Describe how the pattern changes as wavelength increases.
  3. Explain why coherent light is important for a stable fringe pattern.
Show worked answer

(a) Since Δx ∝ λ, the new spacing is 3.00 × (650/500) = 3.90 mm.

(b) Bright fringes move farther apart and so do the dark fringes. Central bright fringe remains centred.

(c) A stable phase relationship between the two overlapping waves is needed for fixed positions of constructive and destructive interference.

QUESTION 4Interference from path difference

At point P on a screen, the path difference from two coherent slits is 1.50 μm. The wavelength is 500 nm.

  1. Express the path difference in wavelengths.
  2. State whether P is bright or dark for two sources that emit in phase.
  3. Describe the condition for destructive interference for equal-amplitude waves.
Show worked answer

(a) 1.50 μm / 0.500 μm = 3.00 wavelengths.

(b) The path difference is 3λ, so the waves arrive in phase and P is a bright maximum.

(c) Destructive interference occurs for path differences (n + ½)λ, when the arriving waves are in antiphase and largely cancel.

QUESTION 5More slits and sharper maxima

A diffraction grating and a double slit are illuminated by the same coherent laser. Their slit spacing is the same.

  1. Explain why the principal maxima occur at the same angles.
  2. Describe how the maxima from a grating differ in width and intensity.
  3. Explain how a grating helps separate two nearby wavelengths.
Show worked answer

(a) Both have maxima when d sinθ = nλ, so the principal directions are set by d and λ.

(b) More coherent slits reinforce strongly only at closely defined angles, giving narrower, more intense principal maxima with subsidiary maxima between them.

(c) The angle depends on wavelength: nearby wavelengths emerge at slightly different angles; narrow grating maxima make them easier to resolve.

Doppler effect foundations

These ideas explain why a passing siren changes pitch. Open each panel for the detail you need.

3.1.1 · What is the Doppler effect?

The Doppler effect is the apparent change in the observed frequency of a wave caused by relative motion between the source and the observer. For example, an ambulance siren seems to drop in pitch as it passes.

The model used in this standard: the observer is stationary relative to the air and the source moves. The speed of sound is set by the medium, so a moving source changes the wavelength reaching the observer, not the sound speed.

3.1.2 · Stationary source

Wavefronts are evenly spaced in every direction.

A stationary source emits frequency f. Its wavefronts are a distance λ apart and travel at wave speed v, so v = fλ. Observers on both sides (green) hear the same pitch.

3.1.3 · Moving source

The source moves right, so the wavefronts bunch up in front and spread out behind.

As the source moves forward at speed vs, each wavefront is emitted from a new position further forward. Wavefronts are closer together in front and further apart behind.

The wave speed is unchanged, so by f = v/λ an observer in front hears a higher pitch and one behind hears a lower pitch.

3.1.4 · Observed frequency

For a stationary observer and a moving source:

f′=fvwvw±vs

Use − for an approaching source (shorter λ, higher f′) and + for a receding source (longer λ, lower f′). Check that your answer goes the way you predicted.

3.1.5 · Doppler-shift graph

For a source moving at steady speed, the observed frequency stays above the emitted frequency while the source approaches. It drops through the emitted frequency as the source passes, then stays below it while the source recedes.

The source speed does not change the wave speed in the stationary air.

QUESTION 1Approaching ambulance

An ambulance siren emits 680 Hz. The ambulance travels towards a stationary pedestrian at 25 m/s. The speed of sound in still air is 340 m/s.

  1. Calculate the observed frequency while the ambulance approaches.
  2. State whether the air carries sound faster in front of the ambulance.
  3. Explain what happens to wavefront spacing in front of the moving source.
Show worked answer

(a) Approach: f′ = fv/(v − vs) = (680 × 340)/(340 − 25) ≈ 734 Hz.

(b) No. Sound speed relative to stationary air remains 340 m/s.

(c) Successive wavefronts are emitted from increasingly forward positions. Their spacing, or wavelength, is shorter ahead of the source.

QUESTION 2Receding source

A sound source emits 500 Hz and moves away from a stationary listener at 20 m/s. Take v = 340 m/s.

  1. Calculate the observed frequency.
  2. Explain why the observed frequency is lower than the emitted frequency.
  3. Compare the wavelengths in front of and behind the source.
Show worked answer

(a) Receding: f′ = fv/(v + vs) = (500 × 340)/(340 + 20) ≈ 472 Hz.

(b) Successive wavefronts reaching the listener are farther apart, so fewer crests arrive each second.

(c) Wavefronts bunch in front of the source and spread out behind it, though wave speed through the air is unchanged.

QUESTION 3Passing a stationary observer

A police car sounds a steady 600 Hz siren as it approaches and then passes a stationary observer at 30 m/s. Speed of sound is 330 m/s.

  1. Calculate the frequency heard immediately before it passes.
  2. Calculate the frequency heard immediately after it passes.
  3. Explain why the pitch changes as the car passes even though the emitted siren frequency is constant.
Show worked answer

(a) Approach: 600 × 330/(330 − 30) = 660 Hz.

(b) Recede: 600 × 330/(330 + 30) = 550 Hz.

(c) The observer changes from receiving compressed wavefronts to stretched wavefronts. The source frequency and sound speed in the air do not change.

QUESTION 4Estimate source speed

A stationary observer hears a source emitting 450 Hz at 480 Hz while it moves directly towards them. The speed of sound is 340 m/s.

  1. Rearrange the source-approach formula for the source speed.
  2. Calculate the source speed.
  3. Explain what limiting condition is assumed by this school-level model.
Show worked answer

(a) From f′ = fv/(v − vs), rearrange to vs = v(1 − f/f′).

(b) vs = 340 × (1 − 450/480) = 21.25 m/s (about 21.3 m/s).

(c) The source moves directly along the line to the observer, the observer and air are stationary, and the source is subsonic.

QUESTION 5Frequency and wave speed

A sound source travels at 15 m/s through still air and emits 400 Hz. The speed of sound in the air is 345 m/s.

  1. Calculate the wavelength of the emitted sound ahead of the source.
  2. Find the observed frequency for a stationary listener directly ahead.
  3. Explain why a moving source changes observed frequency but not the speed of sound.
Show worked answer

(a) Ahead: λahead = (v − vs)/f = (345 − 15)/400 = 0.825 m.

(b) fahead = v/λahead = 345/0.825 ≈ 418 Hz.

(c) Wave speed is governed by the properties of the stationary air. Moving emission positions compress or stretch wavefront spacing; using f = v/λ gives the observed frequency change.

Standing waves, harmonics & patterns

Compare permitted vibration modes in strings, open pipes and closed pipes. The curves show displacement envelopes; pipe modes describe air displacement, not pressure.

Why do nodes and antinodes form?

Waves travelling along a string or through air in a pipe reflect at the ends. The incoming and reflected waves overlap (superpose). At resonant frequencies, this creates a standing-wave pattern with displacement nodes (points that remain still) and displacement antinodes (points with maximum oscillation).

  • Fixed end of a string: The string cannot move at the fixed point, so a displacement node forms there.
  • Closed end of a pipe: Air cannot move through the rigid wall, so a displacement node forms there.
  • Open end of a pipe: Air can move freely, creating approximately a displacement antinode. The pressure remains close to atmospheric pressure, making it a pressure node (minimal pressure variation).

Reading the pattern: geometry & counting

The colours in the simulation show the phase. Loops in the same colour move in phase, and neighbouring loops move in antiphase (one goes up while the other goes down). The green bracket is λ/2 (node to node) and the yellow bracket is λ/4 (node to antinode).

Why a node forms: the phase explanation

At a node the incident and reflected waves always arrive in antiphase, so their displacements are equal and opposite and cancel at every instant. At an antinode they always arrive in phase, so they reinforce to give maximum amplitude.

Worked example A · loops to wave speed

A rope 2.4 m long is fixed at both ends and shows 4 loops when driven at 25 Hz. Find the wave speed.

4 loops = 4 × λ/2 = 2.4 m, so λ = 1.2 m. Then v = fλ = 25 × 1.2 = 30 m s⁻¹.

Trap: there are 5 nodes here. If you counted nodes you would get the wrong λ.

Worked example B · node spacing to frequency

Two neighbouring nodes on a wire are 0.15 m apart and the wave speed is 96 m s⁻¹. Find the frequency and the node-to-antinode distance.

λ = 2 × 0.15 = 0.30 m. f = v/λ = 96 / 0.30 = 320 Hz. Node → antinode = λ/4 = 0.075 m.

Timbre · why a guitar and a flute sound different

Pitch is set by the fundamental frequency. Timbre (tone quality) comes from which higher harmonics are present and how strong they are. Plucking a string exactly at its midpoint makes that point an antinode. That suits the odd harmonics, but every even harmonic has a node at the midpoint, so the even harmonics are weak. Closed pipes give only odd harmonics, which is why they sound "thinner" or hollow.

Fundamental, harmonics & overtones

Fundamental = the lowest resonant frequency = the 1st harmonic (n = 1).

Harmonic n has frequency n × f₁ (2nd harmonic = 2f₁, 3rd = 3f₁ …).

Overtones count only the resonances that can actually occur above the fundamental, so the numbering differs for a closed pipe:

ResonatorFundamentalFirst overtoneSecond overtoneThird overtone
Fixed stringn = 1 (1st harmonic)n = 2 (2nd)n = 3 (3rd)n = 4 (4th)
Open pipe (both ends)n = 1 (1st harmonic)n = 2 (2nd)n = 3 (3rd)n = 4 (4th)
Closed pipe (one end)n = 1 (1st harmonic)n = 3 (3rd)n = 5 (5th)n = 7 (7th)

For an ideal pipe open at both ends, all harmonics are allowed; for an ideal pipe closed at one end, only odd harmonics are allowed. Harmonic number is n. On the animations, N marks a displacement node and A an antinode.

Strings

Both ends of a fixed string are nodes. Strings can produce odd and even harmonics. A harmonic can be accentuated by exciting the string at the corresponding antinode.

The string may vibrate in several modes at once; the overtones superpose with the fundamental to give the observed standing wave.

nλ=2L
fn=nv2L

n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 …

For a transverse wave on a string:

v=τμ

v = speed of the transverse wave travelling along the string, measured in metres per second (m/s). It is not the speed of sound travelling through the surrounding air. The vibrating string makes the air vibrate at the same frequency, but the wave speeds in these two media are generally different.

τ (tau) = tension in the string, measured in newtons (N); μ = mass per unit length of string, measured in kg m⁻¹. Here τ is used for tension to distinguish it from T, the wave period measured in seconds (s). A mechanical system can vibrate only if it has mass and elasticity.

Guitar example: Tightening a guitar string increases its tension (τ). If the string length and mass per unit length stay the same, waves travel faster along it, so its fundamental frequency increases and the note you hear becomes higher in pitch. Loosening the string lowers the frequency and pitch.

Open pipes

An open pipe has a displacement antinode at both ends. It can produce both odd and even harmonics. Several modes can occur at the same time and superpose.

nλ=2L
fn=nv2L

n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 …

Closed pipes

A pipe closed at one end has a displacement node at the closed end and an antinode at the open end.

nλ=4L
fn=nv4L

n = 1, 3, 5, 7 …

Only odd-numbered harmonics are produced. Because closed pipes have fewer overtones, the notes describe them as tending to sound thinner.

Diffraction grating

A diffraction grating has many equally spaced slits. Waves from neighbouring slits interfere constructively at particular angles, producing narrow, bright principal maxima. Change the wavelength or line density to see which orders are possible.

Beam view (animated): the laser splits into a beam for every order that satisfies d sin θ = nλ. Change the sliders to watch beams appear or disappear.

dsinθ=nλ
d=1lines per metre

d is the centre-to-centre slit separation (m), θ the angle from the central maximum, n the integer order (0, ±1, ±2, …), and λ wavelength (m).

Convert lines/mm into lines/m before calculating slit separation. Example: 500 lines/mm = 500,000 lines/m.

The grating equation is provided in the NZQA examination resource. Use it with the appropriate order and converted SI units.

Far-field intensity versus angle, from −90° to +90°. Cyan peaks are principal maxima. The idealised model uses six equally spaced slits, monochromatic coherent light and normal incidence; it omits the single-slit diffraction envelope.

Why do multiple slits give sharp maxima?

At a principal maximum, the path difference between adjacent slits is a whole number of wavelengths. Adding light from many slits makes the principal maxima narrower, with much weaker intensity between them. The central maximum is order n = 0; first-order maxima appear on either side, then higher orders where the condition can be satisfied.

Increasing wavelength moves the non-central maxima to larger angles. Increasing line density decreases slit separation and also increases the angles. Some high orders disappear when no real angle satisfies the grating equation.

A structure for "explain" questions

1Name the principle

Superposition, resonance, path difference, wavelength change…

2Apply it to this situation

The CEO framework for calculation questions

Use the four steps above when the question says explain. When it says calculate, determine or show that, work through Conceptualise, Execute and Outcomes. The C step is also where you name the physics principle, so it supports explain answers too.

CConceptualise

Understand the physical situation before you write any maths.

  • Core physics: which principle applies? Standing waves, path difference, Doppler…
  • Visualise: sketch it. Nodes and antinodes, slits and screen, source and observer. Label knowns and unknowns with units.
  • Define the system: string or pipe? Open or closed ends? Approaching or receding?
EExecute

Turn the concept into a solution.

  • Select equations that link the knowns to the unknown (see 0.4 Formula Sheet).
  • Solve symbolically first: rearrange for the unknown before you put numbers in.
  • Substitute values in SI units (nm, mm, cm and km h⁻¹ converted), then calculate.
OOutcomes

Check the answer makes physical sense.

  • Units: Hz for frequency, m for wavelength, m s⁻¹ for speed.
  • Reality check: visible light is 400–700 nm, sound in air is about 340 m s⁻¹, and for a grating sin θ ≤ 1.
  • Direction: Doppler approaching means f′ > f. A closed pipe's next harmonic is 3f₁.
CEO worked example · pan-flute pipe

Question: A pan-flute pipe is 12.0 cm long and closed at the bottom. The speed of sound is 343 m s⁻¹. Calculate the fundamental frequency, and the frequency of the next note it can produce.

C · Conceptualise: this is a standing sound wave in a pipe closed at one end. The closed end is a displacement node and the open end is an antinode, so the fundamental fits one quarter-wavelength: L = λ/4. Knowns: L = 0.120 m, v = 343 m s⁻¹. Unknowns: f₁ and the next harmonic.

E · Execute: λ = 4L and v = fλ, so f₁ = v/(4L) = 343/(4 × 0.120) = 715 Hz (3 s.f.). A closed pipe has only odd harmonics, so the next note is f₃ = 3f₁ = 2140 Hz (2.14 × 10³ Hz).

O · Outcomes: the units are Hz ✓. 715 Hz is a high but believable note for a short pipe ✓. The next note is 3f₁, not 2f₁, as it must be for a closed pipe ✓. An open pipe of the same length would give twice the fundamental (1430 Hz), so the closed pipe giving the lower note also makes sense ✓.

Numbers, units and significant figures

  • Convert to SI units first: nm → ×10⁻⁹ m, μm → ×10⁻⁶ m, mm → ×10⁻³ m, cm → ×10⁻² m, km h⁻¹ → ÷ 3.6 for m s⁻¹.
  • Give the final answer to a number of significant figures consistent with the data (usually 3), preferably in standard form. Carry unrounded values through your working and round only at the end. In 2025, early rounding pushed some answers up to 15% off.
  • In a “show that” question, write the base equation and your unrounded answer, so the marker can see you did not just copy the given value.
  • Prefixes you must be able to read: giga, mega, kilo, centi, milli, micro, nano, pico.
  • Units may be written as m s⁻¹ or m/s. Both are accepted.
  • Always show the equation, then the substitution, then the answer with a unit. Clear working can still earn credit when the final number is wrong.
  • Sense-check: visible light 400–700 nm; sound in air ≈ 340 m s⁻¹; a closed pipe's "next" harmonic is 3f₁.

What the 2025 examiners' report says

Do this

  • Explain using phase (in phase, 180° out of phase), not just “peaks and troughs”.
  • Doppler: mention all three: the wavelength gets shorter/longer, the wave speed stays constant, so f = v/λ changes.
  • Off the line of travel, use the component of velocity towards the observer. Level with the observer it is zero, so f′ = f.
  • Say increases or decreases, never just “changes”.
  • Draw standing waves that fit the string or pipe, with even loops and labelled N and A.

Avoid this

  • Explaining beats with path difference. Beats come from two slightly different frequencies drifting in and out of phase over time.
  • Mixing up diffraction (spreading through a gap) and interference (superposition of overlapping waves).
  • Treating crests, antinodes and antinodal lines as the same thing.
  • Saying “the waves bunch up” without saying the wavelength decreases.
  • Confusing loudness with frequency in Doppler answers.

The fifteen most common traps

Node spacing is λ/2, not λ. Node to antinode is λ/4.
Count loops, not nodes. n loops needs n + 1 nodes on a string.
Closed pipe: only odd harmonics. After f₁ comes 3f₁.
Harmonic ≠ overtone. The 1st overtone of a closed pipe is the 3rd harmonic.
Wrong wave speed. String: speed along the string. Pipe: speed of sound in air.
Resonance raises amplitude, not frequency. Say "energy is transferred efficiently".
Beats give two answers. f ± fbeat. Then use a test to decide.
What is x? Fringe spacing → n = 1. Distance to the 3rd fringe → n = 3.
Lines per mm. Convert to lines per metre before d = 1/N.
Grating ≠ small angle. Use tan θ = x/L, then d sin θ = nλ. Round nmax down.
Sound does not speed up. The wavelength changes; v is set by the air.
Louder ≠ higher. Getting louder is about distance, not the Doppler effect.
Beats are not path difference. They come from two different frequencies moving in and out of phase.
Diffraction ≠ interference. Diffraction spreads light through each slit; interference makes the fringes.
Level with the observer, f′ = f. The velocity component towards them is zero.

A · Resonance: natural frequency and damping

Demo: two tuning forks

Explore resonance with two tuning forks. Fork A drives sound through the air; Fork B has a natural frequency of 440 Hz. Adjust Fork A's frequency and watch Fork B respond most strongly when their frequencies match.

This is a simplified illustration. The vibration of Fork B is greatest near its resonant frequency, but the actual response also depends on damping and how efficiently sound couples between the forks. The forks do not need to touch.

Driven oscillator: see the resonance curve

Left: a mass on a spring, driven from the top. Right: the resonance curve (amplitude against driving frequency). The dot marks the current driving frequency.

Resonance in one sentence

Resonance happens when a system is driven at one of its natural frequencies. The driving force then pushes in step with the motion, so energy is transferred efficiently every cycle and the amplitude grows until energy lost to damping equals energy supplied.

The system still oscillates at the driving frequency. Resonance changes the amplitude, not the frequency.

Three conditions

  1. A periodic driving force acts on the system.
  2. Its frequency matches a natural frequency (for strings and pipes, any harmonic).
  3. The system is only lightly damped.

More damping: lower peak, broader peak, peak moves very slightly lower in frequency.

Useful resonance

  • Musical instruments (strings, pipes, sound boxes)
  • Resonance tube to measure the speed of sound
  • Microwave ovens (water molecules), MRI scanners
  • Tuning a radio to one station
  • Ultrasonic cleaning of fine parts
  • Singing in the shower: the small room boosts some notes

Problem resonance, and the fixes

  • Bridges and tall buildings swaying (wind, footsteps, earthquakes)
  • Car panels rattling at one engine speed
  • A wine glass shattered by a sung note
  • Concert halls, instruments and loudspeakers boosting some frequencies more than others

To prevent it: change the natural frequency (mass, length, stiffness) or add damping (shock absorbers, tuned mass dampers, base isolators). Give both in an exam answer.

B · Resonance tube: measure the speed of sound

A tuning fork is held over a tube whose bottom is closed by water. Raise or lower the water to change the air-column length. At the right lengths, the sound reflected from the water surface interferes with the incoming sound to form a standing wave, and the air column resonates. Listen for the loud resonances (watch the meter), then record the first two lengths.

Why subtract the two lengths? (end correction)

The antinode actually sits a little above the open top of the tube, by a distance e called the end correction. So the true resonances are

L₁ + e = λ/4    and    L₂ + e = 3λ/4

Subtracting removes e: L₂ − L₁ = λ/2. Then v = fλ. Using L₁ alone (λ = 4L₁) gives a speed that is too low.

Example: a 384 Hz fork gives resonances at 21.6 cm and 66.0 cm. λ/2 = 0.444 m, so λ = 0.888 m and v = 384 × 0.888 ≈ 341 m s⁻¹. (Using 4 × 0.216 = 0.864 m would give 332 m s⁻¹, too low.)

C · Beats

Two notes of slightly different frequency and similar amplitude produce a periodic increase and decrease in loudness as the waves move in and out of phase.

Underlying tone frequency
fnote=f1+f22
Beat frequency
fbeat=|f1−f2|

Top two traces: component waves with frequencies f1 and f2. Bottom trace: their sum. The dashed curves show the beat envelope. This visual uses slowed component waves so the beat pattern is easy to see.

What do you hear? To humans, beats sound like one underlying tone with frequency fnote = (f1 + f2)/2, whose loudness becomes louder and quieter at a rate equal to fbeat.

For distinct beats to be heard clearly, |f1 − f2| usually needs to be less than about 10 Hz. At larger differences, the ear tends to hear roughness or two separate notes rather than clear beats.

Game: tune the mystery string

A 440 Hz reference fork and a guitar string sound together. Count the beat rate, decide whether the string is sharp (too high) or flat (too low), then tighten or loosen it until the beats stop. Fewest moves wins. Use Listen to hear it (headphones help).

Beat lampflashes once per beat

Deciding between two answers

3 beats/s against 440 Hz means the string is 437 Hz or 443 Hz. Make a known change and watch the beat rate:

  • Tighten (raises f) → beats slow down: the string was below, so 437 Hz.
  • Tighten → beats speed up: it was above, so 443 Hz.

Same idea with a tuning fork: a blob of putty on a prong lowers its frequency.

Quick check · standing waves, resonance & beats

Sound interference · two loudspeakers

Two loudspeakers driven by the same signal generator are coherent sources. Move the listener and watch the path difference decide whether the sound is loud or quiet.

The waves spread out from both speakers. Where they keep reinforcing, the pattern flickers strongly (loud). Along the steady grey lines they always cancel (quiet). Tick "Show average loudness" to see the time-averaged map. Click or tap the map to move the listener. Map is 10 m wide.

Same rules as light

  • Loud: path difference = nλ (n = 0, 1, 2…)
  • Quiet: path difference = (n + ½)λ
  • Sound waves have λ ≈ 0.3–2 m, so the loud and quiet regions are spread out at large angles.
  • Large angles mean you cannot use x/L ≈ sin θ. Use nλ = d sin θ.

Explain it like an Excellence answer

"Both speakers are connected to one signal generator, so they are coherent: the same frequency and a constant phase difference. At a quiet point, the path difference is half a wavelength, so a compression from one speaker arrives with a rarefaction from the other. They superpose destructively and the amplitude is small. Moving sideways changes the path difference, so loud and quiet regions alternate."

Worked example · find the frequency

A student walks across the front of two speakers. At the first quiet point, she is 5.60 m from one speaker and 6.05 m from the other. v = 340 m s⁻¹.

Path difference = 0.45 m = λ/2 (first quiet point, n = 0). λ = 0.90 m, so f = 340 / 0.90 ≈ 380 Hz (3.8 × 10² Hz).

3 · What does x mean?

x = fringe spacing (gap between neighbouring bright fringes) → use n = 1: Δx = λL/d.

x = distance from the centre to a particular fringe → n is that fringe's order (centre is n = 0).

Spread of several fringes: if 6 bright fringes span 15 mm, there are only 5 gaps, so the spacing is 3.0 mm.

First dark fringe is at (0 + ½)λ, so n = 0 in (n + ½)λ. Don't use 1.5λ.

Where does nλ = dx/L come from? (derivation)

For a bright fringe at P, the path difference S₂P − S₁P = nλ. Draw the small triangle at the slits: path difference = d sin θ, so d sin θ = nλ.

Draw the large triangle from the slits to the screen: tan θ = x/L.

Because L ≫ d, the rays are nearly parallel and θ is small, so sin θ ≈ tan θ. Then nλ/d = x/L, which gives nλ = dx/L.

Alternative route in the notes: (S₂P)² − (S₁P)² = 2dx and S₂P + S₁P ≈ 2L give the same result.

4 · Ratio reasoning (fast, and fewer mistakes)

Fringe spacing Δx ∝ λL/d. If only one quantity changes, scale the answer: double L → spacing doubles. Double d → spacing halves. Red (650 nm) instead of blue (450 nm) → spacing × 650/450. Source brightness → no change to the spacing.

Worked example · from a wall pattern back to λ

A 250 lines/mm grating is 2.50 m from a wall. The first-order spot is 0.335 m from the centre.

d = 1/(250 000) = 4.00 × 10⁻⁶ m. tan θ = 0.335/2.50 = 0.134, so θ = 7.63°.

λ = d sin θ = 4.00 × 10⁻⁶ × sin 7.63° ≈ 5.31 × 10⁻⁷ m = 531 nm (green).

6 · Grating checklist

  • lines/mm × 1000 = lines/m; then d = 1/N in metres.
  • Before you take sin⁻¹, check sin θ ≤ 1. If it is greater than 1, that order does not exist.
  • nmax = d/λ rounded down. Total spots = 2nmax + 1 (includes the centre).
  • If N spots are visible, there are (N − 1)/2 orders on each side.
  • Between principal maxima a grating is almost completely dark. Don't use (n + ½)λ for a grating: that dark-fringe condition belongs to two sources.

Doppler graphs · frequency heard as a source passes

Exam questions often show a graph of observed frequency and ask what it tells you. The source drives past the observer again and again, and the graph draws itself each time.

Top: plan view of the road. Bottom: frequency heard, plotted against the source's position when it emitted the sound. The dashed line is the emitted frequency f.

Reading the graph

  • Flat high section: approaching at constant speed. The pitch is steady, not rising.
  • Flat low section: receding at constant speed.
  • Passes through f when the source is level with the observer: at that moment no part of its velocity is towards or away from the observer.
  • Close observer: a sudden, steep drop. Far observer: the same two flat levels, but a gentle S-shaped change. The reason is explained below.
  • Sloping ends: the speed is changing (accelerating or slowing).

Why a far observer hears a gradual change

The pitch depends on how fast the source moves along the line of sight, the line from the source to the observer. Only that part of its motion squashes or stretches the wavelength. If the source's velocity makes an angle α with the line of sight, the part directed towards the observer is vs cos α, so f′ = f·vw/(vw − vs cos α). Both observers hear the same source at the same speed. The difference is how quickly α swings from 0° to 180° as the source goes past.

Step by step

  1. Far away on either side, the line of sight lies almost along the road for every observer (α ≈ 0° approaching, α ≈ 180° receding). So near and far observers hear the same two plateau frequencies.
  2. Level with the observer, α = 90° for both. The source is moving neither towards nor away, so both hear exactly f.
  3. In between, α changes noticeably only while the source is within about one observer-distance D of the closest point. From D before it to D after it, α goes from 45° to 135°: the component changes from 0.71 vs towards to 0.71 vs away.
  4. So most of the drop happens over about 2D of road, which takes a time of about 2D / vs. A bigger D spreads the same drop over more road and more time.

Model answer · near and far observers (like 2025 Q1(c))

When the source is a long way off, it is moving almost directly towards both observers, so the whole of vs shortens the wavelength reaching each of them. Both hear the same high frequency. As it gets closer, the angle between its velocity and the line of sight to the far observer grows much sooner than for the near observer. The component of velocity towards the far observer therefore falls gradually, the wavelength reaching them gets gradually longer, and since the speed of sound is constant, the frequency they hear decreases gradually. When the source is level with them, there is no component towards or away from either observer, so both hear the emitted frequency. Afterwards the source is moving away; the near observer's line of sight swings round almost at once, giving a sudden drop to the low frequency, while the far observer's frequency keeps decreasing gradually until it reaches the same low value.

Calculation clinic · Doppler effect

One formula, used carefully. Predict first, calculate second, check third.

Changes

  • Wavelength reaching the observer (shorter ahead, longer behind)
  • Observed frequency f′

Does NOT change

  • Speed of sound (set by the air)
  • Frequency the source emits
  • Amplitude. Getting louder is because it's closer, not the Doppler effect.

The method

1Predict

Approaching → wavelength shorter, f′ > f. Receding → wavelength longer, f′ < f.

2Convert

km h⁻¹ ÷ 3.6 → m s⁻¹. (90 km h⁻¹ = 25 m s⁻¹)

3Substitute

f′ = f·vw/(vw ± vs): − approaching, + receding. vw is always on top.

4Check

Does the answer go the way you predicted? If not, you used the wrong sign.

Observed wavelength: ahead λ′ = (vw − vs)/f, behind λ′ = (vw + vs)/f. Unknown speed: substitute the numbers and rearrange. Don't try to memorise a rearranged formula.

Approach–recede pair solver

You hear a siren approach then recede, but you don't know f or vs. Divide the two equations: f′app/f′rec = (v + vs)/(v − vs). Try it, then check against the solver.

Notice that the true f is not the average of the two values. The rise on approach is bigger than the drop when receding.

Worked example · find the speed of a car

A car horn emits 400 Hz. A stationary listener hears 432 Hz as it approaches. v = 340 m s⁻¹.

432 = 400 × 340/(340 − vs) → 340 − vs = 400 × 340/432 = 314.8 → vs ≈ 25 m s⁻¹ (≈ 91 km h⁻¹).

Worked example · wavelength behind a train

A train whistle (750 Hz) moves away at 34 m s⁻¹. v = 340 m s⁻¹. Behind the train: λ′ = (340 + 34)/750 = 0.499 m, so f′ = 340/0.499 ≈ 682 Hz. Check: f·v/(v + vs) = 750 × 340/374 = 682 Hz ✓.

Where the Doppler effect is used

Speed guns & radar

Reflected microwaves come back shifted. The size of the shift gives the vehicle's speed.

Medical ultrasound

Ultrasound reflected from moving blood cells shows the speed and direction of blood flow.

Weather & astronomy

Weather radar tracks rain and wind. Light from receding galaxies is red-shifted (beyond this course).
